# Observational drawing and recording (AO3)

> **In the exam:** No written questions. This topic is **AO3**: "Record ideas, observations and insights relevant to intentions as work progresses." AO3 is marked out of **18** in both the Personal Portfolio and the Externally Set Assignment, so it is a quarter of your whole grade. Examiners judge your **skills and techniques while recording from observation, experience and ideas**, and whether your recording is relevant to your intentions and continues as the work progresses, not only at the start.

## What you need to know

- What AO3 rewards: recording ideas, observations and insights, with skill, relevant to your intentions, throughout the project.
- Ways to record: drawing from direct observation, photography, notes, thumbnails, studies of the work of others.
- Drawing skills: proportion, measuring, sighting, perspective, composition, tone, line quality.
- How to use primary (own) sources rather than copying the internet.
- How to make recording **relevant** and **developing**, not a one-off.

## Notes

### What AO3 means

| Phrase in the grid | In practice |
|---|---|
| "Record ideas, observations and insights" | Drawing, photos, notes and sketches that show what you see and think |
| "relevant to intentions" | The drawings are chosen to serve your idea or theme, not random |
| "as work progresses" | You keep recording during the project, including developing sketches and studies of the work of others |
| "personal work and the work of others" | Includes your own studies and studies of artists' work |
| "skills and techniques while recording from observation, experience and ideas" | Examiners are judging how well you draw/record, not only that you did |

Higher bands use words such as "confident and assured" and "exceptional use of skills and techniques". Lower bands mean a limited or basic range with little control.

### Primary and secondary sources

- **Primary source:** you saw it and recorded it yourself (a drawing of a real shell; your own photo of your street).
- **Secondary source:** from someone else (internet images, books). Fine for research, but examiners most want to see your own observation. Drawing from your own photo is good; copying a Google image gives weak AO3 evidence.

### Core drawing skills

**1. Proportion and measuring**
- Hold a pencil at arm's length with your arm locked. Use the pencil to **compare** sizes: "the height is about two and a half times the width."
- Use **plumb lines** (imagine a vertical drop) and **horizontal lines** to check what lies above or beside something.
- Draw the **negative space** to check shapes.

**2. Start light, build up**
- Begin with light construction lines and basic shapes (cylinder, sphere, box). Add detail only after the structure looks right.
- Use a range of pencil grades (HB for guidelines, 4B for darks).

**3. Viewfinder and composition**
- A card frame (or your phone camera) helps you decide the crop. Close-up crops make bolder compositions.

**4. Perspective**
- **One-point:** all depth lines meet at one vanishing point on the horizon (a corridor). **Two-point:** two vanishing points (a building corner). Objects get smaller with distance.

**5. Tone and line quality**
- Use varied line weight: thicker line for the shadow side, thin line where light hits the edge.
- See *Formal elements* (art-r1-02) for tone and texture techniques.

**6. Gesture and figure**
- Gesture drawing is quick (30 seconds to 2 minutes) to catch movement. Draw the action line first.
- Typical adult figure proportion is about seven and a half heads tall; use this as a starting guide but draw what you see.

### Drawing techniques to show variety

| Technique | Why it earns marks |
|---|---|
| Blind contour (don't look at the paper) | Shows observation and expressive line |
| Continuous line | Confident, flowing line |
| Tonal study in pencil or charcoal | Shows control of form |
| Mark-making sample | Shows range of texture |
| Grid method | Accurate enlargement from a photo |
| Drawing with non-dominant hand / unusual tools (stick and ink) | Shows experiment, expressive marks |
| Rubbing-out drawing on charcoal ground | Shows tone and light |

### Photography and other recording

- Take your own photos in different light (morning vs evening, flash vs natural), angles (low, high, close-up) and with different crops.
- Print them in the sketchbook, then **draw from or over them**: show how a photo becomes a drawing, painting or print.
- Use notes, measurements, colour swatches, rubbings, found objects and mini-collages as recording too.
- Always record **before** you design, but also **during**: new drawings after an experiment are AO3 evidence too.

### Making your recording relevant to intentions

State a line of enquiry early, for example "How does light change an everyday object?" Then choose subjects, angles and techniques that explore it. Each drawing should answer a part of the question. Add one-line notes: "Low viewpoint to make the kettle look powerful."

### Artists to learn from

- **Leonardo da Vinci:** anatomical and nature studies with notes beside the drawings. Shows that recording and thinking go together.
- **Albrecht Dürer:** detailed observational studies, such as *Young Hare* in watercolour.
- **Egon Schiele:** a bold, confident contour line in figure drawing.
- **Alberto Giacometti:** repeated, scratchy lines drawn over and over to find the form.
- **David Hockney:** photographic "joiners": many photos assembled to show a scene from several viewpoints.
- **Käthe Kollwitz:** powerful charcoal and print portraits with strong tone.

You choose artists whose approach fits your theme. Record how they record, then try it.

### A good AO3 sketchbook page

1. A main observational drawing (at least half the page) from a real object.
2. A smaller close-up or different view.
3. A mark-making or texture strip.
4. A photo or two with a crop box.
5. One precise note about what you found.

### Common recording routines for the externally set assignment

- Spend the preparatory period on 4–6 recording pages in different media, linked to the theme.
- Take them into the 10-hour exam: use them to solve problems in the final piece (for example, colour mixes recorded earlier).

## Key terms and definitions

- **Observational drawing:** drawing what you see directly in front of you.
- **Primary source:** a source you produce yourself from direct experience (drawing, own photo).
- **Secondary source:** an image or information made by someone else.
- **Proportion:** the size relationships between parts of an object.
- **Contour:** the line that follows the edge of a form.
- **Gesture drawing:** a quick drawing capturing movement and pose.
- **Viewfinder:** a frame used to choose and crop a composition.
- **Vanishing point:** where parallel lines appear to meet on the horizon in perspective.
- **Line of enquiry:** the question or focus that guides your recording.
- **Intention:** what you want your work to show or say.

## Worked examples

**1. Explain two ways of making recording relevant to your intentions. [4]**
*Model answer:* State a clear line of enquiry (for example, "how light changes shape") and choose subjects and viewpoints that explore it (2). Add short notes beside drawings that say why you chose the angle, crop or technique, so each study links to your aim (2).

**2. How can photographs be used well in a sketchbook for AO3? [3]**
*Model answer:* Take your own photographs from different angles and in varied light rather than using internet images (1). Print them and draw or paint from them, or crop and annotate to show choices (1). Use them to record details you can't draw in time, and then show the development into drawings (1).

**3. Describe how to check proportions in an observational drawing. [3]**
*Model answer:* Hold a pencil at arm's length and measure one part of the object, then compare other parts to it (1). Use plumb and horizontal lines to see what lines up (1). Draw the negative space around the object to check shapes and gaps (1).

## Common mistakes

- Copying a photo from the internet and calling it observation (use your own source).
- Recording only at the start and then not again as ideas change.
- All drawings in one medium, in one style, with one viewpoint.
- Drawing tiny objects in the middle of a big page; fill the page and crop.
- Outlines only; no tone, so objects look flat.
- Rushing the first drawing and never re-drawing after reviewing it.
- Drawings that don't relate to the theme or to each other.

## Plan for this hour

- **5 min:** Pick one object or place and write one line of enquiry about it.
- **15 min:** Observational drawing of the object: light construction first, measure with a pencil, then tone.
- **10 min:** Two quick studies: a close-up crop and a blind contour.
- **15 min:** Take 10 photos (angles, light), print or sketch your best two with crop boxes.
- **10 min:** Draw from your best photo in a different medium (pen, charcoal).
- **5 min:** Write two precise notes about what you learned.
