# Observational drawing and recording (AO1)

> **In the exam:** AO1 is worth **25 of 100 marks in Component 1 and 25 of 100 in Component 2**. The descriptor: "Record ideas, observations and insights relevant to intentions as work progresses." Examiners look for skilful recording from **a variety of relevant sources** and a clear sense of your **intentions**. There is no written paper; this is judged from the drawings, photographs, notes and studies in your digital portfolio. In Component 2 your first-hand recording is made in the preparation period, after you receive the paper.

## What you need to know

- What AO1 asks for and how the bands (limited to excellent) are described.
- Why first-hand (primary) sources earn more than second-hand (secondary) sources, and how to use both.
- Drawing from observation: measuring, proportion, scale, viewpoint, composition, tone, line.
- Ways to record: drawing, photography, annotated notes, mind maps, colour swatches, thumbnails, visits to galleries and places.
- How to show intentions as work progresses, and how recording links to the rest of the project.

## Notes

### What AO1 rewards

| Band | Key words in the descriptor |
|---|---|
| 21–25 | **Excellent** skill, **variety** of relevant sources, intentions shown **effectively** |
| 16–20 | **Confident** skill, intentions shown **consistently** |
| 11–15 | **Competent** skill, **clear** intentions |
| 6–10 | **Satisfactory** skill, **several** sources, **some** intentions |
| 1–5 | **Limited** skill, **one or more** sources, **basic** intentions |

Two things matter: **how well** you record (skill) and **what you record and why** (sources and intentions). "As work progresses" means recording is not a one-off at the start: add new observations all the way through.

### Primary and secondary sources

| | Primary (first-hand) | Secondary |
|---|---|---|
| What | You observe it yourself | Someone else's image or information |
| Examples | Drawing a plant on your desk; your own photographs; sketches at a museum; a sound or smell noted | Photographs from books, magazines, websites; an artist's painting |
| Value | Shows your own looking, skill and ideas | Useful for things you cannot visit, history, other artists' work |

The syllabus asks for "first-hand studies, such as their own drawings and photography, as well as secondary imagery". If you only draw from internet photographs, your marks are limited. If you use a secondary image, say where it came from (cite it) and add your own interpretation.

### Seeing before drawing

1. **Look more than you draw.** Beginners look at the paper; keep your eyes on the object most of the time.
2. **Draw shapes, not "things".** See a bottle as a rectangle and a half-circle first.
3. **Compare**: use a pencil held at arm's length to measure proportions and angles; check how far one part is to another (eg the handle is half the height of the mug).
4. **Check negative space** (the gaps between parts).
5. **Start light**: build the drawing with light construction lines before committing to dark ones.
6. **Add tone and detail last**, making sure there are lights and darks across the page.

### Useful drawing exercises

| Exercise | What it trains |
|---|---|
| **Blind contour**: draw the edge without looking at the paper | Close observation, line quality |
| **Continuous line** | Flow, confidence |
| **Quick sketches** (1–2 minutes each, eight per page) | Capturing form and gesture, many viewpoints |
| **Tonal study** (20 minutes, graphite or charcoal) | Form, light, texture |
| **Cropped viewpoint**: use a paper frame to select a section | Composition, focus |
| **Non-dominant hand** | Looseness, expressive line |
| **Drawing with a rubber**: cover the paper in charcoal, then lift out the lights | Tone and mood |

### Viewpoint, scale and composition

- Try **unusual angles**: from above, from ground level, close-up, partial views.
- **Scale**: draw a huge close-up of a small object (a shell) or a small version of a large one (a building).
- Use **thumbnails** (small quick layouts) to decide composition before a bigger study.
- Include **groupings**: overlap objects and vary sizes so the page is not a row of single items.

### Recording with photography

- Take **many photographs** and select. Vary angle, distance and lighting.
- Photograph for drawing: take the same subject in strong side light for tone.
- Print and glue them in the sketchbook, or include them in your digital portfolio **with annotation**: "I took this at 5 pm from low down so the shadow is long; I will draw from it to show tall, dramatic forms."
- Photographs are not a substitute for drawing: examiners want to see your **drawing skill** as well.

### Other ways of recording

- **Written notes and mind maps** to explore a theme and record insights ("it reminded me of ...").
- **Colour swatches and tests** taken from your subject.
- **Collected items**: leaves, fabric, tickets, scraps (stuck in or photographed).
- **Interviews or conversations** with a family member, an artist, a craftsperson.
- **Gallery and site visits**: quick sketches on location plus notes on what you noticed. The syllabus encourages visits to galleries, tourist attractions or places of interest and contact with local artists.

### Show your intentions

An **intention** is what you are trying to explore or say. It can be as simple as "to show the fragility of old objects" or "to capture the movement of crowds in Madrid's metro". Show it by:
- a short note at the start of the project;
- choosing sources that fit it (not random items);
- revisiting it: "My first drawings were too tidy, so I will use looser marks to show chaos."

### Recording in Component 2

After you receive the paper, choose one starting point and quickly begin first-hand recording: drawings, photos, notes. This produces your supporting studies. Do not leave this until the last week: **all** the recorded material and its follow-on exploration must support the final outcome you make in the 8-hour supervised test.

### Sketchbook or digital portfolio?

The portfolio is submitted digitally. You can work in a physical sketchbook and photograph it, or work digitally. Make sure photographs and scans are bright, straight, in focus and show whole pages. Insight shown in the notes counts only if the examiner can read it.

## Key terms and definitions

- **Recording:** making a visual or written record of what you observe, experience or think.
- **Observation:** looking carefully at a real object, scene or person to record accurately.
- **Primary (first-hand) source:** something you experience or create yourself, e.g. your own drawing or photograph.
- **Secondary source:** an image or information made by someone else, e.g. from a book or website.
- **Intentions:** the ideas and aims you are trying to realise.
- **Proportion:** the relative size of parts of a subject to each other.
- **Viewpoint:** the position from which you look at the subject.
- **Thumbnail:** a small, quick sketch used to test a composition.
- **Contour drawing:** drawing the edges and outline of a form.

## Worked examples

**1. A student's project theme is "Markets". Suggest a variety of relevant sources for AO1. [4]**

Own drawings of fruit stalls on location (1); own photographs of crowds, colours and signs (1); notes or interview with a stallholder about what they sell (1); secondary: a painting of markets by an artist or old market photographs from a book, cited (1).

**2. How can a student show intentions in their sketchbook? [3]**

Write a short aim at the start (1); choose sources that match it rather than random subjects (1); review work and adjust the plan with comments (1).

**3. Improve this observational drawing note: "I drew a shell. It was hard."**

Better: "I drew a conch shell from above using HB and 4B pencils. I measured the spiral with my pencil and saw the proportions were different from my first sketch. The dark inner opening shows depth, which I want to use in my final piece about hidden places." (names media, technique, decision and intention).

## Common mistakes

- Only drawing from photographs found online (limits AO1).
- Making one big careful drawing and nothing else: examiners want variety.
- Drawing symbols ("an eye shape") rather than what you see.
- Tiny drawings in a corner of the page; or all the same viewpoint.
- No tonal range (everything mid-grey).
- Recording only at the start; no new recording as the work progresses.
- Poor-quality photographs or scans of the work.
- Intentions that never show up in the work.

## Plan for this hour

- 10 min: read the notes; copy the AO1 wording and the five band words.
- 20 min: sketchbook task 1: choose one object (shoe, kettle, plant). Make a page of 6 quick drawings (2 minutes each) from different viewpoints.
- 15 min: sketchbook task 2: one 15-minute tonal drawing of your favourite viewpoint, plus a tonal scale.
- 10 min: take five photographs of the same object at different angles and light, select the best, and write a two-line annotation naming your intention.
- 5 min: quiz.
