# Writing about art: annotation and analysis

> **In the exam:** There is no written exam in Cambridge IGCSE Art & Design 0400. Annotation is **optional** in both Component 1 (Coursework) and Component 2 (Externally Set Assignment), but if you annotate it must be "relevant to the theme, purposeful and written in English". Good annotation helps the examiner see your **intentions** (AO1 Record, AO4 Present), your **choices** (AO2 Explore) and your **critical understanding** (AO3 Develop). Each AO is worth 25 marks out of 100.

## What you need to know

- The syllabus says candidates "do not have to annotate", but when they do it must be **relevant, purposeful and in English**.
- Work can be presented as practical work with little annotation, practical work with some written analysis, or illustrated written analysis (Component 1 coursework studies).
- Annotation should show **intention** (AO1: "ideas, observations and insights relevant to intentions"), **exploration** (AO2), **critical understanding** (AO3) and **visual language** (AO4).
- You should be able to describe an artwork using **content, form, process and mood**.
- You should evaluate your own work: "evaluate their own work, review and edit; reflect, refine and adapt".
- Notes must be **short, specific and useful**, not a diary of what you did.

## Notes

### 1. Why annotate at all?
The examiner cannot ask you what you meant. A few clear sentences turn a drawing into **evidence of thinking**. For example, a messy page of paint tests only shows you experimented if you say what you tried and what you decided.

Remember that Cambridge allows **practical work with little annotation** if the work itself is clear. Do not fill pages with words just to fill them. Annotate where the visual evidence alone does not show your thinking.

### 2. The four words: content, form, process, mood
Use this framework whenever you describe an artist's work **or your own**.

| Word | Question to ask | Example vocabulary |
|---|---|---|
| **Content** | What is it about? What do I see? | subject, figures, objects, setting, symbols, theme, title |
| **Form** | How is it put together? (the formal elements and composition) | line, tone, colour, shape, texture, pattern, space, balance, viewpoint, focal point, contrast, scale |
| **Process** | How was it made? | materials, tools, technique, layers, brushwork, printing, editing, stages |
| **Mood** | What does it feel like, and why? | calm, tense, joyful, lonely, energetic, mysterious, bold, delicate |

A strong analysis uses **all four** and joins them: "The **rough, fast brush marks** (form/process) make the sea feel **violent** (mood), which suits the shipwreck (content)."

### 3. Sentence starters that work
- **Intention:** "I want to show… because…"
- **Observation:** "I noticed that… so I decided to…"
- **Choice of media:** "I chose oil pastel because… / I tried watercolour first, but it was too… so I changed to…"
- **Artist link:** "Like Hockney, I used flat blocks of colour to… The difference is that I…"
- **Evaluation:** "This worked because… It did not work because… Next I will…"
- **Plan:** "My next step is to… on a larger scale."

### 4. Annotation that earns marks versus annotation that doesn't

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "I drew a shoe with pencil." | "I used 4B pencil to show the creases in the leather. The dark tone in the folds makes the shoe look heavy, which matches my theme of 'journeys'." |
| "This is a painting by Monet." | "Monet used short broken brushstrokes to show shimmering light on water. I tried the same with dabs of acrylic, but my colours were too dark." |
| "I like this one." | "I will develop this one because the strong diagonal draws the eye to the figure." |
| "I tried lots of things." | "Test 3 (ink wash over wax) gave the texture I wanted for peeling paint. I will use it for the background." |

### 5. Where to put annotation
- **Beside the work**, close to what it explains, not in a block on the other side of the page.
- **Short**: usually 2–4 lines per piece of work, longer on key pages (artist analysis, review of the best idea).
- **Neat and legible**: it will be photographed or scanned; a typed or printed label is fine. Handwriting must be readable when scanned.
- **Labels and arrows** pointing to details work well: "layering to build texture".
- **Review pages**: after an experiment stage, write a short paragraph: what worked, what didn't, what you chose.

### 6. Analysing an artwork: a model
Take Vincent van Gogh's *The Starry Night* (1889).

- **Content:** a night sky above a village, a tall cypress tree in the foreground, a church spire, a crescent moon and stars.
- **Form:** swirling curved lines in the sky; blue and yellow as strong contrasting colours; the dark vertical tree against the sky; thick paint (impasto) giving texture.
- **Process:** oil paint applied in thick directional brushstrokes, painted from memory and imagination from the asylum at Saint-Rémy (Van Gogh painted it in June 1889).
- **Mood:** restless, emotional, energetic but also awe-inspiring.
- **Link to my work:** "I will use directional curved marks to show wind in my beach landscape."

### 7. Evaluating your own work
Use a simple loop: **What did I intend? What happened? Why? What next?**

Examples:
- "I wanted the portrait to look tired. The grey-blue shadows under the eyes work, but the background is too bright and pulls attention away. I will repaint it in a duller green."
- "The lino print is too heavy because I cut too little away. In the next print I will carve more white space."

Evaluation is not a sign of failure. The syllabus lists "reflect, refine and adapt" as a core skill.

### 8. Language and presentation
- Use **art vocabulary**: composition, focal point, contrast, tone, texture, layering, scale, viewpoint, juxtaposition.
- Write in **first person** about your work ("I chose…") and **present tense** about the artwork ("Hockney uses…").
- Write in **English**, even though you may think in Spanish. Spell artists' names correctly: Cézanne, Kahlo, Hokusai, Basquiat.
- **Proofread**: a quick check for spelling and clarity.

## Key terms and definitions

- **Annotation:** short notes beside your work that explain your ideas, choices, methods and evaluation.
- **Content:** what the artwork is about and what can be seen in it.
- **Form:** how the artwork is put together, using the formal elements and composition.
- **Process:** the materials, techniques and stages used to make the work.
- **Mood:** the feeling or atmosphere created by the work.
- **Composition:** the arrangement of elements within the picture or object.
- **Focal point:** the part of the work that first draws the eye.
- **Evaluation:** judging how successful your work is, and deciding how to improve it.
- **Intention:** what you plan to achieve with a piece of work.

## Worked examples

**Q1. Write annotation (about 50 words) for a pencil study of a bicycle for a theme "Journeys". [4]**

*Model answer:* "I used 2B and 6B pencil to draw the chain and gears, because the metal parts show how a journey is built from small repeated actions (**intention**). Pressing hard in the shadows gave strong contrast and a shiny look (**process/form**). The drawing feels tense and mechanical (**mood**). Next I will try cutting this pattern into a lino print (**plan**)."

*Why it scores:* it explains why the subject suits the theme, names materials and effects, uses mood and states the next step.

**Q2. Describe how the mood is created in Edvard Munch's *The Scream* (1893). [4]**

*Model answer:* "The wavy, curving lines in the sky and landscape make the whole picture feel unstable (form). The vivid orange and red sky against dark blues creates tension (colour). The figure has a skull-like face, a wide-open mouth and hands pressed to its head, which shows fear or anxiety (content). The long straight bridge, running diagonally, pulls the eye towards the figure and increases the sense of panic (composition)."

**Q3. A student writes "I tried watercolour and it didn't work." Rewrite it so that it is purposeful. [3]**

*Model answer:* "I tried watercolour on thin paper, but the colours ran together and lost the sharp edges I wanted for the buildings (**what happened and why**). I will try gouache, which is more opaque, to keep crisp shapes (**decision**)."

## Common mistakes

- Writing a **diary** ("First I drew... then I painted...") instead of explaining decisions.
- Writing long paragraphs that are not related to the **theme**, when the syllabus requires annotation to be relevant and purposeful.
- Using only "nice" or "like", with no art vocabulary.
- Describing only the content (what you can see) and never the form, process or mood.
- Annotation too small, too faint or in places that scan badly.
- Making facts up about an artist. If you are unsure, leave it out or check it.
- Never evaluating: a page with no review looks like no thinking.
