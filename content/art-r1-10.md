# Art movements: modern and contemporary art

> **In the exam:** There is no written paper. Knowing modern and contemporary movements helps with **AO3 Develop** (25 of 100 marks in each of Component 1 and Component 2). The syllabus asks you to refer to "artists, key art movements, historical events, or local or national art, craft and design". Cambridge also expects "traditional, contemporary and emerging techniques and approaches". Use these movements as references in sketchbook pages, studies and annotation.

## What you need to know

- Main features, dates and key artists of: **Fauvism, Expressionism, Cubism, Futurism, Dada, Surrealism, Abstract Expressionism, Pop Art, Minimalism and Conceptual art, street art and contemporary art**.
- The idea that modern art breaks from realism: colour, shape and ideas become as important as copying the world.
- How to connect a movement to a theme and a technique, then to your own idea.
- Contemporary artists use drawing, painting, installation, photography, digital media and found objects. The syllabus allows work in **digital media, non-digital media or both**.

### 1. Fauvism (1905–c.1910)
A critic called the artists "les fauves" (wild beasts) at the 1905 Paris Salon d'Automne. They used **pure, strong colour that did not match real life**.
- **Henri Matisse**: *The Green Stripe* (portrait of his wife, 1905) has a green stripe down the face; later *The Dance* (1910) and paper cut-outs.
- **André Derain**: bright landscapes of Collioure.
- **Use**: colour studies where you choose colours for feeling, not accuracy.

### 2. Expressionism
Art that shows **inner feeling**, using distortion and strong colour.
- **Edvard Munch** (Norwegian): *The Scream* (1893).
- **Ernst Ludwig Kirchner**: Die Brücke group in Germany, harsh colour and angular figures.
- **Use**: distorted portrait, emotional colour, rough line.

### 3. Cubism (c.1907–1914 and after)
Developed by **Pablo Picasso** and **Georges Braque**. Objects are broken into geometric shapes and shown **from several viewpoints at once**.
- **Analytic Cubism** (c.1908–1912): browns and greys, fragmented forms (Picasso's portrait of Kahnweiler, 1910).
- **Synthetic Cubism** (from c.1912): brighter, simpler shapes, **collage** with newspaper and real objects.
- Picasso's *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon* (1907) is an early step; *Guernica* (1937), about the bombing of the Basque town in the Spanish Civil War, is on view at the **Museo Reina Sofía** in Madrid.
- **Use**: draw one object from three angles and combine them; collage over a still life.

### 4. Futurism (1909, Italy)
Started with Filippo Marinetti's manifesto; celebrated **speed, machines and the modern city**.
- **Umberto Boccioni**, **Giacomo Balla** (*Dynamism of a Dog on a Leash*, 1912): repeated shapes to show movement.
- **Use**: repeated overlapping lines or multiple-exposure photographs to show motion.

### 5. Dada (1916 onwards)
Born in Zurich in the First World War as a protest against war and conventional art. It used **chance, humour and found objects**.
- **Marcel Duchamp**: *Fountain* (1917), a urinal signed "R. Mutt" and submitted to an exhibition, which asked "what is art?" (the "readymade").
- **Hannah Höch**: **photomontage**, cutting up magazines to comment on society.
- **Use**: photomontage and collage from magazines, found objects.

### 6. Surrealism (from 1924)
Led by writer André Breton's manifesto (1924). Inspired by **dreams and the unconscious**; impossible scenes painted realistically or in dreamlike forms.
- **Salvador Dalí**: *The Persistence of Memory* (1931), soft melting clocks.
- **René Magritte** (Belgian): *The Treachery of Images* (1929), "Ceci n'est pas une pipe"; ordinary objects in odd situations.
- **Joan Miró** (Catalan): playful, symbolic shapes in primary colours.
- **Use**: juxtapose unrelated objects, dream-like scale changes, automatic drawing.

### 7. Abstract Expressionism (1940s–1950s, USA)
Large canvases showing emotion through **colour and gesture**, not recognisable objects.
- **Jackson Pollock**: **drip and pour** technique, canvases on the floor (*Autumn Rhythm*, 1950).
- **Mark Rothko**: large floating blocks of soft-edged colour to create a meditative mood.
- **Use**: big, fast mark-making; layers of translucent colour.

### 8. Pop Art (1950s–1960s)
Used images from **advertising, comics and celebrity culture**.
- **Richard Hamilton** (British): *Just what is it that makes today's homes so different, so appealing?* (1956).
- **Andy Warhol**: screen-printed repeated images, e.g. *Campbell's Soup Cans* (1962) and *Marilyn Diptych* (1962).
- **Roy Lichtenstein**: comic-strip style with Ben-Day dots and bold outlines.
- **Use**: repeated imagery, flat colour, screen printing or digital halftone.

### 9. Minimalism and Conceptual art
- **Minimalism**: simple geometric forms, industrial materials (Donald Judd's boxes).
- **Conceptual art**: the idea matters more than the object (Sol LeWitt's wall drawings, made from written instructions).

### 10. Street art and contemporary practice
- **Jean-Michel Basquiat** began as a graffiti artist (SAMO) in New York before painting on canvas.
- **Banksy**: anonymous British street artist using stencils and social comment.
- **Yayoi Kusama**: dots, repetition and immersive rooms.
- **Ai Weiwei**: installations on politics and human rights (e.g. *Sunflower Seeds*, 2010, a Tate Modern installation of millions of hand-painted porcelain seeds).
- **David Hockney**: continued to paint and draw, including on an iPad.
- **Kehinde Wiley**: portraits of Black men in poses from Old Master paintings.
- Contemporary practice often mixes **media**, includes **digital** and **installation**, and responds to **social and cultural issues**, which links to the syllabus: "the importance of social and cultural factors in the making, exhibiting and viewing of work".
- **Use**: stencil printing, mixed media, text and image, installation models.

### 11. Using modern art in your work
- Pick a movement because its **idea** helps your theme (Cubism for multiple viewpoints of a busy street; Pop Art for consumer culture; Surrealism for dreams and memory).
- Explore **techniques** the artists used (collage, stencil, drip).
- **Do not imitate blindly**: show your own decisions.
- Explain **why** you chose the movement and what it did for the work.

## Key terms and definitions

- **Fauvism:** an early 20th-century style using pure, strong colours that do not match real life.
- **Expressionism:** art that expresses inner feeling through distortion and strong colour.
- **Cubism:** a style that breaks objects into geometric shapes and shows them from several viewpoints at once.
- **Collage:** an artwork made by sticking materials such as paper or fabric onto a surface.
- **Futurism:** an Italian movement that celebrated speed, machines and modern life.
- **Dada:** an anti-war, anti-art movement using chance and found objects.
- **Readymade:** an ordinary manufactured object presented as art (Duchamp).
- **Photomontage:** a picture made by combining cut-up photographs.
- **Surrealism:** a movement inspired by dreams and the unconscious.
- **Abstract art:** art that does not show recognisable objects from real life.
- **Pop Art:** art that uses imagery from popular culture, advertising and mass media.
- **Conceptual art:** art in which the idea is more important than the finished object.
- **Installation:** an artwork that fills or changes a space.

## Worked examples

**Q1. Describe two features of Cubism. [4]**

*Model answer:* "Cubist artists such as Picasso and Braque broke objects into geometric shapes (1) and showed them from several viewpoints at once instead of a single fixed one (1). Analytic Cubism used a limited palette of browns and greys (1), while Synthetic Cubism introduced collage with real materials such as newspaper (1)."

**Q2. How could you use Pop Art in a project on "Fast food"? [4]**

*Model answer:* "Pop Art used popular culture, as in Warhol's *Campbell's Soup Cans* (1962) (1). I would photograph burger packaging and make a repeated screen print or digital version with bold flat colour (1). I would use Lichtenstein's dots (halftone) in the background to look like cheap printing (1). The repeat and the colour would show how mass-produced food is (1)."

**Q3. Explain why Duchamp's *Fountain* (1917) was important. [3]**

*Model answer:* "Duchamp presented a ready-made urinal as art (1). It challenged the idea that art must be handmade or beautiful (1) and helped start conceptual art, where the idea matters more than the object (1)."

## Common mistakes

- Calling every abstract or odd painting "Cubist" or "Surrealist".
- Confusing **Picasso** (Cubism, *Guernica*) with **Dalí** (Surrealism).
- Saying Pollock painted with a brush on an easel: he poured and dripped paint on canvas laid on the floor.
- Describing Warhol as a painter only: he used screen printing, photography and film.
- Giving wrong dates or titles. If unsure, leave it out.
- Listing movements without saying how they help **your own idea**.
- Forgetting contemporary examples: examiners like references to living artists too.
