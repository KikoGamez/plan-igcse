# Art movements: modern and contemporary art

> **In the exam:** There is no written paper. Pearson wants you to know "a range of work, from contemporary practice, past practice and different cultures" in your endorsed title (Fine Art, Graphic Communication, Photography, Textile Design or Three-dimensional Design). This knowledge supports **AO1** (critical understanding) in both components, 18 of the 72 marks each, and makes your own development (AO2) more informed.

## What you need to know

- Key **features, dates and artists** of the main movements from about 1900 to today.
- How to **choose** modern or contemporary practitioners relevant to your own endorsed title and theme.
- How these artists **broke rules** (colour, form, subject, materials, the idea of "art" itself).
- How to **link** them to your work with a study and analysis.

### Fauvism and Expressionism
- **Fauvism** ("wild beasts", name from a 1905 Paris exhibition): flat, bright colour used for emotion, not realism. Matisse later made cut-paper works (*The Snail*, 1953).
- **Expressionism**: distorted forms and strong colour show inner feeling. Munch's *The Scream* uses swirling lines and acid colour to express anxiety.
- **To try:** paint a portrait in colours chosen for feeling, not appearance; compare it with a realistic version.

### Cubism
- Led by **Picasso** and **Braque**, c.1907–1914. Objects are broken into geometric planes seen from several viewpoints.
- **Analytical Cubism** (to c.1912): muted browns, greys, ochres; fragmented, hard to read. **Synthetic Cubism** (from 1912): simpler, brighter shapes and **collage**, e.g. newspaper and rope in Picasso's *Still Life with Chair Caning* (1912).
- *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon* (1907) is the big early breakthrough. *Guernica* (1937, Museo Reina Sofía, Madrid) shows Cubist-style fragmentation used for protest.
- **To try:** draw a guitar or bottle from three angles and combine them in one image; make a collage still life with newspaper.

### Dada and Surrealism
- **Dada** reacted to the horror of the First World War. Duchamp exhibited a urinal as *Fountain* (1917), arguing that the artist's **idea and choice** could make art. Hannah Höch made photomontage.
- **Surrealism** (Breton's manifesto, 1924): unexpected combinations, dream imagery and the unconscious. Dalí's *The Persistence of Memory* (1931) shows melting clocks in a dream landscape. Magritte's *The Treachery of Images* (1929) says "This is not a pipe". Joan Miró (Catalan) used playful floating symbols and bright colours.
- **To try:** juxtapose two unrelated objects in a photo-collage; draw from a dream.

### Abstract Expressionism
- Post-war New York, large canvases, emphasis on process and emotion.
- **Jackson Pollock** poured and dripped paint onto canvas on the floor (action painting). **Mark Rothko** painted soft-edged rectangles of colour (colour field) meant to evoke emotion.
- **To try:** large-scale drips with fluid acrylic on a floor sheet; a three-layer colour-block study.

### Pop Art
- Everyday objects, ads, comics and celebrity. **Warhol** used screen printing and repetition (*Campbell's Soup Cans*, 1962; Marilyn series). **Lichtenstein** enlarged comic-strip images with Ben-Day dots and bold outlines (*Whaam!*, 1963). **Richard Hamilton** made one of the first Pop collages in 1956.
- **To try:** a repeated-image screen print or stencil; comic dots with a pencil-end stamp.

### Design movements (useful for Graphic, Textile and 3D)
- **Bauhaus** (Germany, 1919–33): form follows function, simple geometric design and typography.
- **Art Nouveau** and **Art Deco**: decorative, flowing natural lines versus geometric, luxurious style.
- **Modernist graphic design**: grid layouts, sans-serif type.
Linking a design movement to your own work shows real contextual awareness.

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Abstract art | Art that does not attempt to represent visual reality accurately, using shape, colour and form |
| Cubism | An early 20th-century style showing objects from multiple viewpoints as geometric fragments |
| Collage | A work made by sticking materials such as paper or fabric onto a surface |
| Readymade | An ordinary manufactured object presented as art by the artist, as with Duchamp |
| Surrealism | A movement exploring dreams and the unconscious through unexpected imagery |
| Pop Art | Art that uses images from popular culture, advertising and mass media |
| Installation | An artwork made for a particular space that viewers can walk through or experience |
| Photomontage | A picture made by combining pieces of different photographs |
| Contemporary art | Art made today, from about the 1970s onwards |

## Worked examples

**1.** Describe two differences between Analytical and Synthetic Cubism. [2]

*Model:* Analytical Cubism used muted browns and greys with highly fragmented forms (1); Synthetic Cubism used simpler, brighter shapes and introduced collage with real materials such as newspaper (1).

**2.** Explain how Duchamp's *Fountain* challenged ideas about art. [3]

*Model:* Duchamp presented an ordinary manufactured urinal as art (1), which suggested that the artist's idea and choice matter more than skill in making (1), so it questioned what art is (1).

**3.** Choose one contemporary artist and explain how they could influence a project on "identity". [4]

*Model:* Cindy Sherman photographs herself in staged roles and costumes (1) to question stereotypes of identity (1). I would photograph myself in three constructed roles using costume and lighting (1) and compare them with my own self-portrait to show how identity can be performed (1).

## Common mistakes

- Mixing up Dada and Surrealism: Dada is anti-art and chance; Surrealism is dreams and the unconscious.
- Saying Picasso invented abstract art: Cubism broke up form but still represents objects.
- Saying "modern art is random": explain the idea behind the work.
- Using Banksy only because he is famous without analysing technique and meaning.
- Naming artists without a specific work, date and analysis.
- Choosing practitioners that don't fit your endorsed title (a photographer needs photographers).
