# Classification and cells

> **In the exam:** Paper 2 almost always has 3–5 questions here (characteristics of living things, using a key, identifying a cell or organelle, a magnification calculation). Paper 4 often opens with a classification/key question (4–8 marks) and cell structure appears inside other questions. Paper 6 regularly asks you to draw a specimen, measure it and calculate magnification, or to build a dichotomous key (3–5 marks).

## What you need to know

- Describe the characteristics of living organisms: movement, respiration, sensitivity, growth, reproduction, excretion, nutrition.
- Define *species*; describe the binomial system of naming.
- Construct and use dichotomous keys based on identifiable features.
- State the main features used to place organisms into groups: animal, plant, fungus, prokaryote, protoctist (Extended: describe their features).
- State the main features of vertebrates (mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, fish) and arthropods (insects, arachnids, crustaceans, myriapods); Extended: ferns and flowering plants (monocotyledons, dicotyledons); features of viruses.
- Extended: explain that classification shows evolutionary relationships and that DNA base sequences are used; organisms sharing a more recent ancestor have more similar base sequences.
- Describe and compare the structure of plant, animal and bacterial cells and state the functions of their parts.
- Identify specialised cells and relate structure to function: ciliated cells, root hair cells, palisade mesophyll cells, neurones, red blood cells, sperm and egg cells.
- Define cell, tissue, organ, organ system.
- Calculate magnification and size of biological specimens using millimetres and micrometres (Extended: convert between mm and µm).

## Notes

### Characteristics of living things (MRS GREN)

| Characteristic | Syllabus definition |
|---|---|
| Movement | an action by an organism or part of an organism causing a change of position or place |
| Respiration | the chemical reactions in cells that break down nutrient molecules and release energy for metabolism |
| Sensitivity | the ability to detect and respond to changes in the internal or external environment |
| Growth | a permanent increase in size and dry mass |
| Reproduction | the processes that make more of the same kind of organism |
| Excretion | the removal of waste products of metabolism and substances in excess of requirements |
| Nutrition | the taking in of materials for energy, growth and development |

Note "dry mass" in growth and "release energy" (never "produce/make energy") in respiration.

### Classification and naming

- **Species**: a group of organisms that can reproduce to produce fertile offspring.
- **Binomial system**: an internationally agreed system in which the scientific name of an organism is made up of two parts showing the genus and species, e.g. *Homo sapiens* (genus capital letter, species lower case).
- Organisms are grouped by shared features. Extended: these groups reflect evolutionary relationships; today classification also compares **DNA base sequences** (and amino acid sequences of proteins). The more similar the sequences, the more closely related the organisms.

### The five kingdoms (Extended features)

| Kingdom | Key features |
|---|---|
| Animal | multicellular; no cell walls, no chlorophyll; feed on organic substances made by other organisms |
| Plant | multicellular; cell walls of cellulose; chloroplasts with chlorophyll; photosynthesis |
| Fungus | usually multicellular (yeast is single-celled); cell walls (not cellulose); no chlorophyll; feed by extracellular digestion and absorption (saprotrophic) or parasitic; some store glycogen |
| Prokaryote | single-celled; no nucleus (circular DNA, plasmids); cell wall (not cellulose); no mitochondria |
| Protoctist | mostly single-celled, with a nucleus; some photosynthesise (like plants), some feed like animals |

**Viruses** are not in any kingdom: a protein coat around genetic material (DNA or RNA); no cytoplasm, ribosomes or cell membrane; reproduce only inside host cells.

### Vertebrates

| Group | Body covering | Other features |
|---|---|---|
| Mammals | fur/hair | external ears (pinna), mammary glands, whiskers; most give birth to live young |
| Birds | feathers | beak, wings, hard-shelled eggs |
| Reptiles | dry scales | soft leathery-shelled eggs laid on land |
| Amphibians | moist, scaleless skin | eggs laid in water (jelly); larvae (tadpoles) live in water |
| Fish | wet scales | fins, gills, lateral line |

### Arthropods (jointed legs, exoskeleton, segmented body)

| Group | Legs | Body parts | Antennae | Other |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Insects | 3 pairs | 3 (head, thorax, abdomen) | 1 pair | usually 2 pairs of wings, compound eyes |
| Arachnids | 4 pairs | 2 | none | |
| Crustaceans | 5 or more pairs | 2 | 2 pairs | many live in water |
| Myriapods | many (1–2 pairs per segment) | many segments | 1 pair | |

### Plants (Extended)

- **Ferns**: leaves called fronds; no flowers; reproduce by spores.
- **Flowering plants**: reproduce sexually with flowers and seeds.
  - **Monocotyledons**: one cotyledon, parallel leaf veins, flower parts in threes.
  - **Dicotyledons**: two cotyledons, branching (net) veins, flower parts in fours or fives.

### Dichotomous keys

- Each step splits into **two** choices using a visible feature (number of legs, shape of leaf, presence of wings).
- To **construct** a key: list the organisms, choose a feature that splits the group, keep splitting until every organism is alone. You need (n − 1) questions for n organisms.
- Avoid features that vary or are vague ("large", "dark", "fast"). Use observable, either/or features ("wings present / wings absent").

### Cell structure

| Part | Animal | Plant | Bacterium | Function |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cell membrane | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | controls what enters and leaves the cell |
| Cytoplasm | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | where most chemical reactions happen |
| Nucleus | ✓ | ✓ | ✗ | contains genetic material (chromosomes) that controls the cell |
| Ribosomes | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | protein synthesis |
| Mitochondria | ✓ | ✓ | ✗ | aerobic respiration (release energy) |
| Cell wall | ✗ | ✓ (cellulose) | ✓ (not cellulose) | strength, keeps shape, stops bursting |
| Chloroplasts | ✗ | ✓ (some cells) | ✗ | photosynthesis |
| Large permanent vacuole | ✗ | ✓ | ✗ | contains cell sap; keeps cell turgid |
| Circular DNA, plasmids | ✗ | ✗ | ✓ | genetic material |

Extended: ribosomes are found on rough endoplasmic reticulum; cells with high rates of respiration (muscle, sperm) have many mitochondria. New cells are produced by division of existing cells.

### Levels of organisation

- **Tissue**: a group of cells with similar structures, working together to perform a shared function.
- **Organ**: a structure made up of a group of tissues, working together to perform specific functions.
- **Organ system**: a group of organs with related functions, working together to perform body functions.

### Specialised cells

| Cell | Function | Adaptation |
|---|---|---|
| Ciliated cell | moves mucus in trachea and bronchi | cilia beat to sweep mucus |
| Root hair cell | absorbs water and mineral ions | long extension → large surface area |
| Palisade mesophyll cell | photosynthesis | many chloroplasts; near upper surface |
| Neurone | conducts electrical impulses | long axon; branched endings |
| Red blood cell | transports oxygen | haemoglobin; no nucleus; biconcave → large surface area |
| Sperm cell | fertilisation (male gamete) | flagellum to swim; many mitochondria; enzymes in acrosome; haploid nucleus |
| Egg cell | fertilisation (female gamete) | large; energy store in cytoplasm; jelly coat changes after fertilisation; haploid nucleus |

### Magnification

**magnification = image size ÷ actual size** (no units — write "×").

- Both sizes in the **same unit** first. 1 mm = 1000 µm; so mm → µm × 1000, µm → mm ÷ 1000.
- Rearranged: actual size = image size ÷ magnification.
- Measure the image with a ruler in mm, from edge to edge along the line shown.

### Paper 6 skills on this topic

- **Biological drawings**: sharp pencil, single clear continuous lines, no shading, drawing larger than the specimen and filling most of the space, correct proportions, label lines drawn with a ruler that touch the structure and do not cross.
- **Measuring** a line on a photo or your drawing to the nearest mm; then calculate magnification of your drawing: drawing length ÷ real length.
- **Keys**: construct a key from photographs using visible features only.
- Microscope slides: onion epidermis stained with iodine; cheek cells stained with methylene blue. Risk: iodine and methylene blue stain skin/eyes (wear eye protection); take care with coverslips (sharp glass) and lower the coverslip at an angle to avoid air bubbles.

## Worked examples

**1.** A student draws a cheek cell. The drawing is 45 mm across. The real cell is 15 µm across. Calculate the magnification of the drawing. Show your working. [2]

- Convert: 45 mm = 45 000 µm (or 15 µm = 0.015 mm) (1)
- 45 000 ÷ 15 = **×3000** (1)

**2.** A photomicrograph has magnification ×400. A chloroplast in it measures 2 mm. Calculate the actual length in µm. [2]

- actual = 2 ÷ 400 = 0.005 mm (1)
- 0.005 × 1000 = **5 µm** (1)

**3.** Explain why a fungus is not classified as a plant. [3]

- fungi have no chlorophyll / chloroplasts (1)
- do not photosynthesise / feed saprotrophically by extracellular digestion (1)
- cell walls not made of cellulose (1)

**4.** Describe how a red blood cell is adapted to its function. [3]

- contains haemoglobin to carry oxygen (1)
- no nucleus, so more room for haemoglobin (1)
- biconcave shape, giving a large surface area (to volume ratio) for diffusion of oxygen (1)

## Common mistakes

- Writing "respiration makes energy" — energy is **released**, never made.
- Defining growth as "getting bigger" — you must say **permanent** increase in **size and dry mass**.
- Forgetting to convert units before calculating magnification, or giving magnification a unit (it has none).
- Saying bacteria have a nucleus, or that plant cells have no mitochondria (they do).
- In keys, using vague features ("big ears", "dark colour") instead of observable either/or features.
- Confusing tissue and organ: blood and xylem are tissues; heart and leaf are organs.
- Writing the binomial name with the species name capitalised.

## Practice questions

1. State **three** structures found in a plant cell but not in an animal cell. [3]
2. A red blood cell is 8 µm in diameter. In a textbook diagram it is 24 mm across. Calculate the magnification. [2]
3. A student finds an animal with four pairs of jointed legs, two body sections and no antennae. Name the group. [1]
4. State **two** features of a virus that show it is not a cell. [2]
5. Extended: explain how DNA base sequences are used to decide how closely related two species are. [2]
6. Write a dichotomous key to separate: bee (wings, 3 pairs legs), spider (no wings, 4 pairs legs), crab (no wings, 5 pairs legs), housefly (one pair wings, 3 pairs legs). Bees have two pairs of wings. [3]
7. Describe how a root hair cell is adapted for absorption. [2]

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Cell wall; chloroplasts; large permanent vacuole (with cell sap).
2. 24 mm = 24 000 µm (1); 24 000 ÷ 8 = ×3000 (1).
3. Arachnid.
4. Any two: no cell membrane; no cytoplasm; no ribosomes; only a protein coat and genetic material; can only reproduce inside a host cell.
5. Compare the sequence of bases (1); the more similar the sequence, the more recent the common ancestor / more closely related (1).
6. 1 Wings present → go to 2; wings absent → go to 3. 2 Two pairs of wings → bee; one pair of wings → housefly. 3 Four pairs of legs → spider; five pairs of legs → crab. (1 mark per correct split.)
7. Long extension / hair gives large surface area (1); for uptake of water (by osmosis) and mineral ions (by active transport) (1); thin wall / many mitochondria for active transport also credited.
