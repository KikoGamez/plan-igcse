## Notes

### Diffusion

- **Diffusion**: the net movement of particles from a region of their higher concentration to a region of their lower concentration (i.e. down a concentration gradient), as a result of their random movement.
- It is **passive**: no energy from respiration is needed — the particles' own random movement is enough.
- Some substances move into and out of cells by diffusion **through the cell membrane**.

Importance of diffusion of **gases and solutes**:
- O₂ diffuses from alveoli into the blood; CO₂ from the blood into the alveoli.
- CO₂ diffuses into leaves through stomata; O₂ and water vapour diffuse out.
- Glucose and O₂ diffuse from blood into respiring cells; CO₂ diffuses out.
- Digested food molecules diffuse from the small intestine into the blood.

Factors that influence the **rate** of diffusion (Supplement — investigate):

| Factor | Effect on rate | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Larger surface area | faster | more particles can cross at the same time |
| Higher temperature | faster | particles have more kinetic energy and move faster |
| Steeper concentration gradient | faster | greater difference → greater net movement |
| Shorter distance | faster | particles have less far to travel (thin walls, one cell thick) |

### Osmosis

- Core: water diffuses through **partially permeable membranes** by osmosis; water moves into and out of cells by osmosis through the **cell membrane**.
- Supplement definition — **osmosis**: the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential (dilute solution) to a region of lower water potential (concentrated solution), through a partially permeable membrane.
- A **partially permeable membrane** lets small molecules (water) through but not larger ones (e.g. sucrose).
- **Water potential**: pure water has the highest water potential; the more solute dissolved, the lower the water potential.

Effects of solutions on cells:

| Solution outside | Plant cell | Animal cell (e.g. red blood cell) |
|---|---|---|
| Higher water potential (dilute / pure water) | water enters by osmosis; vacuole swells; cell becomes **turgid**; the wall stops it bursting | water enters; cell swells and **bursts** (no wall) |
| Same water potential | no net movement | no net movement |
| Lower water potential (concentrated) | water leaves; cell becomes **flaccid**; then the membrane pulls away from the wall = **plasmolysis** | water leaves; cell shrinks |

Core (plant tissue): potato or carrot strips **gain mass and become firm** in dilute solution / water, and **lose mass and become soft/bendy** in concentrated solution.

Supplement terms:
- **Turgid**: the cell is swollen with water and the contents push against the cell wall.
- **Turgor pressure**: the outward pressure of the cell contents on the cell wall. It keeps non-woody plants upright; loss of turgor pressure → **wilting**.
- **Flaccid**: the cell has lost water, is soft, with little turgor pressure.
- **Plasmolysis**: the cell membrane and cytoplasm pull away from the cell wall as the cell loses water by osmosis.

Importance of water potential and osmosis in uptake and loss of water (Supplement):
- Root hair cells have a **lower water potential** than soil water, so water enters by osmosis.
- Water moves from cell to cell across the root down a water potential gradient.
- Leaves lose water; if loss is greater than uptake, cells become flaccid and the plant wilts.
- Animal cells have no wall, so the water potential of blood and tissue fluid must be kept suitable or cells are damaged (they burst or shrink).

### Active transport

- **Active transport**: the movement of particles through a cell membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration (i.e. against a concentration gradient), using energy from respiration.
- Supplement — importance: it lets cells take up molecules or ions that are scarce outside, e.g. **ion uptake by root hairs** (nitrate and magnesium ions are at a lower concentration in soil water than in the root hair cell).
- Cells doing a lot of active transport have **many mitochondria**. Anything that stops respiration (no oxygen, poison, low temperature) slows or stops active transport — this is a common "suggest" question.

| | Diffusion | Osmosis | Active transport |
|---|---|---|---|
| What moves | any particles | water only | molecules or ions |
| Direction | down a concentration gradient | higher → lower water potential | against a concentration gradient |
| Membrane needed? | not always | partially permeable | cell membrane |
| Energy from respiration? | no | no | **yes** |

### Biological molecules

| Molecule | Elements | Large molecule made from |
|---|---|---|
| Carbohydrates | C, H, O | starch, glycogen and cellulose are made from **glucose** |
| Proteins | C, H, O, N (some also S) | **amino acids** |
| Fats and oils | C, H, O | **fatty acids and glycerol** |

### Food tests

| Test for | Method | Positive result | Negative |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starch | add **iodine solution** | **blue-black** | stays orange-brown |
| Reducing sugars (e.g. glucose) | add **Benedict's solution**, **heat** in a hot water bath for a few minutes | green → yellow → orange → **brick-red** (more sugar = further along) | stays blue |
| Protein | add **biuret** reagent (or sodium hydroxide then copper sulfate solution) | **purple / violet** | stays blue |
| Fats and oils | **ethanol emulsion** test: shake food with ethanol, then pour into water | **cloudy white** emulsion | stays clear |

### Paper 6 skills on this topic

**Osmosis practical (potato cylinders in sucrose solutions):**
- Independent variable: concentration of sucrose solution. Dependent: change in mass (as % change).
- Controlled: size and surface area of cylinders, same potato, volume of solution, time, temperature; blot dry the same way each time.
- **Percentage change** = (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100, because cylinders start with different masses.
- Plot % change (y) against concentration (x); where the line crosses 0 %, the solution has the same water potential as the cells.
- Risk: cork borer and scalpel are sharp — cut on a tile, away from fingers.

**Diffusion practical:** agar cubes containing indicator placed in acid (or coloured dye); time for the colour to change right through. Smaller cubes have a larger surface area to volume ratio and a shorter distance to the centre, so they change faster. Visking (dialysis) tubing can model a partially permeable membrane.

**Food tests safety:** wear eye protection; heat Benedict's in a **water bath**, not directly in a flame; ethanol is **flammable** (no naked flames); biuret reagent contains alkali (irritant/corrosive).

**Results tables:** column headings with units (e.g. "concentration / mol per dm³", "mass / g"), independent variable in the first column, same number of decimal places throughout.

## Worked examples

**1.** A potato cylinder has a mass of 2.50 g. After 30 minutes in a concentrated sucrose solution its mass is 2.20 g. Calculate the percentage change in mass. [2]

- change = 2.20 − 2.50 = −0.30 g (1)
- −0.30 ÷ 2.50 × 100 = **−12 %** (1)

**2.** Explain the change in mass in Q1. [3]

- the solution had a lower water potential than the potato cells (1)
- water left the cells by osmosis (1)
- through the partially permeable (cell) membrane (1)

**3.** Root hair cells take up nitrate ions from soil water where the concentration of nitrate is lower than inside the cell. Explain how. [3]

- active transport (1)
- (movement) against the concentration gradient (1)
- using energy from (aerobic) respiration / mitochondria (1)

**4.** A student adds iodine solution to a food sample and it turns blue-black. Benedict's solution, heated, stays blue. State what the student can conclude. [2]

- starch present (1)
- no reducing sugar (present) (1)

## Common mistakes

- Saying "water moves from high concentration to low concentration" in osmosis — use **water potential** or "dilute solution to concentrated solution".
- Forgetting **"partially permeable membrane"** in osmosis answers, or leaving out the word **net**.
- Writing that plant cells "burst" in water — the wall prevents it; they become turgid.
- Saying active transport moves "with" the gradient, or forgetting "energy from respiration".
- Forgetting to **heat** in the Benedict's test, or writing "red" instead of "brick-red".
- In osmosis practicals, comparing raw mass change instead of percentage change.
- Writing that fats are made of "fatty acids" only — they are made from fatty acids **and glycerol**.
