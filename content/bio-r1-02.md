# Movement into and out of cells; biological molecules

> **In the exam:** Paper 2 usually has 3–4 questions (which process is happening, direction of water movement, food test results). Paper 4 often has a 6–10 mark question on osmosis in plant tissue or on absorption (active transport) linked to roots or the gut. Paper 6 loves the potato/osmosis experiment (percentage change in mass, graphs, variables) and food tests (method, colours, safety).

## What you need to know

- Define diffusion; state that the energy for diffusion comes from the kinetic energy of random movement of molecules and ions.
- Investigate factors affecting diffusion: surface area, temperature, concentration gradient, (Extended) distance.
- State the importance of diffusion of gases and solutes; state that water is important as a solvent.
- Define osmosis (Extended: in terms of water potential); investigate osmosis in plant tissues.
- Extended: explain the effects on plant cells of solutions of different concentrations using the terms turgid, turgor pressure, plasmolysis, flaccid; explain the importance of water potential and osmosis in water uptake by plants and in animal cells.
- Define active transport; state that it needs energy from respiration; Extended: protein carriers move molecules or ions across a membrane; example of ion uptake by root hair cells.
- List the chemical elements in carbohydrates, fats and proteins; state the sub-units of starch, glycogen, cellulose, proteins, fats and oils.
- Describe the food tests for starch, reducing sugars, proteins, fats and oils, vitamin C.
- Extended: describe the structure of DNA.

## Notes

### Diffusion

- **Diffusion**: the net movement of particles from a region of their higher concentration to a region of their lower concentration (i.e. down a concentration gradient), as a result of their random movement.
- Passive: the energy comes from the **kinetic energy of random movement** of molecules/ions; no energy from respiration needed.
- Examples: O₂ and CO₂ in alveoli; CO₂ into leaves through stomata; glucose from blood into cells; urea from cells into blood.

Factors increasing the **rate** of diffusion:

| Factor | Why |
|---|---|
| Larger surface area | more particles can cross at once |
| Higher temperature | particles have more kinetic energy, move faster |
| Steeper concentration gradient | greater net movement in one direction |
| Shorter distance (Extended) | particles have less far to travel (thin walls) |

### Osmosis

- **Osmosis**: the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential (dilute solution) to a region of lower water potential (concentrated solution), through a partially permeable membrane.
- A **partially permeable membrane** lets small molecules (water) through but not larger ones (e.g. sucrose).
- Water potential: pure water has the highest water potential; adding solute lowers it.

Effects on cells:

| Solution outside | Plant cell | Animal cell (e.g. red blood cell) |
|---|---|---|
| Higher water potential (dilute / pure water) | water enters by osmosis; vacuole swells; cell becomes **turgid**; wall stops it bursting | water enters; cell swells and **bursts** (no wall) |
| Same water potential | no net movement | no net movement |
| Lower water potential (concentrated) | water leaves; cell becomes **flaccid**; then membrane pulls away from wall = **plasmolysis** | water leaves; cell shrinks (crenated) |

Extended:
- **Turgor pressure**: the pressure of the cell contents (water in the vacuole) pushing outwards on the cell wall. It keeps plants upright; loss of turgor → **wilting**.
- Root hair cells have a lower water potential than the soil water, so water enters by osmosis.
- Animals must keep tissue fluid and blood at a suitable water potential (link to kidneys) so cells are not damaged.

### Active transport

- **Active transport**: the movement of particles through a cell membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration (i.e. against a concentration gradient), using energy from respiration.
- Extended: **protein carriers** in the membrane bind the molecule/ion and change shape to move it across; energy comes from respiration.
- Examples: uptake of **mineral ions** (e.g. nitrate) by root hair cells; absorption of glucose in the small intestine; reabsorption of glucose in the kidney.
- Cells doing a lot of active transport have **many mitochondria**. Anything that stops respiration (no oxygen, cyanide, low temperature) stops active transport.

### Biological molecules

| Molecule | Elements | Made from |
|---|---|---|
| Carbohydrates | C, H, O | starch, glycogen and cellulose are made from **glucose** |
| Proteins | C, H, O, N (some S) | **amino acids** |
| Fats and oils | C, H, O | **fatty acids and glycerol** |

- Water is important as a **solvent**: in digestion, in excretion (urea dissolved in urine), and in transport (blood plasma, xylem and phloem).

### Food tests

| Test for | Reagent and method | Positive result | Negative |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starch | add iodine solution | **blue-black** | stays orange-brown |
| Reducing sugars (e.g. glucose) | add Benedict's solution, **heat** in water bath (about 80 °C) for a few minutes | green → yellow → orange → **brick-red** (more sugar = further along) | stays blue |
| Protein | add biuret solution | **purple / violet** | stays blue |
| Fats and oils | ethanol emulsion test: shake food with ethanol, pour into water | **cloudy white** emulsion | stays clear |
| Vitamin C | add food solution drop by drop to **DCPIP** | blue DCPIP becomes **colourless** | stays blue |

Paper 6: the fewer drops of juice needed to decolourise a fixed volume of DCPIP, the **more** vitamin C it contains.

### DNA structure (Extended)

- Two strands coiled together to form a **double helix**.
- Each strand contains chemicals called **bases**: A, T, C, G.
- Cross-links between the strands are formed by **pairs of bases**; the bases always pair the same way: **A with T, C with G**.

### Paper 6 skills on this topic

**Osmosis practical (potato cylinders in sucrose solutions):**
- Independent variable: concentration of sucrose solution. Dependent: change in mass (as % change).
- Controlled: size/surface area of cylinders, same potato, volume of solution, time, temperature; blot dry the same way each time.
- Use **percentage change** = (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100, because cylinders start with different masses.
- Plot % change (y) against concentration (x); where the line crosses 0 % change, the solution has the same water potential as the cells.
- Risk: cork borer and scalpel are sharp — cut on a tile, away from fingers.

**Diffusion practical:** agar cubes with indicator in acid; time for colour to disappear; smaller cubes (larger surface area : volume) change faster.

**Food tests safety:** wear eye protection; Benedict's heated in a **water bath**, not a flame; ethanol is **flammable** (no naked flames); biuret contains alkali (irritant).

**Results tables:** column headings with units (e.g. "concentration / mol dm⁻³", "mass / g"), independent variable in the first column, same number of decimal places throughout.

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Diffusion | the net movement of particles from a region of their higher concentration to a region of their lower concentration (i.e. down a concentration gradient), as a result of their random movement |
| Osmosis | the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential (dilute solution) to a region of lower water potential (concentrated solution), through a partially permeable membrane |
| Active transport | the movement of particles through a cell membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration (i.e. against a concentration gradient), using energy from respiration |
| Turgid | a plant cell swollen with water, its contents pushing against the cell wall |
| Plasmolysis | the cell membrane and cytoplasm pull away from the cell wall as the cell loses water by osmosis |
| Flaccid | a plant cell that has lost water and is soft, with no turgor pressure |

## Worked examples

**1.** A potato cylinder has a mass of 2.50 g. After 30 minutes in a concentrated sucrose solution its mass is 2.20 g. Calculate the percentage change in mass. [2]

- change = 2.20 − 2.50 = −0.30 g (1)
- −0.30 ÷ 2.50 × 100 = **−12 %** (1)

**2.** Explain the change in mass in Q1. [3]

- the solution had a lower water potential than the potato cells (1)
- water left the cells by osmosis (1)
- through the partially permeable (cell) membrane (1)

**3.** Extended: root hair cells take up nitrate ions from soil where the concentration is lower than inside the cell. Explain how. [3]

- active transport (1)
- against the concentration gradient using protein carriers in the membrane (1)
- energy from (aerobic) respiration / mitochondria (1)

## Common mistakes

- Saying "water moves from high concentration to low concentration" in osmosis — use **water potential** or "dilute to concentrated solution".
- Forgetting **"partially permeable membrane"** in osmosis answers.
- Writing that plant cells "burst" in water — the wall prevents it; they become turgid.
- Saying active transport moves "with" the gradient, or forgetting "energy from respiration".
- Forgetting to **heat** in the Benedict's test, or writing "red" instead of "brick-red" / "orange".
- In osmosis practicals, comparing raw mass change instead of percentage change.
- Using "net" — examiners want "net movement" in diffusion and osmosis.

## Practice questions

2. Red blood cells are placed in distilled water. Describe and explain what happens. [3]
3. A cylinder of 3.20 g becomes 3.60 g in water. Calculate the percentage change in mass to one decimal place. [2]
4. A student adds biuret solution to egg white and Benedict's solution (heated) to egg white. Predict the colours seen. [2]
5. Juice A needed 5 drops to decolourise 1 cm³ DCPIP; juice B needed 12 drops. Which has more vitamin C? Explain. [2]
6. Extended: describe the structure of DNA. [3]
7. Suggest why cells lining the small intestine have many mitochondria. [2]
