## Notes

### What enzymes are

- **Enzymes**: proteins that are involved in all metabolic reactions, where they function as biological catalysts.
- A catalyst increases the rate of a reaction and is unchanged at the end (you also meet this in Chemistry). So enzymes are **not used up** and can be reused many times.
- Without enzymes, reactions at body temperature would be far too slow to keep cells alive.
- Because enzymes are **proteins**, their shape can be changed by heat and by pH.

### How enzymes work (Supplement)

1. Each enzyme has an **active site** with a specific shape.
2. The **substrate** has a shape **complementary** to the active site, so it fits.
3. The substrate binds to the active site, forming an **enzyme–substrate complex**.
4. The reaction happens and the **product(s)** are released; the enzyme is unchanged and can catalyse another reaction.

**Specificity**: each enzyme catalyses only one type of reaction, because only one substrate has a shape complementary to (fits) its active site. Example: amylase breaks down starch but not protein.

### Effect of temperature

Core — describe: as temperature rises, activity increases to a maximum at the **optimum temperature**, then decreases rapidly at higher temperatures.

| Part of the graph | Supplement explanation |
|---|---|
| Low temperature → rate low | enzyme and substrate molecules have little **kinetic energy**; move slowly; few **effective collisions**; few enzyme–substrate complexes form |
| Rising to the optimum | more kinetic energy → more **frequent effective collisions** → more enzyme–substrate complexes formed per second |
| Optimum (about 37 °C for many human enzymes) | fastest rate |
| Above the optimum → rate falls quickly to zero | enzyme is **denatured**: the **shape of the active site changes**, so the substrate no longer fits / is no longer complementary; fewer (then no) enzyme–substrate complexes |

- Denaturation is **permanent**. Low temperature does **not** denature enzymes — they are just slow, and work again when warmed.

### Effect of pH

- Core — describe: each enzyme has an **optimum pH** at which activity is highest; activity falls either side.
- Examples: proteases in the stomach work best in acidic conditions (about pH 2, provided by hydrochloric acid); amylase and lipase in the small intestine work best in slightly alkaline/neutral conditions.
- Supplement: away from the optimum the **shape of the active site** changes, so the substrate **fits** less well and fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form; at extreme pH the enzyme is **denatured**.
- Graph: a peak at the optimum, falling on both sides.

### Investigating enzyme activity (Core)

Useful background for experiments (not separate learning objectives):
- More **substrate** → faster rate until all active sites are occupied, then the rate levels off.
- More **enzyme** → faster rate if there is plenty of substrate.

### Paper 6 skills on this topic

**Amylase and starch (iodine on a spotting tile):**
- Mix starch solution and amylase at a set temperature (water bath); every 30 s take a drop and add to iodine on a spotting tile.
- **End point**: iodine stays **orange-brown** (no starch left). Record the time.
- Rate = 1 ÷ time (or 1000 ÷ time); shorter time = faster rate.
- Independent variable: temperature (or pH, using buffer solutions). Dependent: time for starch to disappear.
- Controlled: volume and concentration of starch and amylase; pH (buffer); sampling interval; drop size.
- **Equilibrate**: put enzyme and substrate in separate tubes in the water bath for about 5 min before mixing, so both start at the test temperature.
- **Control**: boiled (denatured) amylase, or water instead of amylase — shows the enzyme causes the change.
- Limitations: judging the colour change is subjective; sampling every 30 s means the real end point could be up to 30 s earlier; water bath temperature may drift. Improvements: shorter sampling intervals, a thermostatically controlled water bath, repeats and a mean.

**Catalase and hydrogen peroxide:** catalase (in potato, liver or yeast) breaks down hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen. Measure the volume of oxygen in a gas syringe or upturned measuring cylinder in a fixed time, or count bubbles. Risk: hydrogen peroxide is an irritant — wear eye protection.

**Protease and protein:** e.g. milk powder suspension goes from cloudy to clear as protein is digested; time how long until a cross under the tube becomes visible.

**Graphs:** independent variable on the x-axis; axis labels with units; small crosses; ruled point-to-point lines or a smooth best-fit curve; do not extrapolate beyond the data unless asked.

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Enzymes | proteins that are involved in all metabolic reactions, where they function as biological catalysts |
| Active site | the part of the enzyme where the substrate binds; its shape is complementary to the substrate |
| Substrate | the molecule(s) the enzyme acts on |
| Product | the molecule(s) formed by the reaction and released from the active site |
| Enzyme–substrate complex | formed when the substrate binds to the active site |
| Denatured | the shape of the enzyme's active site has changed permanently, so the substrate no longer fits |
| Optimum | the temperature or pH at which the enzyme works fastest |

## Worked examples

**1.** Explain why the rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction increases from 10 °C to 35 °C. [3]

- molecules gain kinetic energy / move faster (1)
- more frequent effective collisions between enzyme and substrate (1)
- more enzyme–substrate complexes formed (per unit time) (1)

**2.** Explain why the rate falls to zero at 60 °C. [3]

- enzyme denatured (1)
- shape of active site changed (1)
- substrate no longer complementary / cannot fit, so no enzyme–substrate complexes form (1)

**3.** At 40 °C, starch disappeared in 80 s. Calculate the rate as 1000 ÷ time. Give the units. [2]

- 1000 ÷ 80 = **12.5** (1)
- units: **s⁻¹** (per second) (1)

**4.** Suggest why the amylase and starch solutions were left in the water bath for 5 minutes before mixing. [1]

- so both reach the required temperature before the reaction starts (1)

## Common mistakes

- Saying enzymes are "killed" — they are not alive; they are **denatured**.
- Saying the active site is the "same shape" as the substrate — it is **complementary**.
- Saying low temperatures denature enzymes.
- Writing "the enzyme has more energy so works harder" — refer to **kinetic energy** and **frequency of effective collisions**.
- Describing a graph without quoting data: give the optimum value with units read from the graph.
- In denaturation answers, missing a link in the chain: shape of active site changes → substrate cannot fit → no enzyme–substrate complexes.
- Forgetting that enzymes are **proteins** (a favourite 1-mark question).

## Practice questions

1. Describe what is meant by an *enzyme*. [2]
2. Explain why each enzyme only catalyses one reaction. [2]
3. A protease works in the stomach at pH 2. Predict its activity at pH 8 and explain. [3]
4. Describe the control you would use to show that amylase is needed for starch to be broken down. [1]
5. In an amylase experiment, times were 300 s (20 °C), 120 s (30 °C), 60 s (40 °C), 240 s (50 °C) and no colour change at 60 °C. State the optimum temperature from these data and suggest how to find it more precisely. [2]
6. Name **three** variables that should be kept constant in Q5. [3]
7. Explain why putting food in a fridge slows down decay but boiling food stops it. [3]
8. Calculate the rate (1000 ÷ time) at 30 °C in Q5. [1]

1. A protein (1); that acts as a biological catalyst / speeds up (metabolic) reactions without being changed (1).
2. Active site has a specific shape (1); only one substrate is complementary / fits (1).
3. Low / no activity (1); pH 8 is far from its optimum (pH 2) (1); active site changes shape / denatured, substrate does not fit (1).
4. Repeat with boiled (cooled) amylase or with water instead of amylase.
5. 40 °C (1); repeat with smaller intervals between 30 °C and 50 °C, e.g. every 2 °C (1).
6. Any three: volume/concentration of starch; volume/concentration of amylase; pH (buffer); sampling interval; volume of iodine.
7. Fridge: low temperature, enzymes (of microorganisms) have little kinetic energy, fewer effective collisions, slow reactions (1); not denatured, so work again when warmed (1); boiling denatures enzymes (active site changes shape) / kills microorganisms, so decay stops (1).
