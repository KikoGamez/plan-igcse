# Enzymes

## What you need to know

- Define *catalyst*; define *enzymes* as proteins that function as biological catalysts.
- Describe why enzymes are important in all living organisms (to keep reaction rates fast enough for life).
- Describe enzyme action with reference to the active site, enzyme–substrate complex, substrate and product; the shape of the active site is complementary to the substrate.
- Investigate and describe the effect of temperature and pH on enzyme activity.
- Extended: explain enzyme specificity; explain the effect of temperature in terms of kinetic energy, frequency of effective collisions, shape and denaturation; explain the effect of pH in terms of shape and fit and denaturation.

## Notes

### What enzymes are

- **Catalyst**: a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction and is not changed by the reaction.
- **Enzymes**: proteins that are involved in all metabolic reactions, where they function as biological catalysts.
- Without enzymes, reactions at body temperature would be too slow to sustain life. Enzymes allow reactions to happen quickly at body temperature.
- Enzymes are not used up: one enzyme molecule can be reused many times.

### How enzymes work (lock and key)

1. Each enzyme has an **active site** with a specific shape.
2. The **substrate** has a shape **complementary** to the active site, so it fits.
3. The substrate binds, forming an **enzyme–substrate complex**.
4. The reaction happens and **products** are released; the enzyme is unchanged and can be used again.

Extended — **specificity**: each enzyme catalyses only one reaction because only one substrate has a shape complementary to its active site.

### Effect of temperature

Typical graph: rate rises to a peak (optimum) then falls steeply.

| Part of the graph | Explanation (Extended wording) |
|---|---|
| Low temperature → rate low but rising | enzyme and substrate molecules have little **kinetic energy**; move slowly; few **effective collisions**; fewer enzyme–substrate complexes |
| Rising to the optimum | more kinetic energy → more frequent effective collisions → more enzyme–substrate complexes formed per second |
| Optimum (about 37 °C for human enzymes) | fastest rate |
| Above the optimum → rate falls quickly to zero | enzyme is **denatured**: the **shape of the active site changes**, so the substrate no longer fits / is no longer complementary; fewer enzyme–substrate complexes |

- Denaturation is permanent. Low temperature does **not** denature enzymes — they are just inactive (slow) and work again if warmed.
- Enzymes from organisms in hot springs have higher optimum temperatures.

### Effect of pH

- Each enzyme has an **optimum pH**: pepsin (stomach) about pH 2; salivary amylase about pH 7; trypsin and lipase in the small intestine slightly alkaline (about pH 8).
- Extended: above or below the optimum, the **shape of the active site** changes, so the substrate fits less well; fewer enzyme–substrate complexes; at extremes the enzyme is **denatured**.
- Graph: a peak at the optimum, falling on both sides (roughly symmetrical).

### Other things that change the rate (for experiments)

- Increasing **substrate concentration** increases rate until all active sites are occupied; then the rate levels off (enzyme concentration becomes the limit).
- Increasing **enzyme concentration** increases rate if there is plenty of substrate.

### Paper 6 skills on this topic

**Amylase and starch (iodine test on a spotting tile):**
- Mix starch solution and amylase at a set temperature (water bath); every 30 s take a drop and add to iodine on a spotting tile.
- End point: iodine stays **orange-brown** (no starch left). Record the time.
- Rate = 1 ÷ time (or 1000 ÷ time); shorter time = faster rate.
- Independent variable: temperature (or pH, using buffer solutions). Dependent: time for starch to disappear.
- Controlled: volume and concentration of starch and amylase; pH (use buffer); time intervals for sampling; mixing.
- **Equilibrate**: put enzyme and substrate in separate tubes in the water bath for about 5 min before mixing, so both start at the test temperature.
- **Control**: boiled (denatured) amylase, or water instead of amylase — shows the enzyme causes the change.
- Limitations: judging the colour change is subjective; sampling every 30 s means the true time could be up to 30 s earlier; water bath temperature may drift. Improvements: shorter sampling intervals, use a colorimeter, thermostatically controlled water bath, repeats and a mean.

**Catalase and hydrogen peroxide:** catalase (from potato, liver or yeast) breaks hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen. Measure volume of oxygen in a gas syringe or upturned measuring cylinder in a fixed time, or count bubbles. Risk: hydrogen peroxide is an irritant — wear eye protection.

**Graphs:** independent variable on x-axis, axis labels with units, plot points with small crosses, join with a ruled line point to point or a smooth best-fit curve (do not extend beyond the data).

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Catalyst | a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction and is not changed by the reaction |
| Enzymes | proteins that are involved in all metabolic reactions, where they function as biological catalysts |
| Active site | the part of the enzyme where the substrate binds; its shape is complementary to the substrate |
| Substrate | the molecule(s) the enzyme acts on |
| Enzyme–substrate complex | formed when the substrate binds to the active site |
| Denatured | the enzyme's shape (active site) has changed permanently, so the substrate no longer fits |
| Optimum | the temperature or pH at which the enzyme works fastest |

## Worked examples

**1.** Extended: explain why the rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction increases from 10 °C to 35 °C. [3]

- molecules gain kinetic energy / move faster (1)
- more frequent (effective) collisions between enzyme and substrate (1)
- more enzyme–substrate complexes formed (per unit time) (1)

**2.** Extended: explain why the rate falls to zero at 60 °C. [3]

- enzyme denatured (1)
- shape of active site changed (1)
- substrate no longer complementary / cannot fit, so no enzyme–substrate complexes form (1)

**3.** At 40 °C, starch disappeared in 80 s. Calculate the rate as 1000 ÷ time. Give the units. [2]

- 1000 ÷ 80 = **12.5** (1)
- units: **s⁻¹** (1)

**4.** Suggest why the amylase and starch solutions were left in the water bath for 5 minutes before mixing. [1]

- so both reach the required temperature before the reaction starts (1)

## Common mistakes

- Saying enzymes are "killed" — they are not alive; they are **denatured**.
- Saying the active site is the "same shape" as the substrate — it is **complementary**.
- Saying low temperatures denature enzymes.
- Writing "the enzyme has more energy so works harder" — refer to **kinetic energy and collisions**.
- Describing a graph without quoting data: always give the optimum value and units from the graph.
- In explanations of denaturation, forgetting the chain: shape of active site changes → substrate cannot fit → no enzyme–substrate complexes.

## Practice questions

2. Explain why each enzyme only catalyses one reaction. [2]
3. Pepsin works in the stomach. Predict its activity at pH 8 and explain. [3]
4. Describe the control you would use to show that amylase is needed for starch to be broken down. [1]
5. In an amylase experiment, times were 300 s (20 °C), 120 s (30 °C), 60 s (40 °C), 240 s (50 °C) and no colour change at 60 °C. State the optimum temperature from these data and suggest how to find it more precisely. [2]
6. Name **three** variables that should be kept constant in Q5. [3]
7. Explain why putting food in a fridge slows down decay but boiling food stops it. [3]
