## Notes

### Pathogens and transmission

- **Pathogen**: a disease-causing organism (e.g. some bacteria, viruses, fungi).
- **Transmissible disease**: a disease in which the pathogen can be passed from one host to another.
- **Direct contact**: through blood and other body fluids (e.g. HIV through sexual contact or shared needles).
- **Indirect**: from contaminated **surfaces**, **food**, **animals** (e.g. insects) and **air** (droplets from coughs and sneezes).

**Supplement – features of viruses**: a **protein coat** surrounding **genetic material**. (Viruses are not cells: no cytoplasm, cell membrane or ribosomes.)

### Body defences

| Defence | How it protects |
|---|---|
| Skin | tough barrier stops pathogens entering |
| Hairs in the nose | trap dust and pathogens in the air breathed in |
| Mucus | sticky; traps pathogens in the airways |
| Stomach acid | hydrochloric acid kills pathogens in food |
| White blood cells | phagocytes engulf pathogens (phagocytosis); lymphocytes produce antibodies |

### Controlling the spread of disease

| Measure | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Clean water supply | water treated to kill pathogens, so drinking water does not spread disease |
| Hygienic food preparation | wash hands, cook food thoroughly, keep raw and cooked food apart, keep food cold → pathogens not transferred to food or killed |
| Good personal hygiene | washing hands (after the toilet, before eating), covering coughs → fewer pathogens passed on |
| Waste disposal | rubbish removed so it does not attract flies and rats that carry pathogens |
| Sewage treatment | removes pathogens from human waste before water is returned to rivers (stages not required) |

Vaccinations are available for **some** pathogens to help control the spread of diseases.

### Antigens and antibodies (Supplement)

- Each pathogen has its own **antigens**, which have **specific shapes** (molecules on the pathogen's surface).
- **Antibodies** are **proteins** that bind to antigens, leading to **direct destruction** of pathogens or **marking** of pathogens for destruction by **phagocytes**.
- Specific antibodies have **complementary shapes** which fit specific antigens — so an antibody against one pathogen does not fit the antigens of a different pathogen.
- Antibodies are made by **lymphocytes**.

### Active immunity and vaccination (Supplement)

- **Active immunity**: defence against a pathogen by antibody production in the body.
- It is gained **after an infection** by a pathogen **or by vaccination**.

Process of vaccination:
1. **Weakened pathogens or their antigens** are put into the body.
2. The antigens stimulate an **immune response** by **lymphocytes**, which produce **antibodies**.
3. **Memory cells** are produced that give **long-term immunity**.

If the real pathogen enters later, memory cells cause a **faster** response with **more** antibodies, so the pathogen is destroyed before the person becomes ill.

On a graph of antibody concentration: the first exposure gives a slow, small rise; a second exposure (or booster) gives a faster and much larger rise.

**Role of vaccination in controlling spread**: vaccinated people do not become ill and do not pass the pathogen on. When a large proportion of a population is vaccinated, the pathogen has few hosts to spread to, so the number of cases falls and even people who are not vaccinated are less likely to meet it. Some diseases can be (almost) eliminated this way.

### Drugs and antibiotics

- **Drug**: any substance taken into the body that modifies or affects chemical reactions in the body.
- **Antibiotics** are used to treat **bacterial infections**: they kill bacteria (or stop them growing).
- Antibiotics **do not affect viruses** — so they are useless against colds or influenza.
- Some bacteria are **resistant** to antibiotics, which reduces the effectiveness of antibiotics.

**Supplement – limiting resistance (e.g. MRSA)**:
1. In a population of bacteria, a few may be resistant (because of a mutation).
2. Every time an antibiotic is used, the non-resistant bacteria are killed but the **resistant bacteria survive** and **reproduce**, passing on resistance.
3. The more often antibiotics are used, the more often resistant bacteria are selected, so resistant strains such as **MRSA** become common.
4. Using antibiotics **only when essential** (not for viral infections or minor infections) means fewer chances for resistant bacteria to be selected, so resistance develops more slowly and antibiotics stay effective.

This is an example of **natural selection** — see *Inheritance, variation and selection* (development of antibiotic-resistant strains of bacteria).

## Worked examples

**1.** Outline how vaccination provides long-term protection against a disease. [4]

- weakened pathogens / antigens put into the body (1)
- antigens stimulate an immune response by lymphocytes (1)
- lymphocytes produce antibodies (1)
- memory cells are produced, giving long-term immunity / a faster response on later infection (1)

**2.** Explain why antibodies made after a measles vaccination do not protect against chickenpox. [2]

- antibodies have a specific shape, complementary to one antigen (1)
- chickenpox virus has different antigens / different shape, so the antibodies cannot bind (1)

**3.** Suggest why a doctor will not prescribe antibiotics for influenza. [2]

- influenza is caused by a virus (1)
- antibiotics do not affect viruses / unnecessary use can lead to resistant bacteria (1)

**4.** Explain the importance of a clean water supply and sewage treatment in controlling the spread of disease. [3]

- human waste / sewage contains pathogens (1)
- sewage treatment removes pathogens before water returns to rivers (1)
- clean (treated) drinking water does not carry pathogens, so they are not passed to new hosts (1)

## Common mistakes

- Writing "antibiotics kill viruses" or "vaccines kill pathogens".
- Saying antibodies "eat" pathogens — **phagocytes** engulf; antibodies **bind to antigens**.
- Saying bacteria "become resistant" or "get used to" an antibiotic — resistant bacteria are **selected**: they survive and reproduce.
- Forgetting **memory cells** in vaccination answers.
- Mixing up antigen (on the pathogen) and antibody (protein made by lymphocytes).
- Defining a pathogen as "a disease" — it is a disease-causing **organism**.
