# Diseases and immunity

> **In the exam:** Paper 2 has 2–4 questions (defences, active vs passive immunity, antibiotics). Paper 4 often has a 6–12 mark section on vaccination, antibodies, antibiotic resistance or controlling the spread of a disease, frequently with a graph of antibody concentration over time. Paper 6 occasionally tests antibiotic discs on agar plates (zones of inhibition, aseptic technique).

## What you need to know

- Define pathogen and transmissible disease; state that the pathogen is transmitted by direct contact (blood, other body fluids) or indirectly (contaminated surfaces, food, animals, air).
- Describe the body defences: skin, hairs in the nose, mucus, stomach acid, white blood cells.
- Explain the importance of hygiene in controlling the spread of disease: clean water, food hygiene, personal hygiene, waste disposal, sewage treatment.
- Extended: describe antibodies as proteins that bind to antigens; explain that each pathogen has its own antigens with specific shapes, so specific antibodies are needed.
- Extended: define active immunity; explain how vaccination gives long-term immunity (memory cells); explain the role of vaccination in controlling the spread of diseases.
- Extended: define passive immunity; explain the importance of breast-feeding; state that memory cells are not made in passive immunity.
- Extended: state that some diseases are caused by the immune system targeting body cells (Type 1 diabetes).
- Describe the use of antibiotics to treat bacterial infections; state that they do not affect viruses; Extended: explain why; explain how antibiotic resistance develops (e.g. MRSA) and how to reduce it.

## Notes

### Pathogens and transmission

- **Pathogen**: a disease-causing organism (bacteria, viruses, fungi, protoctists).
- **Transmissible disease**: a disease in which the pathogen can be passed from one host to another.
- **Direct contact**: through blood or other body fluids (e.g. HIV through sexual intercourse or shared needles).
- **Indirect contact**: from contaminated surfaces, food, animals (e.g. mosquitoes), or the air (droplets from coughs and sneezes).

### Body defences

| Type | Defence | How it works |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanical barriers | skin | tough outer layer stops pathogens entering |
| | hairs in the nose | trap dust and pathogens |
| Chemical barriers | mucus | traps pathogens in airways; swept away by cilia |
| | stomach acid (hydrochloric acid) | kills pathogens in food |
| Cells | phagocytes | engulf and digest pathogens (phagocytosis) |
| | lymphocytes | produce antibodies |
| Help | vaccination | stimulates immunity in advance |

### Controlling spread (hygiene)

- **Clean water supply** — prevents water-borne diseases (cholera).
- **Hygienic food preparation** — wash hands, cook food thoroughly, keep raw and cooked food apart, refrigerate.
- **Good personal hygiene** — washing hands, covering coughs.
- **Waste disposal** — collected waste does not attract flies and rats.
- **Sewage treatment** — removes pathogens before water is returned to rivers.

### Antibodies and antigens (Extended)

- **Antigens** are molecules (often proteins) on the surface of pathogens. Each pathogen has antigens with **specific shapes**.
- **Antibodies** are proteins that bind to antigens, leading to direct destruction of pathogens or marking of pathogens for destruction by phagocytes.
- Antibodies have a shape **complementary** to one particular antigen, so a **specific antibody** is needed for each pathogen.
- Lymphocytes with the right antibody divide (by mitosis) to make many cells producing that antibody; some become **memory cells**.

### Active immunity (Extended)

- **Active immunity**: defence against a pathogen by antibody production in the body.
- Gained by **infection** or by **vaccination**. It is slow to develop but **long-term**, because **memory cells** are made.

How vaccination works:
1. A vaccine contains **weakened (harmless) pathogens or their antigens** (or dead pathogens).
2. The antigens trigger an **immune response**: **lymphocytes** produce **antibodies**.
3. **Memory cells** are produced and remain in the blood.
4. On later infection, memory cells respond **faster**, producing **more** antibodies, so the pathogen is destroyed before symptoms develop → **long-term immunity**.

Graph of antibody concentration: after the first exposure, a slow, small rise (primary response); after the second exposure, a faster, much higher rise (secondary response).

Vaccination controls the spread of disease: if a large proportion of a population is immune, the pathogen cannot pass easily from host to host, so unvaccinated people are also protected (herd immunity).

### Passive immunity (Extended)

- **Passive immunity**: short-term defence against a pathogen by antibodies acquired from another individual, e.g. across the placenta and in breast milk.
- **No memory cells** are made, so it is short-term (antibodies are broken down).
- Breast-feeding is important because the baby's immune system is not fully developed; antibodies in milk protect it in the first months.

### When the immune system attacks the body (Extended)

- Some diseases are caused by the immune system targeting and destroying body cells, e.g. **Type 1 diabetes** (the insulin-secreting cells of the pancreas are destroyed).

### Antibiotics

- A **drug** is any substance taken into the body that modifies or affects chemical reactions in the body.
- **Antibiotics** treat **bacterial** infections by killing bacteria or stopping their growth.
- They have **no effect on viruses**. Extended: antibiotics act on structures/processes found in bacteria (e.g. cell walls, bacterial ribosomes); viruses do not have these structures and reproduce inside host cells.
- Extended — **resistance** develops by natural selection:
  1. A random **mutation** makes some bacteria resistant.
  2. When the antibiotic is used, non-resistant bacteria die; **resistant bacteria survive** and reproduce.
  3. They pass on the resistance allele/gene; the population becomes mostly resistant (e.g. **MRSA**).
- To reduce resistance: use antibiotics **only when essential**; always **complete the course**; do not use them for viral infections; limit use in farming.

### Paper 6 skills on this topic

- **Antibiotic discs on an agar plate** seeded with (safe) bacteria: measure the diameter of the **clear zone** (zone of inhibition) around each disc; the larger the zone, the more effective the antibiotic. Area = πr² if asked.
- Variables: independent — type or concentration of antibiotic; dependent — diameter of clear zone; controlled — same bacteria, same volume of culture, same disc size, same incubation temperature and time.
- **Control**: a disc soaked in sterile water.
- **Aseptic technique**: sterilise equipment (flame the loop, autoclave agar); lift the lid only slightly; tape the lid with two pieces of tape but **do not seal completely** (oxygen still enters, preventing harmful anaerobic bacteria); incubate at **25 °C** (not 37 °C, to avoid growing human pathogens); wash hands; do not open plates after incubation.

## Worked examples

**1.** Extended: explain how vaccination provides long-term protection against a disease. [4]

- vaccine contains weakened pathogen / antigens (1)
- lymphocytes produce (specific) antibodies (1)
- memory cells produced (1)
- on infection, memory cells give a faster and larger antibody response, so pathogen destroyed before symptoms (1)

**2.** Extended: explain why antibodies against measles do not protect against chickenpox. [2]

- antibodies have a specific shape, complementary to one antigen (1)
- chickenpox virus has different antigens / different shape, so antibodies cannot bind (1)

**3.** Extended: explain why passive immunity only lasts a few weeks. [2]

- antibodies are broken down / not replaced (1)
- no memory cells are made (1)

**4.** Suggest why a doctor will not prescribe antibiotics for influenza. [2]

- influenza is caused by a virus (1)
- antibiotics do not affect viruses / unnecessary use increases resistance (1)

## Common mistakes

- Saying "antibiotics kill viruses" or "vaccines kill pathogens".
- Saying antibodies "eat" pathogens — **phagocytes** engulf; antibodies **bind to antigens**.
- Saying antibiotics make bacteria "become resistant" or "get used to it" — resistance arises from a random **mutation** and is **selected for**.
- Forgetting **memory cells** in vaccination answers.
- Saying passive immunity is "short-term because the baby's immune system is weak" — it is because **no memory cells** are made.
- Mixing up antigen (on the pathogen) and antibody (made by lymphocytes).
