# Inheritance, variation and selection

> **In the exam:** Paper 2 has 4–6 questions (definitions, cross ratios, mitosis vs meiosis, natural selection). Paper 4 almost always has a genetics question (10–15 marks) with a genetic diagram or pedigree, plus natural selection/selective breeding. Paper 6 links through variation data: measuring a feature in a sample, tallying, drawing histograms (continuous) or bar charts (discontinuous).

## What you need to know

- Define inheritance, chromosome, gene, allele; describe the inheritance of sex in humans (XX, XY).
- Extended: state that DNA controls cell function by controlling protein production; describe protein synthesis (mRNA, ribosomes); explain that all body cells contain the same genes but only some are expressed.
- Define haploid and diploid (human: 23 and 46); define mitosis and state its roles; Extended: describe chromosome replication and separation in mitosis; describe stem cells.
- Define meiosis; state that it is involved in gamete production.
- Define genotype, phenotype, homozygous, heterozygous, dominant, recessive; interpret pedigree diagrams; use genetic diagrams and Punnett squares to predict ratios (1:1, 3:1); Extended: test cross; codominance (ABO blood groups); sex linkage (colour blindness).
- Define variation; continuous and discontinuous variation; Extended: causes of each.
- Define mutation and gene mutation; state that ionising radiation and some chemicals increase the rate of mutation.
- Extended: sickle-cell anaemia and resistance to malaria.
- Define adaptive feature; Extended: fitness; adaptive features of hydrophytes and xerophytes.
- Describe natural selection; Extended: define adaptation; antibiotic resistance; selective breeding and how it differs from natural selection.

## Notes

### Chromosomes, genes and alleles

- **Inheritance**: the transmission of genetic information from generation to generation.
- **Chromosome**: a thread-like structure of DNA, carrying genetic information in the form of genes.
- **Gene**: a length of DNA that codes for a protein.
- **Allele**: an alternative form of a gene.
- Sex: females **XX**, males **XY**. Mother always passes X; father passes X or Y, so a 50 % chance of each sex.

### Protein synthesis (Extended)

- The **sequence of bases** in a gene determines the **sequence of amino acids** in a protein (e.g. enzymes, antibodies, receptors for neurotransmitters).
- The gene (DNA) stays in the **nucleus**; a copy is made as **mRNA**.
- mRNA moves to a **ribosome** in the cytoplasm; the ribosome assembles amino acids into a protein in the order coded by the mRNA.
- Most body cells contain the **same genes**, but many genes are not **expressed** — each cell only makes the proteins it needs.

### Cell division

- **Haploid nucleus**: a nucleus containing a single set of chromosomes (human gametes: 23).
- **Diploid nucleus**: a nucleus containing two sets of chromosomes (human body cells: 46, in 23 pairs).
- **Mitosis**: nuclear division giving rise to genetically identical cells. Used for **growth, repair of damaged tissues, replacement of cells, asexual reproduction**.
  - Extended: chromosomes are **replicated** (copied) before mitosis; during mitosis the copies **separate**, so each new cell gets an identical set and the chromosome number is kept.
  - Extended — **stem cells**: unspecialised cells that divide by mitosis to produce daughter cells that can become specialised for specific functions.
- **Meiosis**: a reduction division in which the chromosome number is halved from diploid to haploid, resulting in genetically different cells. Used to make **gametes**.

| | Mitosis | Meiosis |
|---|---|---|
| Cells made | genetically identical | genetically different |
| Chromosome number | stays the same (diploid → diploid) | halved (diploid → haploid) |
| Where/when | growth, repair, asexual reproduction | gamete production (testes, ovaries, anthers, ovules) |

### Monohybrid inheritance

- **Genotype**: the genetic make-up of an organism in terms of the alleles present.
- **Phenotype**: the observable features of an organism.
- **Homozygous**: having two identical alleles of a particular gene (e.g. TT, tt). Two identical homozygous individuals that breed together are **pure-breeding**.
- **Heterozygous**: having two different alleles of a particular gene (e.g. Tt).
- **Dominant**: an allele that is expressed if it is present in the genotype.
- **Recessive**: an allele that is only expressed when there is no dominant allele of the gene present in the genotype.

**How to lay out a genetic diagram** (examiners award a mark for each line):
1. Parental phenotypes
2. Parental genotypes
3. Gametes (circle them)
4. Punnett square / offspring genotypes
5. Offspring phenotypes
6. Ratio

Example — tall (T) dominant to short (t); Tt × Tt:

| | T | t |
|---|---|---|
| **T** | TT | Tt |
| **t** | Tt | tt |

Genotypes 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt → phenotypes **3 tall : 1 short**. Tt × tt gives **1 : 1**.

**Test cross** (Extended): cross an individual showing the dominant phenotype with a **homozygous recessive**. If any offspring show the recessive phenotype, the unknown parent was heterozygous; if all show the dominant, it was (probably) homozygous dominant.

**Pedigree diagrams**: if two unaffected parents have an affected child, the condition is **recessive** and both parents are heterozygous.

### Codominance and sex linkage (Extended)

- **Codominance**: both alleles in a heterozygote are expressed in the phenotype. ABO blood groups: alleles **Iᴬ**, **Iᴮ** (codominant) and **Iᴼ** (recessive).
  - Iᴬ Iᴼ × Iᴮ Iᴼ → Iᴬ Iᴮ (AB), Iᴬ Iᴼ (A), Iᴮ Iᴼ (B), Iᴼ Iᴼ (O): **1 : 1 : 1 : 1**.
- **Sex-linked characteristic**: a characteristic in which the gene responsible is located on a sex chromosome, making it more common in one sex than in the other.
  - Red–green colour blindness: recessive allele on the X chromosome (Xᵇ). Males have only one X, so one recessive allele makes them colour blind.
  - Carrier mother XᴮXᵇ × normal father XᴮY → daughters XᴮXᴮ, XᴮXᵇ (all normal vision, half carriers); sons XᴮY, XᵇY (**half of sons colour blind**).

### Variation

- **Variation**: differences between individuals of the same species.
- **Continuous variation**: a range of phenotypes between two extremes (height, mass) — histogram/line graph. Extended: caused by **genes and the environment** (many genes).
- **Discontinuous variation**: a limited number of distinct phenotypes, no intermediates (ABO blood group, tongue rolling) — bar chart. Extended: caused by **genes only**.
- **Mutation**: genetic change. **Gene mutation**: a random change in the base sequence of DNA. Mutation is the way new alleles are formed. Rate increased by **ionising radiation** and some **chemicals**.
- Extended — **sickle-cell anaemia**: a gene mutation changes haemoglobin; homozygous (HˢHˢ) → sickle-shaped red blood cells that carry less oxygen and block capillaries. Heterozygotes (HᴬHˢ) have **resistance to malaria**, so the allele is more common where malaria is common.

### Adaptation and selection

- **Adaptive feature**: an inherited feature that helps an organism to survive and reproduce in its environment. Extended: the inherited functional features of an organism that increase its fitness.
- **Fitness** (Extended): the probability of that organism surviving and reproducing in the environment in which it is found.
- Extended — **xerophytes** (dry habitats, e.g. cactus): thick waxy cuticle, sunken stomata, few stomata, rolled leaves, hairs (trap moist air), leaves as spines, deep/extensive roots, water storage in stems. **Hydrophytes** (water plants): stomata on the upper surface, air spaces for buoyancy, little xylem/support tissue, thin or no cuticle.

**Natural selection** (key points in order):
1. **Variation** within a population (due to mutation and sexual reproduction).
2. More offspring are produced than the environment can support.
3. **Competition** for resources (food, mates) / struggle for survival.
4. Individuals with **better adaptive features** are more likely to **survive and reproduce**.
5. They **pass on their alleles** to offspring.
6. Over many generations, the allele frequency changes — **evolution**: the change in adaptive features of a population over time as a result of natural selection.

- Extended — **process of adaptation**: the process, resulting from natural selection, by which populations become more suited to their environment over many generations.
- Antibiotic resistance in bacteria is an example (see Diseases and immunity).

**Selective breeding**: humans choose individuals with **desirable features**, breed them, choose the best offspring and repeat over **many generations** (e.g. high-yield crops, disease resistance, cows with high milk yield).
- Extended difference: in selective breeding **humans** choose the features; in natural selection the **environment** "chooses". Selective breeding is usually faster and the features may not help survival.

### Paper 6 skills on this topic

- Measure a feature (e.g. leaf length) in a sample of 20–30; group into **classes** of equal width (e.g. 30–39 mm, 40–49 mm), tally, draw a **histogram** with no gaps; discontinuous data → bar chart with gaps.
- Sample randomly; larger samples are more representative.

## Worked examples

**1.** In pea plants, purple flowers (P) are dominant to white (p). Two heterozygous plants are crossed. Use a genetic diagram to show the expected ratio of offspring. [4]

- parental genotypes Pp × Pp (1)
- gametes P, p and P, p (1)
- offspring PP, Pp, Pp, pp (1)
- 3 purple : 1 white (1)

**2.** Extended: a man with blood group AB has children with a woman with group O. State the possible blood groups of the children. [3]

- genotypes IᴬIᴮ × IᴼIᴼ (1)
- offspring IᴬIᴼ and IᴮIᴼ (1)
- blood groups **A and B only**, 1 : 1 (1)

**3.** Explain how a population of insects becomes resistant to an insecticide. [4]

- variation / random mutation gives some insects resistance (1)
- insecticide kills non-resistant insects (1)
- resistant insects survive and reproduce (1)
- pass on the resistance allele; over generations most of population resistant (1)

## Common mistakes

- Confusing gene and allele.
- Writing gametes with two letters (gametes are haploid: one allele each).
- Forgetting to give the **ratio and phenotypes** at the end of a genetic diagram.
- Saying organisms "adapt because they need to" or "decide to change" — mutation is **random**, then selected.
- Saying mitosis produces gametes in animals (it is **meiosis**).
- In sex linkage, writing alleles without the X (e.g. "b" instead of Xᵇ).
- Confusing continuous (histogram) and discontinuous (bar chart) variation.
