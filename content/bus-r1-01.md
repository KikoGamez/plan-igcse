# Business activity

> **In the exam:** Appears in both papers. Paper 1 often opens a question with "Define…" [2] or "Identify two…" [2] on terms such as opportunity cost, added value or limited liability, then builds to "Explain two…" [6] and a "justify your answer" question [6] about which type of business organisation suits a business in the data. In Paper 2 the case-study business's legal form, sector and the entrepreneur's decisions are used as context. Comes up in almost every exam series.

## What you need to know

- The concepts of needs, wants, scarcity and opportunity cost.
- The importance of specialisation (and division of labour).
- The purpose of business activity and the concept of adding value; how added value can be increased.
- Primary, secondary and tertiary sectors; reasons for changes in the importance of each sector (e.g. in developed and developing economies).
- Private sector and public sector in a mixed economy.
- Enterprise and entrepreneurship: the characteristics of successful entrepreneurs.
- The contents of a business plan and how it helps entrepreneurs.
- Why and how governments support business start-ups.
- Types of business organisation: sole traders, partnerships, private limited companies, public limited companies, franchises and joint ventures — their main features, advantages and disadvantages.
- Limited and unlimited liability; the concept of risk.
- Public sector corporations (state-owned businesses).
- Recommending and justifying a suitable form of business organisation for a given situation.

## Notes

### Needs, wants, scarcity and opportunity cost

| Concept | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Need | A good or service essential for living | Food, water, shelter, clothing |
| Want | A good or service people would like to have but is not essential for living | Smartphone, holiday |
| Scarcity | Unlimited wants but limited resources (factors of production) to produce goods and services | Not enough land for every housing project |
| Opportunity cost | The next best alternative given up when making a choice | A business spends €20,000 on a new oven, so it cannot spend it on advertising |

Factors of production: **land** (natural resources), **labour** (workers), **capital** (man-made goods used in production, e.g. machines) and **enterprise** (the risk-taking and organising skill of the entrepreneur).

### Specialisation and division of labour

- **Specialisation:** people and businesses concentrate on what they are best at.
- **Division of labour:** production is split into separate tasks, each done by a different worker.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Workers become skilled at one task → higher efficiency/productivity | Repetitive work → boredom, lower motivation |
| Saves time moving between tasks | If one worker is absent, the whole process can stop |
| Easier, cheaper training | Workers have fewer skills, so less flexible |

### Purpose of business activity and added value

Businesses combine factors of production to make goods and services that satisfy needs and wants.

**Added value = selling price − cost of bought-in materials (and components)**

Example: a bakery buys ingredients for €1.20 per cake and sells the cake for €4.00. Added value = €4.00 − €1.20 = €2.80 per cake. Added value is not profit: other costs (wages, rent) still have to be paid from it.

Ways to increase added value: raise the price (e.g. through branding, better packaging, high-quality service, convenience) or reduce the cost of materials (cheaper supplier, less waste). Each has a risk: a higher price may reduce sales; cheaper materials may reduce quality.

### Economic sectors

| Sector | Activity | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Extracting natural resources | Farming, fishing, mining, forestry |
| Secondary | Manufacturing and construction — turning raw materials into products | Car factory, bakery, builder |
| Tertiary | Providing services | Banks, shops, hotels, transport |

- **Industrialisation:** in many developing economies the secondary sector grows as employment moves from farming into manufacturing.
- **De-industrialisation:** in developed economies (e.g. Spain, UK) the secondary sector declines and the tertiary sector grows, because of rising incomes (more spending on services), competition from lower-cost manufacturers abroad and depletion of natural resources.

### Private and public sector — the mixed economy

- **Private sector:** businesses owned by individuals or shareholders, usually aiming for profit.
- **Public sector:** organisations owned and controlled by the government (state), e.g. health care, education, public transport. Aims include providing an essential service, keeping jobs, and serving the public.
- **Mixed economy:** has both sectors.

### Entrepreneurs

An **entrepreneur** is a person who organises, operates and takes the risk for a new business venture.

Characteristics of successful entrepreneurs: hard-working, risk-taker, creative, optimistic, self-confident, innovative, independent, effective communicator.

**Business plan:** a document containing the business objectives and important details about its operations, finance and owners. Typical contents: the business idea and objectives, market research and target market, marketing plan, production/operations, finance (forecasts, sources of finance, cash-flow forecast), the owner's details.
- Helps the entrepreneur think through risks and plan; needed to obtain a bank loan or investment; sets targets to measure progress.
- But it does not guarantee success, forecasts may be wrong, and it takes time to write.

**Why governments support start-ups:** they create jobs, increase competition and choice, increase output (economic growth), may grow into large businesses that export.
**How:** advice and training, grants and low-interest loans, low-cost premises (e.g. business parks/incubators), lower taxes for new firms.

### Types of business organisation

**Unlimited liability:** the owners are personally responsible for all the debts of the business; they can lose personal possessions.
**Limited liability:** the owners (shareholders) can lose only the amount they invested in the business.
A business with limited liability is an **incorporated** business: it has a **separate legal identity** from its owners and **continuity** (it continues if an owner dies or leaves).

| Type | Owners / control | Liability | Main advantages | Main disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sole trader | One owner | Unlimited | Easy and cheap to set up; owner keeps all profit; full control; fast decisions | Long hours; limited finance; no continuity; unlimited liability |
| Partnership | Usually 2–20 partners; partnership agreement (deed) | Unlimited (normally) | More capital and skills than a sole trader; shares workload | Disagreements; profits shared; no continuity; unlimited liability |
| Private limited company (Ltd) | Shareholders; shares cannot be sold to the general public; directors run it | Limited | Limited liability; continuity; can raise capital from friends/family shareholders; owners keep control | Legal formalities to set up; accounts must be filed (less privacy); shares hard to sell |
| Public limited company (plc) | Shareholders; shares sold to the public on the stock exchange | Limited | Can raise very large amounts of capital; limited liability; continuity | Expensive to set up; must publish accounts; risk of takeover; separation of ownership and control (directors may pursue different aims from shareholders) |
| Franchise | Franchisee buys the right to use the franchisor's name, logo and business model | Depends on franchisee's own legal form | Franchisee: well-known brand, lower risk, training and support. Franchisor: fast expansion with less own capital | Franchisee: pays fee and royalties, little freedom. Franchisor: risk to brand image from a bad franchisee |
| Joint venture | Two or more businesses agree to work together on a project, sharing capital, risks and profits | — | Shared costs and risks; combined expertise; access to new markets | Profits shared; disagreements; different cultures/methods |

**Public sector corporation:** a business owned by the government that provides a service (e.g. a state-owned railway or postal service). Aims: provide a service to the public, protect jobs, sometimes cover costs rather than maximise profit. May be less efficient (no profit motive) and depend on government funding.

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Opportunity cost | The next best alternative given up by choosing another item |
| Scarcity | The lack of sufficient products to fulfil the total wants of the population (unlimited wants, limited resources) |
| Specialisation | When people and businesses concentrate on what they are best at |
| Division of labour | When the production process is split up into different tasks and each worker performs one of these tasks |
| Added value | The difference between the selling price of a product and the cost of bought-in materials and components |
| Entrepreneur | A person who organises, operates and takes the risk for a new business venture |
| Business plan | A document containing the business objectives and important details about the operations, finance and owners of the new business |
| Unlimited liability | The owners of a business can be held responsible for all the debts of the business |
| Limited liability | The liability of shareholders for the company's debts is limited to the amount they invested |
| Franchise | A business based on the use of the brand names, promotional logos and trading methods of an existing successful business |
| Joint venture | Two or more businesses agree to work closely together on a particular project and create a separate business division to do so |
| Mixed economy | An economy with both a private sector and a public sector |

## Worked examples

**Case context:** Inés runs *Raíz*, a sole-trader shop in Valencia selling plant-based ready meals. Sales have grown 30% a year for three years. She wants to open a second shop costing €80,000 but has only €15,000 of savings. Her friend Pablo, a chef, has offered to invest €40,000.

**Q1. Define "unlimited liability". [2]**

Model answer: The owner of the business is personally responsible for all its debts (1), so personal possessions such as a house could be sold to pay them (1).

**Q2. Explain two disadvantages to Inés of operating as a sole trader. [6]**

How the marks work: for each disadvantage — knowledge (1), application to Raíz (1), analysis of the consequence (1).

Model answer:
- **Limited capital** (K). Inés has only €15,000 of savings but needs €80,000 for the second shop (App). This means she cannot finance the expansion herself and may lose the chance to grow while demand is rising 30% a year (An).
- **Unlimited liability** (K). If the second shop of plant-based meals fails, debts would fall on Inés personally (App), so she could lose her personal assets, which increases the risk of expanding (An).

**Q3. Should Inés form a partnership with Pablo or a private limited company? Justify your answer. [6]**

How the marks work (typical): knowledge of both options, application to Raíz, analysis of each option, and a justified decision (evaluation).

Model answer:
- **Partnership:** quick and cheap to set up, and Pablo brings €40,000 plus chef skills that suit a ready-meal business (K + App). However, liability stays unlimited, so both partners risk personal assets on an €80,000 expansion, and disagreements over menus could slow decisions (An).
- **Private limited company:** Inés and Pablo would become shareholders with limited liability, so they could only lose the €55,000 they invest (K + App). It also gives continuity and makes it easier to sell shares to other investors later to raise the remaining €25,000 (An). The cost is more legal paperwork and published accounts.
- **Decision (Ev):** I recommend a private limited company. The expansion is large compared with Inés's savings, so reducing personal risk matters most, and it keeps control within the two of them because shares cannot be sold to the public. This depends on Inés being willing to share decisions with Pablo; if she is not, she should look for a bank loan instead.

**Q4. Calculate the added value on one meal that sells for €7.50 and uses ingredients and packaging costing €2.90. [2]**

€7.50 − €2.90 (1) = €4.60 (1).

## Common mistakes

- Confusing **added value** with **profit** — added value ignores wages, rent and other overheads.
- Writing "a plc is owned by the government". A plc is a **private sector** business; public sector corporations are owned by the state.
- Saying a private limited company "can sell shares on the stock exchange" — only a plc can.
- Giving generic advantages ("more capital") without applying them to the business in the data — this loses application marks.
- In "justify" questions, describing both options but never making a clear decision, or making a decision without explaining **why it beats the alternative**.
- Mixing up sectors: a bakery that makes bread is secondary; a supermarket is tertiary.

4. Outline two reasons why the tertiary sector has grown in many developed countries. [4]
5. Explain two benefits to a franchisee of opening a franchise of a well-known coffee brand. [6]
6. *Hogar Ltd* makes kitchen furniture and wants to raise €2 million to build a new factory. Do you think it should become a public limited company? Justify your answer. [6]
