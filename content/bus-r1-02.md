# Objectives, stakeholders and growth

> **In the exam:** Very common in both papers. Paper 1: "Identify two stakeholders" [2], "Outline two objectives" [4], "Explain two ways to measure size" [6], "Do you think the business should grow by takeover? Justify your answer" [6]. Paper 2: case-study questions on conflicting stakeholder objectives, growth options (merger, takeover, internal growth) or expanding abroad are frequent 12-mark evaluation questions.

## What you need to know

- Methods of measuring business size: number of employees, value of output, capital employed. Why profit is not a good measure of size.
- Why the owners of a business may want to expand it; internal growth and external growth.
- Types of external growth: horizontal, vertical (forward and backward) and conglomerate integration (mergers and takeovers).
- Problems linked to business growth and how they might be overcome.
- Why some businesses remain small.
- Why some businesses fail (internal and external causes).
- Business objectives: survival, growth, profit, market share; objectives of social enterprises (social, environmental, financial — the "triple bottom line").
- Why objectives change over time.
- Main internal and external stakeholder groups and their objectives; how stakeholder objectives can conflict.
- Differences in objectives between private sector and public sector enterprises.
- Multinational companies: benefits and drawbacks to the host country and to the business (part of business and the international economy).

## Notes

### Measuring business size

| Method | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Number of employees | Easy to find and compare | A capital-intensive firm can be large with few workers |
| Value of output (or revenue) | Shows how much the business sells | Value-of-output comparisons are hard across industries with very different prices |
| Capital employed | Total long-term finance invested in the business | A labour-intensive business may need little capital but be large |
| Market share | Shows size relative to the market | A small market makes a large share misleading |

**Profit is not a measure of size**: a small business can make high profit and a large one can make a loss. Use more than one measure.

Market share (%) = business sales ÷ total market sales × 100.
Example: sales €45 million in a market of €300 million → 45 ÷ 300 × 100 = 15%.

### Why and how businesses grow

**Why owners want growth:** more profit, higher market share, economies of scale (lower average costs), more status and power, lower risk through diversification.

**Internal (organic) growth:** expanding the existing business — opening new branches, new products, new markets. Slower, but less risky and the business keeps control and its culture.

**External growth:** integrating with another business.
- **Merger:** two businesses agree to join and form one business.
- **Takeover (acquisition):** one business buys a controlling share of another.

| Type of integration | Meaning | Example | Main benefits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Horizontal | Same industry, same stage of production | Two hotel chains | Removes a competitor; larger market share; economies of scale |
| Vertical backward | With a supplier (earlier stage) | Chocolate maker buys a cocoa farm | Secure supplies; control of quality and cost of materials |
| Vertical forward | With a customer / later stage | Brewery buys a chain of bars | Guaranteed outlet; profit margin of retailer gained; control of how product is sold |
| Conglomerate | Businesses in unrelated industries | Clothing firm buys a travel agency | Diversification spreads risk |

### Problems of growth and solutions

| Problem | Solution |
|---|---|
| Difficult to control and coordinate many staff and branches | Decentralise; delegate to branch managers; clear organisation structure |
| Poor communication | Better IT systems, regular meetings, fewer levels of hierarchy |
| Lack of finance for expansion | Choose suitable source (retained profit, loans, share issue) |
| Integration problems after merger: different cultures, job losses, low morale | Communicate plans early, consult staff, train |
| Diseconomies of scale → rising average costs | Keep units small; manage well |

### Why some businesses remain small

Type of industry (personal services such as hairdressers); small market size (niche or local market); owners' objectives (want control, less stress); lack of finance.

### Why businesses fail

| Internal causes | External causes |
|---|---|
| Lack of management skills / experience | Changes in the business environment (e.g. recession, higher interest rates) |
| Poor planning — no business plan, poor cash-flow management | New competitors, new technology making products obsolete |
| Over-expansion too quickly | Changes in consumer tastes |
| Poor location or poor marketing | New laws or taxes; rising costs of supplies |

New businesses are especially at risk: no track record, few customers, little capital, owner inexperience. **Lack of cash** (not just lack of profit) is a common reason for failure.

### Business objectives

- **Survival** — main aim of new businesses and in recessions.
- **Growth** — expanding size.
- **Profit** — reward for owners; source of finance.
- **Market share** — higher share gives status and bargaining power with retailers.
- **Social enterprises** have social and environmental objectives as well as financial ones: the **triple bottom line** (social, environmental, financial). Profits are reinvested to help their social aim (e.g. a café that employs and trains homeless people).

Objectives change over time: a new business aims to survive; once established it aims for growth and profit; in a recession it may return to survival; a public pressure campaign may add environmental objectives.

Why set objectives: give direction, a target to measure success, help decision-making, motivate staff.

### Stakeholders

A **stakeholder** is any person or group with a direct interest in the performance and activities of a business.

| Internal | Main objectives | External | Main objectives |
|---|---|---|---|
| Owners / shareholders | Profit, dividends, rising share value | Customers | Good quality at fair prices, safe products |
| Managers | High salary, job security, status | Suppliers | Regular orders, prompt payment |
| Employees (workers) | Fair pay, job security, good conditions | Government | Jobs, taxes, obeying laws, economic growth |
| | | Banks / lenders | Loan repaid with interest |
| | | Local community | Jobs, low pollution, safe environment |

**Conflicts:** owners want higher profit → may cut wages or move production abroad, which conflicts with workers' job security; expanding a factory creates jobs (government, community happy) but more noise and traffic (local residents unhappy); low prices for customers vs. profit for owners.

**Public sector objectives:** provide a public service, keep costs within budget, protect employment, meet quality targets — rather than maximise profit.

### Multinationals

A **multinational company (MNC)** has factories, production or service operations in more than one country.

**Why become a multinational:** be closer to new markets, lower costs (labour, rent), access raw materials, avoid trade barriers (tariffs, quotas), spread risk.

| Host country benefits | Host country drawbacks |
|---|---|
| Jobs and training for local workers | Profits sent back to home country |
| Tax revenue for the government | Local firms may be forced out of business |
| Investment, new technology and skills | Use of scarce natural resources; pollution |
| Exports improve balance of trade; more choice | Could leave suddenly; may influence government |

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Capital employed | The total value of all long-term finance invested in the business |
| Internal growth | When a business expands its existing operations |
| External growth | When a business takes over or merges with another business |
| Merger | When the owners of two businesses agree to join their businesses together to make one business |
| Takeover | When one business buys out the owners of another business, which then becomes part of the "predator" business |
| Horizontal integration | When one business merges with or takes over another one in the same industry at the same stage of production |
| Vertical integration | When one business merges with or takes over another one in the same industry but at a different stage of production (backward or forward) |
| Conglomerate integration | When one business merges with or takes over a business in a completely different industry |
| Stakeholder | Any person or group with a direct interest in the performance and activities of a business |
| Social enterprise | A business with social objectives that reinvests its profits to achieve them |
| Multinational company | A business with factories, production or service operations in more than one country |

## Worked examples

**Case context:** *VeloMar* is a private limited company in Barcelona that makes electric bicycles. It employs 120 people and has 8% of the Spanish e-bike market. The directors are considering two growth options: (A) taking over *CellPro*, a battery supplier; (B) opening its own shops in five cities.

**Q1. Identify two ways of measuring the size of VeloMar. [2]**

Number of employees (1); market share / value of output / capital employed (1).

**Q2. Explain one benefit and one drawback to VeloMar of taking over CellPro. [6]**

- Benefit: backward vertical integration secures supply (K). VeloMar would control the supply of batteries, the most important component of an e-bike (App), so production would not stop if batteries were in short supply and it could control their quality and cost, raising its profit margin (An).
- Drawback: a takeover is very expensive and may cause integration problems (K). CellPro's staff may have a different culture from VeloMar's 120 employees (App), so there may be low morale and poor communication, raising costs and reducing the expected benefits (An).

**Q3. Consider whether VeloMar should choose option A or option B. Justify your answer. [6]** *(typical: up to 2 K, 2 App/An, 2 Ev)*

- Option A secures battery supplies and quality; it gains the supplier's profit margin, but needs a large amount of finance and VeloMar has no experience running a battery factory.
- Option B is forward vertical integration through internal growth: VeloMar gains the retailer's profit margin and controls how e-bikes are displayed, and can expand gradually; however, it needs shop staff and good locations in five cities, and may anger existing retailers who stock its bikes, reducing sales through them.
- **Decision:** Option B is better, because with only 8% market share VeloMar's main problem is reaching more customers, not supply; own shops raise sales and brand awareness and can be opened one at a time, which is less risky than one large takeover. However, if battery shortages are already stopping production, option A should come first.

## Common mistakes

- Listing **profit** as a way to measure size.
- Confusing **merger** (agreement to join) with **takeover** (one buys the other).
- Calling a supplier takeover "forward" integration — buying a supplier is **backward**.
- Writing about stakeholders generally instead of naming the specific conflict in the case (e.g. "local residents want less traffic from the new warehouse").
- Assuming all businesses want profit — social enterprises and public sector organisations have other objectives.
- Listing multinational benefits without saying **to whom** (host country, the business itself, the home country).

## Practice questions

1. Define "stakeholder". [2]
2. A business has sales of €6 million in a market worth €48 million. Calculate its market share. [2]
3. Identify two reasons why businesses fail. [2]
4. Outline one advantage of internal growth and one advantage of external growth. [4]
5. Explain two possible conflicts between stakeholders when a supermarket chain plans to open a large new store on the edge of a small town. [6]
6. A car manufacturer is considering building a factory in another country. Do you think the benefits to the host country are greater than the drawbacks? Justify your answer. [6]

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Any person or group (1) with a direct interest in the performance and activities of a business (1).
2. 6 ÷ 48 × 100 (1) = 12.5% (1).
3. Any two: poor management, lack of finance / poor cash flow, over-expansion, competition, recession, changes in consumer tastes, poor location.
4. Internal: less risky / keeps control and culture / can be financed gradually (2). External: very fast growth / instant market share / removes a competitor / gains supplier or outlet (2).
5. e.g. Owners want profit from the new store vs. local small shops (community) losing customers and closing (K+App+An). Customers welcome lower prices and choice vs. residents worried about traffic and noise near homes (K+App+An). Also accept workers vs. owners over wages/hours.
