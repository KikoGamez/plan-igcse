# Marketing and market research

> **In the exam:** Appears in nearly every series. Paper 1: "Define market segment" [2], "Identify two methods of primary research" [2], "Outline two benefits of secondary research" [4], calculations of market share or market growth [2], "Explain two ways the business could segment its market" [6], "Should the business use primary or secondary research? Justify your answer" [6]. In Paper 2 the case often includes survey results or market data in an appendix that you must interpret and use.

## What you need to know

- The role of marketing: identifying and satisfying customer needs, maintaining customer loyalty, building customer relationships, gaining information about customers.
- Why customer (spending) patterns change; why some markets have become more competitive; how businesses respond.
- Niche marketing and mass marketing: benefits and limitations.
- How and why markets are segmented (by income, age, gender, location, lifestyle/use…); identifying a target segment and its potential.
- Market-orientated and product-orientated businesses.
- The role of market research and methods used: primary research (questionnaires, interviews, focus groups, observation, test marketing) and secondary research (internal and external sources).
- Sampling: why samples are used; random and quota samples.
- Factors influencing the accuracy of market research data.
- Presenting and analysing data: tables, charts, graphs; interpreting results.

## Notes

### The role of marketing

Marketing aims to:
- identify customer needs (via market research);
- satisfy customer needs (right product, right price, right place, right promotion);
- maintain customer loyalty (repeat purchases, loyalty schemes);
- build customer relationships (understand customers to keep them);
- gain information about customers (to anticipate changes).

**Why spending patterns change:** changes in income, tastes and fashion, population and age structure, technology (e.g. online shopping), and concern for the environment.

**Why markets have become more competitive:** globalisation (foreign firms enter), online selling (customers compare prices easily), better transport.
**Business responses:** keep improving products, cut costs to keep prices competitive, keep researching the market, build brand loyalty through excellent service.

### Mass and niche marketing

| | Mass marketing | Niche marketing |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Selling the same product to the whole market, no attempt to target groups | Identifying and serving a small, specific part of the market |
| Advantages | Large sales; economies of scale → lower average costs; risk spread over a large market | Less competition; can charge higher prices; closer to customer needs; suits small firms |
| Disadvantages | High competition; often low prices; high promotion costs | Small sales volume; risky if tastes change; may attract larger competitors |

### Market segmentation

A **market segment** is an identifiable sub-group of a whole market in which consumers have similar characteristics or preferences.

| Way of segmenting | Example |
|---|---|
| Income / socio-economic group | Luxury hotels vs budget hostels |
| Age | Toys for under-5s; holidays for over-60s |
| Gender | Grooming products |
| Location (region, urban/rural, climate) | Heating products for colder regions |
| Lifestyle / use of product | Vegan foods; sports drinks for athletes |

**Benefits:** products and promotion match each segment's needs → higher sales; less wasted advertising; can identify new gaps in the market. **Limitations:** different products for each segment raise costs; research needed.

### Market orientation vs product orientation

- **Market-orientated:** carries out market research to find out what consumers want, then develops products to meet these needs (most businesses).
- **Product-orientated:** produces products it believes are good or that it is able to make, then tries to sell them (e.g. basic necessities, or innovative technology where consumers cannot yet imagine the product).

### Market research

**Market research** is the process of gathering, analysing and interpreting information about a market.

### Primary research (field research)

The collection of original data — first-hand, for the business's own purpose.

| Method | Description | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Questionnaire (survey) | Set of questions answered in person, by phone, by post or online | Large amount of data; can be analysed statistically | Poorly worded or leading questions; low response rate online |
| Interview | One-to-one discussion with set questions | Detailed answers; can explain questions | Time-consuming, costly; interviewer bias |
| Focus group | Small group discusses a product, led by a researcher | Detailed opinions and reactions; ideas | Expensive; group members influence each other; small number |
| Observation | Watching / counting (e.g. counting people passing a shop) | Cheap; real behaviour | Shows what people do, not why |
| Test marketing | Selling a product in a small area before national launch | Real sales data; reduces risk of failure | Competitors see the product; area may not represent whole market |

**Primary overall:** up to date, specific to the business's needs, not available to competitors — but expensive and time-consuming.

### Secondary research (desk research)

The use of information that has already been collected by someone else for another purpose.

- **Internal sources:** sales records, customer records (e.g. loyalty card data), finance department reports, sales staff reports.
- **External sources:** government statistics (population, income), newspapers and trade magazines, market research agency reports, competitors' websites and published accounts, the internet.

**Secondary overall:** cheaper and quicker; gives overall market data (size, trends) — but may be out of date, may not be exactly what the business needs, available to competitors, may be inaccurate or biased.

### Sampling

A **sample** is a group of people who are selected to respond to a market research survey — asking the whole population would be too expensive and slow.
- **Random sample:** every member of the population has an equal chance of being selected.
- **Quota sample:** people are selected on the basis of certain characteristics (e.g. age, gender) in set numbers, to match the target market.

### Accuracy of market research

Data may be inaccurate because of: small or unrepresentative sample; poorly worded or leading questions; bias (respondents giving "polite" answers; interviewer bias); out-of-date secondary data; source bias (e.g. a competitor's claims). Results show what people **say** they will do — not always what they actually do.

### Presenting and using data

Tables, bar charts, pie charts, line graphs. You may need to calculate:
- **Percentage:** 240 of 400 people said "yes" → 240 ÷ 400 × 100 = 60%.
- **Percentage change (market growth):** market sales rose from €2.0 m to €2.3 m → (2.3 − 2.0) ÷ 2.0 × 100 = 15% growth.
- **Market share:** firm's sales ÷ total market sales × 100.

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Market segment | An identifiable sub-group of a whole market in which consumers have similar characteristics or preferences |
| Mass market | Where there is a very large number of sales of a product, with no targeting of particular groups |
| Niche market | A small, usually specialised, segment of a much larger market |
| Market-orientated business | A business that carries out market research to find out consumer wants before developing a product |
| Product-orientated business | A business that produces products it is good at making, then tries to find a market for them |
| Market research | The process of gathering, analysing and interpreting information about a market |
| Primary research | The collection of original data (first-hand), e.g. by questionnaire, interview, observation |
| Secondary research | The use of information that has already been collected and is available for use by others |
| Sample | The group of people selected to respond to a market research survey |
| Random sample | When people are selected at random, so every member of the population has an equal chance of being selected |
| Quota sample | When people are selected on the basis of certain characteristics (e.g. age, gender) |
| Customer loyalty | When existing customers continue to buy products from the same business |

## Worked examples

**Case context:** *Bruma* is a small business in Madrid that plans to launch a range of sugar-free energy drinks for students aged 16–24. The owner has a budget of €3,000 for market research. She found a government report showing that 18% of 16–24-year-olds in Spain drink energy drinks at least once a week. She then asked 400 students outside two universities; 236 said they would try a sugar-free energy drink.

**Q1. Calculate the percentage of students surveyed who said they would try the drink. [2]**

236 ÷ 400 × 100 (1) = 59% (1).

**Q2. Explain two ways Bruma could segment the market for energy drinks. [6]**

- By age (K): Bruma targets 16–24-year-olds (App), so it can design flavours, packaging and social-media promotion that appeal to students, making its limited promotion budget more effective (An).
- By lifestyle (K): health-conscious young people who want to avoid sugar (App), so Bruma can position the product as "healthy energy" and avoid direct price competition with big brands, possibly charging a higher price (An).

**Q3. Do you think Bruma's market research results are reliable enough to decide whether to launch the drink? Justify your answer. [6]**

- Reliable aspects: the government report is from an official source and gives the size of the target segment (App/K); 400 is a reasonably large sample and the questionnaire was primary data aimed at the target market (An).
- Problems: the sample was taken outside only two universities in Madrid, so it may not represent all 16–24-year-olds in Spain (e.g. people not studying, other regions) (An). Saying they "would try" the drink does not mean they would buy it regularly or at what price (An).
- **Judgement (Ev):** The results are useful but not reliable enough on their own. 59% interest is encouraging, but Bruma should spend part of its €3,000 on a small test market — e.g. selling in a few university cafés for a month — because real sales data shows repeat purchases at a real price, which reduces the risk of a costly failed launch.

## Common mistakes

- Defining primary research as "research done first" — it is **original data collected first-hand** for the business's purpose.
- Saying secondary research is "always inaccurate" — explain *why* (out of date, collected for a different purpose).
- Confusing **random** and **quota** samples.
- Calculation errors in percentage change: divide by the **original** value.
- Answering "how could the business segment the market" with marketing-mix ideas (price, promotion) instead of segmentation bases (age, income, location, lifestyle).
- Not using the figures from the data in the answer — examiners reward quoting and interpreting the numbers.

## Practice questions

1. Define "niche market". [2]
2. Identify two sources of secondary research. [2]
3. A survey of 250 people found 85 preferred Brand X. Calculate the percentage who preferred Brand X. [2]
4. Outline two reasons why a business might use a quota sample rather than a random sample. [4]
5. Explain two benefits to a small hotel of focusing on a niche market. [6]
6. A sports-clothing business wants to launch a new running shoe. Should it use focus groups or a questionnaire? Justify your answer. [6]
7. Total market sales of e-scooters in a city fell from €5.0 m to €4.2 m. Calculate the percentage change. [2]

1. A small, usually specialised, segment (1) of a much larger market (1).
2. Any two: government statistics, newspapers/magazines, market research agency reports, internet, competitors' accounts/websites, internal sales records.
3. 85 ÷ 250 × 100 (1) = 34% (1).
4. Ensures the sample matches the target market (e.g. correct mix of ages) (2); cheaper/quicker than a random sample, which needs a full list of the population (2).
5. e.g. Less competition (K): e.g. a hotel specialising in cycling holidays (App) competes with fewer hotels, so it can charge higher prices and earn a higher profit margin (An). Better meets customer needs (K): bike storage, early breakfasts (App), creating loyalty and repeat bookings (An).
