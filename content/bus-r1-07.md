## Notes

### Production and productivity

- **Production:** making goods or providing services that satisfy customer needs, using factors of production.
- **Productivity:** a measure of the efficiency of inputs used in production — usually labour productivity.

**Labour productivity = output in a time period ÷ number of employees**

Example: 40 workers produce 4,800 units a week → 4,800 ÷ 40 = 120 units per worker per week. After training, 40 workers produce 5,400 → 135 units per worker, an increase of (135 − 120) ÷ 120 × 100 = 12.5%.

Higher productivity → lower average (unit) costs → lower prices or higher profit margin → more competitive.

Ways to increase productivity: training, better motivation, automation and new technology, improved quality control (less waste), lean production, better management of inventory.

### Inventory management

Businesses hold inventories of raw materials, work in progress and finished goods so they can produce and meet customer orders.

| Holding too much inventory | Holding too little inventory |
|---|---|
| High storage and insurance costs | Production may stop (run out of materials) |
| Cash tied up (opportunity cost) | Customers' orders not met → lost sales and reputation |
| Risk of waste, damage, theft, going out of date | Cannot benefit from bulk-buying discounts |

**Inventory graph** (saw-tooth shape):
- **Maximum level** — highest level of inventory held.
- **Re-order level** — when inventory falls to this level, a new order is placed.
- **Lead time** — time between placing an order and receiving it.
- **Buffer inventory** — minimum level kept for emergencies (unexpected demand or late delivery).

Example: maximum 1,000 units, buffer 200 units, usage 100 units per day, lead time 2 days. Re-order level = 200 + (100 × 2) = 400 units. Order quantity = 1,000 − 200 = 800 units, so inventory returns to the maximum when the order arrives at the buffer level.

### Lean production

**Lean production** — techniques used to reduce waste and increase efficiency (waste of time, materials, movement, inventory, faulty products).

| Method | Meaning | Benefit |
|---|---|---|
| Kaizen | Continuous improvement through small, frequent changes suggested by workers (often in teams) | Increased productivity; less waste; workers involved and motivated |
| Just-in-time (JIT) | Little or no inventory held; materials arrive exactly when needed and finished goods go straight to customers | Saves storage costs; less cash tied up; less waste — but needs very reliable suppliers; risk of production stopping |
| Cell production | Production line divided into separate units (cells), each making a complete part, run by a team | Teamworking improves motivation; workers multi-skilled; quality responsibility |

Benefits of lean production: less waste, lower costs, faster production, less space needed, higher quality, better motivation.

### Methods of production

| | Job production | Batch production | Flow production |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Products made one at a time, usually to a specific order | Quantities of identical products made together; machines changed between batches | Large quantities of identical products made in a continuous process (production line) |
| Examples | Wedding dress, custom house, bridge, hand-made furniture | Bread, clothing sizes, furniture ranges | Cars, soft drinks, mobile phones |
| Advantages | Meets customer's exact needs; high quality; workers motivated by varied skilled work | Some variety; flexibility; some economies of scale; less risky than flow | Low unit cost (economies of scale); high output; automated; little training needed |
| Disadvantages | High unit cost; slow; skilled labour needed | Machines idle while switching batches; inventory of each batch needs storing | Very high set-up cost; boring, repetitive work; one breakdown stops the whole line; no variety |

**Choosing a method:** depends on the nature of the product (unique vs standard), size of the market (small/local vs mass), and the amount of capital available.

### Technology in production

- **Automation / robots:** machines do the work with little human input → faster, consistent quality, can work 24 hours, lower long-run costs; but high initial cost, job losses, maintenance, low worker morale.
- **CAD (computer-aided design):** designs on a computer — fast, accurate, easy to change.
- **CAM (computer-aided manufacturing):** computers control machines on the production line.
- **Services:** EPOS (electronic point of sale), contactless payment, online shopping, automated stock re-ordering.

### Quality

**Quality** = producing a good or service that meets customer expectations (free from faults, fit for purpose).

Why it matters: builds brand image and loyalty; customers pay higher prices; fewer complaints, returns and repairs; less waste; legal requirements for safety.

| Approach | How it works | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quality control | Checking finished products (or samples) by inspectors at the end of production | Aims to stop faulty products reaching customers; not much staff training needed | Expensive (inspectors); finds faults after they are made, so waste; workers not responsible for quality |
| Quality assurance | Checking quality throughout the production process — standards are agreed and checked at every stage | Faults found earlier → less waste; customers trust a guarantee of quality | Relies on employees following standards; training costs |
| Total quality management (TQM) | Every employee aims to ensure customer satisfaction; quality is part of the culture; "customers" include the next worker in the process | Quality built into every step; no faults; less waste and lower costs | Needs commitment and training of all staff; takes time |

Customers can be assured of quality through quality marks/certificates, guarantees, reviews and brand reputation.

### Location decisions

**Manufacturing business — factors:**
- Near **raw materials** if they are bulky/heavy (bulk-reducing industry, e.g. steel).
- Near the **market** if the product gains weight or is fragile/perishable (e.g. soft drinks, bread).
- Availability and cost of **labour** with the right skills.
- **Transport** links and infrastructure (motorways, ports).
- Cost and availability of **land**, **power** and water.
- **Government** grants and incentives, laws.
- **External economies** — being near related businesses and suppliers.

**Service business (retail, hotels, banks) — factors:** customers (footfall, near the market), personal visits needed or not, competitors nearby, technology (online services can be anywhere), rent costs, availability of labour, security and parking.

**Choosing a country:** labour and other costs, new markets, avoiding trade barriers (tariffs), government incentives, political stability, exchange rates, language and culture, infrastructure, availability of raw materials.

**Legal controls on location:** planning permission / zoning laws (keeping factories away from homes), environmental laws, incentives for areas of high unemployment, restrictions on foreign ownership in some countries.

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Production | The effective management of resources in producing goods and services |
| Productivity | A measure of the efficiency of inputs used in the production process over a period of time |
| Labour productivity | Output per worker in a period of time |
| Buffer inventory | The minimum inventory level that should be held to ensure that production can still take place if a delay in delivery or a sudden rise in demand happens |
| Lead time | The time taken between placing an order and receiving the delivery |
| Lean production | Techniques used to reduce waste and therefore increase efficiency |
| Kaizen | Continuous improvement, usually through small changes suggested by workers |
| Just-in-time | A method that eliminates the need to hold inventories: materials arrive just as they are needed |
| Job production | When a single product is made at a time, usually to a customer's specification |
| Batch production | When quantities of a product are made, a batch at a time, before production switches to a different product |
| Flow production | When large quantities of a product are produced in a continuous process |
| Quality control | Checking for quality at the end of the production process |
| Quality assurance | Checking for quality standards throughout the production process |
| Total quality management (TQM) | The continuous improvement of products and processes by focusing on quality at each stage of production, involving all employees |

## Worked examples

**Case context:** *Cerámica Sol* makes hand-painted tiles to order for hotels and homes using job production. It employs 12 skilled painters who produced 3,000 tiles last month. A large DIY chain has offered a contract for 50,000 identical tiles a year. Cerámica Sol could buy an automated printing line (€250,000) and switch part of its production to batch or flow production.

**Q1. Calculate the labour productivity of Cerámica Sol last month. [2]**

3,000 ÷ 12 (1) = 250 tiles per worker per month (1).

**Q2. Explain one advantage and one disadvantage to Cerámica Sol of using automated production for the DIY contract. [6]**

- Advantage: lower unit costs (K). The machine can print 50,000 identical tiles faster than the 12 painters (App), so average costs fall and Cerámica Sol can offer the low price a DIY chain wants while still making a profit (An).
- Disadvantage: high capital cost and risk (K). The printing line costs €250,000 (App); if the DIY chain cancels the contract the machine may stand idle, and the business would struggle to repay any loan used to buy it (An).

**Q3. Should Cerámica Sol use quality control or total quality management (TQM) for the new contract? Justify your answer. [6]**

- Quality control: inspectors check finished tiles (K); simple to introduce for one large order (App), but faulty tiles are found only at the end, so many could be wasted in a 50,000-tile batch before the problem is seen (An).
- TQM: every worker is responsible for quality at every stage (K); fits a workforce of skilled painters who already take pride in their work (App); problems are prevented rather than detected, reducing waste and protecting the relationship with the DIY chain (An). It needs training and time.
- **Decision (Ev):** TQM, because with an automated line producing large quantities, one fault early in the process could ruin thousands of tiles, and losing a large customer would be very costly. Cerámica Sol is small, so training 12 staff is affordable. In the short term it should still sample-check finished tiles until TQM is fully working.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing **production** (total output) with **productivity** (output per worker/input).
- Saying JIT means "no inventory at all, so no risk" — it needs reliable suppliers; any delay stops production.
- Confusing **quality control** (checking at the end) with **quality assurance** (checking throughout).
- Choosing flow production for a small business with a niche, customised product — method must fit the product and market size.
- Location answers that list factors without using the data given (e.g. wage costs, distance to market) or without a final decision.
- Drawing the inventory graph with the re-order level below the buffer level.

7. Usage is 50 units per day, lead time is 4 days and buffer inventory is 150 units. Calculate the re-order level. [2]
