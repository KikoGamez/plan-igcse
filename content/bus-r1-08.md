# Costs and break-even

> **In the exam:** Tested in almost every series. Paper 1: "Define fixed costs" [2], "Calculate the break-even level of output" [2], "Identify two economies of scale" [2], "Explain two limitations of break-even analysis" [6], "Should the business raise its price? Justify your answer" (using break-even) [6]. Paper 2 appendices often contain cost and price data: you calculate break-even or profit and then use the result to evaluate a decision. Calculators are allowed — always show your working.

## What you need to know

- Classification of costs: fixed, variable, total and average costs; using cost data to help make decisions.
- Economies of scale: purchasing, marketing, financial, managerial, technical.
- Diseconomies of scale: poor communication, lack of commitment from employees, weak coordination.
- Break-even: the concept of break-even; break-even charts (drawing and interpreting); the break-even level of output; margin of safety.
- Calculating break-even output using the formula; using break-even analysis to make simple decisions, e.g. the effect of changes in price or costs.
- Limitations of break-even analysis.

## Notes

### Types of cost

| Cost | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed costs (FC) | Costs that do not change with output in the short run; paid even if output is zero | Rent, insurance, salaries of managers, loan interest, advertising |
| Variable costs (VC) | Costs that change directly with the level of output | Raw materials, packaging, piece-rate wages, energy used by machines |
| Total costs (TC) | Fixed costs + variable costs | |
| Average cost (unit cost) | Total cost ÷ output | |

Formulae:
- Total variable cost = variable cost per unit × output
- **Total cost = fixed costs + total variable costs**
- **Average cost per unit = total cost ÷ output**
- **Total revenue = selling price × quantity sold**
- **Profit = total revenue − total costs**

As output rises, fixed costs are spread over more units, so average cost falls.

**Using cost data:** setting prices (price must cover average cost to make a profit), deciding whether to accept an order, comparing locations or methods, deciding whether to stop making a product.

### Economies of scale

**Economies of scale** — factors that lead to a reduction in average costs as a business increases in size.

| Type | How it lowers average cost |
|---|---|
| Purchasing | Buying in bulk → discounts from suppliers |
| Marketing | Advertising cost spread over more units; own vehicles for distribution |
| Financial | Banks see large firms as less risky → lower interest rates, easier to get loans |
| Managerial | Can employ specialist managers (e.g. accountant, marketing manager) → more efficient |
| Technical | Can afford advanced machinery and flow production → higher productivity |

### Diseconomies of scale

**Diseconomies of scale** — factors that lead to an increase in average costs as a business grows beyond a certain size.
- **Poor communication:** many levels of hierarchy, slow and distorted messages.
- **Lack of commitment from employees:** workers feel unimportant and not valued → low motivation, low productivity.
- **Weak coordination:** hard for senior managers to make sure all departments and branches work towards the same objectives.

### Break-even

**Break-even level of output** — the quantity that must be produced/sold for total revenue to equal total costs (no profit, no loss).

**Contribution per unit = selling price − variable cost per unit**
(Each unit sold "contributes" this amount towards paying fixed costs, then to profit.)

**Break-even output = total fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit**

If the answer is not a whole number, **round up** — you cannot break even until you sell the next whole unit.

**Margin of safety = actual (or planned) output − break-even output**
It shows how far sales can fall before the business makes a loss.

**Profit (quick method) = margin of safety × contribution per unit** (works because all fixed costs are already covered at break-even).

### Break-even charts

How to draw:
1. x-axis: output (units); y-axis: costs and revenue (€). Title and labels.
2. **Fixed cost line**: horizontal line at the FC value.
3. **Total cost line**: starts at the FC value on the y-axis (at zero output) and rises with variable costs.
4. **Total revenue line**: starts at the origin (0, 0) and rises with price.
5. **Break-even point**: where total revenue and total cost lines cross. Read down to the x-axis for break-even output.
6. Area to the right between TR and TC = **profit**; to the left = **loss**. Mark the margin of safety as the distance between break-even output and actual output.

Plot each line using two points (e.g. zero and maximum output) and check with a third.

**Effects of changes** (what happens to break-even output):

| Change | Effect on break-even output | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Price rises | Falls | Contribution per unit increases |
| Variable cost per unit rises | Rises | Contribution per unit falls |
| Fixed costs rise | Rises | More contribution needed to cover them |

But a price rise may reduce the number of units sold (especially if demand is price-elastic), so the margin of safety could fall even though break-even falls.

### Uses and limitations of break-even analysis

**Uses:** shows how many units must be sold to avoid a loss; shows profit or loss at any output; shows the margin of safety; tests "what if" changes (price, costs); supports a business plan or loan application.

**Limitations:**
- Assumes all output produced is sold (no inventory left over).
- Assumes the selling price is the same at all levels of output (in reality, discounts for bulk orders).
- Assumes costs are linear — in reality, bulk discounts reduce variable costs per unit; fixed costs may rise in steps at higher output (e.g. a new factory).
- Assumes fixed costs stay fixed.
- Only as accurate as the data/forecasts used.
- Focuses only on costs and revenue — does not consider other factors (e.g. quality, competitors' actions).

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Fixed costs | Costs that do not vary with the number of items sold or produced in the short run |
| Variable costs | Costs that vary directly with the number of items sold or produced |
| Total costs | Fixed costs plus variable costs |
| Average cost per unit | Total cost of production divided by total output |
| Economies of scale | The factors that lead to a reduction in average costs as a business increases in size |
| Diseconomies of scale | The factors that lead to an increase in average costs as a business grows beyond a certain size |
| Break-even level of output | The quantity that must be produced/sold for total revenue to equal total costs |
| Break-even chart | A graph showing total revenue and total costs (and fixed costs) at different levels of output, used to identify the break-even point |
| Margin of safety | The amount by which sales exceed the break-even point |
| Contribution per unit | Selling price minus variable cost per unit |

## Worked examples

**Case context:** *Café Rodante* sells coffee from a mobile cart in a business park. Fixed costs are €4,800 per month (van lease, insurance, licence, the owner's salary). Each coffee sells for €3.50 and has variable costs of €1.10 (beans, milk, cup). Café Rodante sells about 2,600 coffees per month.

**Q1. Calculate the break-even level of output per month. [2]**

Contribution = €3.50 − €1.10 = €2.40. Break-even = €4,800 ÷ €2.40 (1) = 2,000 coffees (1).

**Q2. Calculate the margin of safety and the monthly profit. [3]**

Margin of safety = 2,600 − 2,000 = 600 coffees (1).
Profit = 600 × €2.40 = €1,440 (1).
Check: revenue 2,600 × €3.50 = €9,100; total costs €4,800 + (2,600 × €1.10) = €4,800 + €2,860 = €7,660; profit = €9,100 − €7,660 = €1,440 (1).

**Q3. The owner is considering raising the price to €4.00. Do you think she should? Justify your answer. [6]**

- New contribution = €4.00 − €1.10 = €2.90. New break-even = €4,800 ÷ €2.90 = 1,655.2 → **1,656 coffees** (K/App, using the data).
- Benefit: break-even falls by 344 coffees, so the cart needs fewer sales to cover costs; if sales stayed at 2,600 the margin of safety would rise to 944 coffees and profit would rise to (2,600 × €2.90) − €4,800 = €2,740, compared with €1,440 now (An).
- Risk: customers in a business park can buy coffee elsewhere (office machines, nearby cafés), so demand may be price-elastic. If sales fell below 1,656 she would make a loss (An).
- **Decision (Ev):** She should raise the price only if she can keep most of her customers — for example by adding value with better beans or a loyalty card. Sales could fall by over a third (from 2,600 to 1,656) before she made a loss, so the risk is moderate, but she should test the price for a month and compare sales before deciding permanently.

(Check: revenue 2,600 × €4.00 = €10,400; total costs €4,800 + €2,860 = €7,660; profit = €2,740. Note that the quick method "margin of safety × contribution" gives 944 × €2.90 = €2,737.60 here only because break-even was rounded up — when break-even is not a whole number, calculate profit as revenue − total costs.)

**Q4. Explain two limitations of break-even analysis for Café Rodante. [4]**

- Assumes all coffees are sold at the same price (1) — but Café Rodante may give discounts with a loyalty card, so revenue is lower than shown (1).
- Assumes costs stay constant (1) — but the price of coffee beans and milk may change, so variable cost per unit and the break-even point change (1).

## Common mistakes

- Dividing fixed costs by the **selling price** instead of by **contribution**.
- Rounding break-even down — always round **up** to the next whole unit.
- Drawing the total cost line from the origin — it starts at the **fixed cost** value.
- Forgetting to label axes and lines or to give the chart a title (chart marks are lost).
- Giving the margin of safety in € when the question asks for units (or forgetting units altogether).
- Listing economies of scale in general terms without saying **how average cost falls**.
- Treating break-even as a forecast of what will happen: it only shows what *must* happen.
