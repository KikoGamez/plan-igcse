# Finance and cash flow

> **In the exam:** Very frequent in both papers. Paper 1: "Define overdraft" [2], completing missing figures in a cash-flow forecast [2–4], "Explain two ways to improve cash flow" [6], "Should the business use a bank loan or a share issue? Justify your answer" [6]. Paper 2 almost always contains a cash-flow forecast or finance options in the appendices, and a 12-mark question asking you to recommend a source of finance or a way to solve a cash-flow problem.

## What you need to know

- Why businesses need finance: start-up capital, capital expenditure, revenue expenditure; the need for short-term and long-term finance.
- Sources of finance: internal (retained profit, sale of existing assets, owner's savings) and external (issue of shares, bank loans, overdrafts, trade credit, debt factoring, hire purchase, leasing, government grants, microfinance, crowdfunding).
- Short-term and long-term sources and their uses.
- Main factors in choosing a source of finance: purpose and time period, amount needed, legal form and size of business, control, risk and cost, existing debt.
- What lenders consider before lending (e.g. business plan, cash-flow forecast, security).
- Why cash is important; cash-flow forecasts: cash inflows, cash outflows, net cash flow, opening and closing balances.
- Interpreting a simple cash-flow forecast; amending one; ways to deal with a short-term cash-flow problem.
- Working capital: meaning and importance.

## Notes

### Why businesses need finance

- **Start-up capital:** finance needed to set up a business (premises, equipment, first inventory).
- **Capital expenditure:** money spent on non-current assets that will last for more than one year (buildings, machinery, vehicles).
- **Revenue expenditure:** money spent on day-to-day expenses that do not involve buying long-term assets (wages, rent, raw materials, electricity).
- Also needed for expansion and to survive periods of low sales.

### Sources of finance

**Internal sources** (from within the business):

| Source | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Retained profit | No interest; no repayment; no loss of control | Not available to new or loss-making businesses; less paid to owners as dividends |
| Sale of existing assets | Uses unwanted assets; no interest | Takes time; assets may be needed later; not available to new businesses |
| Owner's savings (sole traders/partnerships) | No interest; quick | Limited amount; owner risks personal money |

**External sources** (from outside the business):

| Source | Term | Key features |
|---|---|---|
| Issue of shares | Long-term (permanent) | Only for limited companies; no interest and never repaid; but shareholders expect dividends and new shareholders dilute control (plc shares can be bought by anyone — takeover risk) |
| Bank loan | Medium to long-term | Fixed amount, repaid with interest over a set period; owners keep control; needs security (collateral); interest adds to costs |
| Overdraft | Short-term | Bank lets the business take more out of its account than it has, up to a limit; flexible — interest only on amount overdrawn; but high interest rates, bank can demand repayment at any time |
| Trade credit | Short-term | Suppliers let the business pay for goods later (e.g. 30–60 days); no interest; but may lose discounts for early payment and damage relationship if late |
| Debt factoring | Short-term | Selling trade receivables (money owed by customers) to a debt factor for immediate cash; factor keeps a percentage, so the business receives less than the full amount |
| Hire purchase | Medium-term | Buy an asset by paying a deposit and instalments, owning it after the last payment; spreads cost, but total cost is higher (interest) |
| Leasing | Medium-term | Renting an asset (e.g. vehicles, machines); no large outlay, maintenance often included; but never owned and total cost higher |
| Government grants | Varies | Free money, not repaid, for start-ups or businesses in areas of high unemployment; but conditions attached and limited |
| Microfinance | Short/medium | Very small loans to people (often in developing countries) who cannot get bank loans; helps poor entrepreneurs start businesses |
| Crowdfunding | Varies | Raising money from a large number of people, usually online, who each invest a small amount; also tests market interest; but may fail to reach target and the idea is public (can be copied) |

### Choosing a source of finance

Ask:
1. **Purpose and time period:** long-term assets (factory) → long-term finance (loan, shares); short-term needs (paying wages) → short-term finance (overdraft, trade credit). Never finance a new factory with an overdraft.
2. **Amount needed:** large amounts → share issue or bank loan; small → overdraft, owner's savings.
3. **Legal form and size:** sole traders and partnerships cannot issue shares; large plcs find it easier to borrow.
4. **Control:** issuing shares may reduce the owners' control; loans do not.
5. **Risk and cost:** interest rates; a business with lots of existing debt is riskier for lenders and finds loans costlier.

**What lenders look for:** a business plan and cash-flow forecast showing the loan can be repaid; collateral (security); existing debts; the owner's experience; how much the owners are investing themselves.

### Cash flow

**Cash flow** is the cash inflows and outflows of a business over a period of time. **Profit is not cash**: a profitable business can run out of cash (e.g. if customers pay late, or if it buys expensive equipment), and a business without enough cash cannot pay suppliers, wages or loans and may have to close.

- **Cash inflows:** cash sales, payments by trade receivables (credit customers), loans received, sale of assets, owner's capital, investment from shareholders.
- **Cash outflows:** purchases of materials, wages, rent, electricity, loan repayments, interest, purchase of non-current assets, tax.

### Cash-flow forecast

A **cash-flow forecast** is an estimate of future cash inflows and outflows of a business, usually on a month-by-month basis.

Key rules:
- **Net cash flow = total cash inflows − total cash outflows**
- **Closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow**
- **Opening balance of next month = closing balance of this month**

Worked layout — *Café Luna*, January–April (€):

| | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cash sales | 8,000 | 9,000 | 6,000 | 7,500 |
| Bank loan | 0 | 5,000 | 0 | 0 |
| **Total cash inflows** | 8,000 | 14,000 | 6,000 | 7,500 |
| Rent | 1,500 | 1,500 | 1,500 | 1,500 |
| Wages | 3,000 | 3,000 | 3,000 | 3,000 |
| Supplies | 2,800 | 3,100 | 2,200 | 2,600 |
| New equipment | 0 | 9,000 | 0 | 0 |
| **Total cash outflows** | 7,300 | 16,600 | 6,700 | 7,100 |
| **Net cash flow** | 700 | (2,600) | (700) | 400 |
| Opening balance | 2,000 | 2,700 | 100 | (600) |
| **Closing balance** | 2,700 | 100 | (600) | (200) |

Brackets ( ) mean a negative figure. Café Luna has a negative closing balance in March and April: it would need an overdraft of at least €600 in March, or must change its plans (e.g. lease the equipment instead of buying it).

**Uses of a cash-flow forecast:** shows when the business may run short of cash so it can arrange an overdraft in advance; supports a loan application; helps plan when to buy assets; helps manage the business and set targets. **Limitation:** it is only a forecast — sales and costs may differ.

### Solving a short-term cash-flow problem

| Method | How it helps | Drawback |
|---|---|---|
| Increase bank loans / overdraft | Immediate cash | Interest; bank may refuse |
| Delay payments to suppliers (more trade credit) | Cash stays in business longer | Suppliers may refuse, or stop supplying |
| Ask customers to pay sooner / reduce credit given | Faster inflows | Customers may go to competitors |
| Delay or cancel capital expenditure | Reduces outflows | Expansion delayed; old equipment less efficient |
| Lease rather than buy assets | No large outflow | Higher total cost |
| Reduce inventory / sell unwanted assets | Releases cash | Risk of running out of inventory |
| Debt factoring | Immediate cash from receivables | Lose a percentage of the money owed |

### Working capital

**Working capital = current assets − current liabilities.** It is the finance available to pay day-to-day expenses (wages, suppliers). Too little → cannot pay debts when due; too much (e.g. lots of idle cash or inventory) → resources not used profitably.

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Start-up capital | The finance needed by a new business to pay for essential non-current and current assets before it can start trading |
| Capital expenditure | Money spent on non-current assets that will last for more than one year |
| Revenue expenditure | Money spent on day-to-day expenses which do not involve the purchase of a long-term asset |
| Overdraft | Bank agrees to a business borrowing up to an agreed limit as and when required |
| Trade credit | When a business delays paying its suppliers, which leaves it with more cash in the short term |
| Debt factoring | Selling trade receivables (claims over debtors) to a debt factor for immediate cash |
| Hire purchase | An asset is sold to a business, which makes a deposit and pays the remaining amount in instalments over time |
| Leasing | Obtaining the use of an asset and paying a leasing charge over a fixed period, without owning it |
| Microfinance | Providing financial services, including small loans, to poor people not served by traditional banks |
| Crowdfunding | Funding a project or venture by raising money from a large number of people who each contribute a relatively small amount, usually online |
| Cash-flow forecast | An estimate of future cash inflows and outflows of a business, usually on a month-by-month basis |
| Net cash flow | Cash inflows minus cash outflows over a period of time |
| Working capital | The capital needed to pay for raw materials, day-to-day running costs and credit offered to customers; current assets minus current liabilities |

## Worked examples

**Case context:** *Fit&Go* is a partnership that runs a small gym. It wants €60,000 to buy new exercise machines that will last about 8 years. Its cash-flow forecast shows a negative closing balance of €3,500 in August, when many members are on holiday.

**Q1. Identify two sources of short-term finance. [2]**

Overdraft (1); trade credit / debt factoring (1).

**Q2. Explain two ways Fit&Go could improve its cash flow in August. [6]**

- Arrange an overdraft (K) of at least €3,500 for August (App), so it can still pay wages and rent; as members return in September the overdraft can be repaid quickly, so the interest cost is small (An).
- Offer a summer membership deal paid in advance (K) to members who stay in the city in August (App), increasing cash inflows in the weakest month, although at a lower price per member (An).

**Q3. Should Fit&Go finance the €60,000 machines with a bank loan or by leasing them? Justify your answer. [6]**

- Bank loan (K): the partners would own the machines, which last 8 years, so a long-term loan matches the purpose (App/An). But the monthly repayments plus interest would add to outflows and could make the August cash shortage worse; as a partnership with unlimited liability, the partners' personal assets are at risk if they cannot repay (An).
- Leasing (K): no large payment of €60,000; maintenance is often included and machines can be upgraded (App/An). But total cost over 8 years is higher and Fit&Go never owns them.
- **Decision (Ev):** Leasing is better for Fit&Go. Its main problem is cash flow, and leasing avoids a large debt and keeps payments predictable. Gym equipment also goes out of fashion, so being able to upgrade matters to keep members. A loan would be better only if the partners had strong, steady cash inflows throughout the year.

**Q4. Complete the missing values (€). [3]**

| | Month 1 | Month 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Total inflows | 6,000 | 8,000 |
| Total outflows | 7,200 | 6,500 |
| Net cash flow | (i) | 1,500 |
| Opening balance | 1,500 | (ii) |
| Closing balance | 300 | (iii) |

(i) 6,000 − 7,200 = (1,200) (1); (ii) 300 (1); (iii) 300 + 1,500 = 1,800 (1).

## Common mistakes

- Confusing **profit** and **cash** — say clearly why a profitable business can still run out of cash.
- Forgetting that the next month's **opening balance** is this month's **closing balance**.
- Recommending an overdraft (short-term) to buy long-term assets such as buildings.
- Saying sole traders or partnerships can "issue shares".
- Not applying finance choices to the legal form, size, amount and purpose in the case.
- Arithmetic with negative numbers: (600) + 400 = (200), not 1,000.
