# Financial statements and ratios

> **In the exam:** Appears in most series. Paper 1: "Define retained profit" [2], "Calculate the current ratio" or "Calculate the gross profit margin" [2], "Outline two reasons why shareholders would be interested in the income statement" [4], "Explain two ways to improve the profit margin" [6], "Has the business's performance improved? Justify your answer" [6]. In Paper 2 an appendix often gives an income statement or statement of financial position extract for two years: you calculate ratios and use them to evaluate the business's performance or a decision.

## What you need to know

- What profit is and why it matters (reward for risk, source of finance, indicator of success); the difference between profit and cash.
- **Income statement:** main features — revenue, cost of sales, gross profit, expenses, profit, retained profit; using it to make decisions.
- **Statement of financial position:** main elements — assets and liabilities; non-current and current assets; current and non-current liabilities; shareholders' equity. Interpreting it.
- Profitability ratios: **gross profit margin, profit margin, return on capital employed (ROCE)**.
- Liquidity ratios: **current ratio, acid test ratio**.
- The difference between profitability and liquidity; why each matters.
- Interpreting ratios; how they can be improved; comparing over time and with other businesses.
- Why and how different users (stakeholders) use accounts and ratio analysis.
- Limitations of using accounts and ratios.

## Notes

### Profit and accounts

**Profit** = total revenue − total costs. It rewards owners for taking risks, is a source of finance (retained profit) and is a sign of success to lenders and investors.

**Profit ≠ cash.** A business can be profitable but have little cash (credit sales not yet paid, money spent on inventory or assets).

### Income statement

An **income statement** is a financial statement that records the income of a business and all costs incurred to earn that income over a period of time (e.g. one year).

Example — *Lumen Ltd*, year ended 31 December (€000):

| | €000 |
|---|---|
| Revenue | 500 |
| Cost of sales | (300) |
| **Gross profit** | **200** |
| Expenses (overheads: rent, salaries, marketing, interest…) | (140) |
| **Profit** | **60** |
| Tax | (12) |
| Dividends paid to shareholders | (18) |
| **Retained profit** | **30** |

- **Revenue** = selling price × quantity sold (sales).
- **Cost of sales** = cost of producing or buying the goods that were sold. (Cost of sales = opening inventory + purchases − closing inventory.)
- **Gross profit** = revenue − cost of sales.
- **Profit** = gross profit − expenses.
- **Retained profit** = profit after tax and dividends kept in the business (a source of finance for next year).

**Uses:** compare revenue, gross profit and profit with previous years and with competitors; decide on prices, cost control, expansion; shows whether the business can afford dividends.

### Statement of financial position

A **statement of financial position** shows the value of a business's assets and liabilities at a particular point in time (e.g. on 31 December).

Example — *Lumen Ltd* at 31 December (€000):

| | €000 | €000 |
|---|---|---|
| **Non-current assets** (land, buildings, machinery, vehicles) | | 350 |
| **Current assets** | | |
| Inventories | 40 | |
| Trade receivables | 25 | |
| Cash | 15 | |
| Total current assets | | 80 |
| **Total assets** | | **430** |
| **Current liabilities** | | |
| Trade payables | 30 | |
| Bank overdraft | 10 | |
| Total current liabilities | | 40 |
| **Non-current liabilities** (long-term bank loan) | | 100 |
| **Total liabilities** | | **140** |
| **Net assets** (total assets − total liabilities) | | **290** |
| **Shareholders' equity** | | |
| Share capital | 200 | |
| Retained earnings (accumulated retained profit) | 90 | |
| **Total shareholders' equity** | | **290** |

- **Assets:** items of value owned by the business.
  - **Non-current assets:** kept and used for more than one year.
  - **Current assets:** likely to be turned into cash within a year (inventories, trade receivables, cash).
- **Liabilities:** debts owed by the business.
  - **Current liabilities:** must be paid within a year (trade payables, overdraft).
  - **Non-current liabilities:** repaid over more than a year (long-term loans).
- Net assets = shareholders' equity (the statement "balances").
- **Capital employed** = shareholders' equity + non-current liabilities = 290 + 100 = €390,000.

### Profitability ratios

Profitability: the ability of a business to generate profit relative to its revenue or the capital invested.

| Ratio | Formula | Lumen Ltd |
|---|---|---|
| Gross profit margin (%) | gross profit ÷ revenue × 100 | 200 ÷ 500 × 100 = **40%** |
| Profit margin (%) | profit ÷ revenue × 100 | 60 ÷ 500 × 100 = **12%** |
| Return on capital employed (ROCE) (%) | profit ÷ capital employed × 100 | 60 ÷ 390 × 100 = **15.4%** |

Meaning: of every €1 of revenue, 40 cents is gross profit and 12 cents is profit. Every €100 invested earns €15.40 profit. Higher is better. Compare ROCE with the interest rate a bank would pay — if ROCE is lower, the investment is not worthwhile.

**Improving gross profit margin:** raise prices (risk: lower sales if demand is price-elastic); find cheaper suppliers (risk: lower quality).
**Improving profit margin:** as above, plus cut expenses (e.g. cheaper premises, less advertising — risk: lower sales or quality).
**Improving ROCE:** increase profit without increasing capital employed; sell unused assets.

### Liquidity ratios

**Liquidity** is the ability of a business to pay back its short-term debts (current liabilities). A business that cannot pay its short-term debts may be forced to close — even if it is profitable.

| Ratio | Formula | Lumen Ltd |
|---|---|---|
| Current ratio | current assets ÷ current liabilities | 80 ÷ 40 = **2.0** (or 2:1) |
| Acid test ratio | (current assets − inventories) ÷ current liabilities | (80 − 40) ÷ 40 = **1.0** (or 1:1) |

- The acid test is stricter because inventory may be hard to turn into cash quickly.
- **Interpretation:** a current ratio of around 1.5–2 and an acid test of around 1 are usually considered acceptable. Below 1 → possible difficulty paying short-term debts. Very high → too much cash/inventory tied up and not used profitably.
- The right level depends on the industry (e.g. supermarkets sell inventory quickly for cash, so they can run lower ratios).

**Improving liquidity:** sell unused non-current assets for cash, raise long-term finance (share issue, long-term loan) to replace an overdraft, reduce inventory (sell it), collect receivables faster.

### Comparing performance (Lumen Ltd, two years)

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue (€000) | 500 | 560 |
| Gross profit (€000) | 200 | 196 |
| Profit (€000) | 60 | 42 |
| Gross profit margin | 40% | 35% |
| Profit margin | 12% | 7.5% |

Revenue rose 12%, but both margins fell: cost of sales rose faster than revenue (perhaps higher material costs or price cuts) and expenses rose from €140,000 to €154,000. Performance has worsened in terms of profitability even though the business sold more.

### Users of accounts

| User | Why they are interested |
|---|---|
| Shareholders / owners | Profitability, dividends, whether to buy more shares or sell |
| Managers | Measure performance, control costs, make decisions, compare with competitors |
| Banks / lenders | Liquidity and profit — can the business repay a loan with interest? |
| Suppliers | Liquidity — will they be paid for goods sold on credit? |
| Employees | Job security; can the business afford pay rises? |
| Government | Tax to pay; whether the business is successful (jobs) |
| Competitors | Compare performance |

### Limitations of accounts and ratios

- Based on past data — the future may be different.
- Ratios compare two numbers but do not explain **why** they changed.
- Different businesses may use different accounting methods, so comparisons may be misleading.
- Inflation distorts comparisons over time.
- Non-financial factors are not shown (staff motivation, brand image, environmental impact).
- The statement of financial position is a snapshot of a single day.

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Income statement | A financial statement that records the income of a business and all costs incurred to earn that income over a period of time |
| Revenue | The income during a period of time from the sale of goods or services |
| Cost of sales | The cost of producing or buying in the goods actually sold by the business during a period |
| Gross profit | Revenue minus cost of sales |
| Profit | Gross profit minus expenses (overheads) |
| Retained profit | The profit left after tax and dividends have been paid, kept in the business |
| Statement of financial position | A statement showing the value of a business's assets and liabilities at a particular time |
| Non-current assets | Assets kept and used by the business for more than one year |
| Current assets | Assets that are likely to be turned into cash before the next statement of financial position (within a year) |
| Current liabilities | Debts of the business that must be paid within a year |
| Non-current liabilities | Debts that are repayable after more than one year |
| Capital employed | Shareholders' equity plus non-current liabilities: the total long-term finance invested in the business |
| Liquidity | The ability of a business to pay back its short-term debts |

## Worked examples

**Case context:** *Pinar Ltd* makes garden furniture. Extracts from its accounts (€000):

| | 2025 | 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 240 | 300 |
| Gross profit | 96 | 108 |
| Profit | 18 | 15 |
| Capital employed | 150 | 200 |
| Current assets | 45 | 48 |
| of which inventories | 20 | 30 |
| Current liabilities | 30 | 40 |

**Q1. Calculate the gross profit margin for 2025 and 2026. [2]**

2025: 96 ÷ 240 × 100 = 40% (1). 2026: 108 ÷ 300 × 100 = 36% (1).

**Q2. Calculate the acid test ratio for 2026. [2]**

(48 − 30) ÷ 40 (1) = 0.45 (1).

**Q3. Using the data, do you think Pinar Ltd's performance improved between 2025 and 2026? Justify your answer. [6]**

Supporting calculations: profit margin 2025 = 18 ÷ 240 × 100 = 7.5%; 2026 = 15 ÷ 300 × 100 = 5%. ROCE 2025 = 18 ÷ 150 × 100 = 12%; 2026 = 15 ÷ 200 × 100 = 7.5%. Current ratio 2025 = 45 ÷ 30 = 1.5; 2026 = 48 ÷ 40 = 1.2. Acid test 2025 = 25 ÷ 30 = 0.83; 2026 = 0.45.

- Improvement: revenue grew by 25% (€240,000 → €300,000), so more furniture is being sold and market share may have grown (App/An).
- Worsening profitability: gross profit margin fell from 40% to 36% and profit margin from 7.5% to 5%, so costs grew faster than sales — perhaps higher wood prices or discounts to win sales. ROCE fell from 12% to 7.5%: the extra €50,000 of capital earned less profit (An).
- Worsening liquidity: the acid test fell from 0.83 to 0.45 because inventories rose from €20,000 to €30,000; Pinar may struggle to pay suppliers if furniture does not sell (An).
- **Judgement (Ev):** Overall performance worsened. Higher sales are only valuable if they are profitable, and profit actually fell by €3,000 while liquidity weakened. The most urgent issue is the low acid test ratio: Pinar should reduce its inventory of furniture (e.g. a seasonal promotion) before it causes a cash crisis, and then review its costs and pricing.

## Common mistakes

- Using profit instead of gross profit (or the reverse) in a margin calculation — read the formula carefully.
- Forgetting the × 100 for percentage ratios, or adding a % sign to the current ratio (it is a number or a ratio such as 1.5:1).
- Subtracting the wrong item in the acid test — subtract **inventories**, not cash or receivables.
- Mixing up the two statements: the income statement covers a **period**; the statement of financial position shows **one point in time**.
- Saying "profitability and liquidity are the same" — explain the difference.
- Calculating ratios but not interpreting them: always say what the change means and suggest a reason.
