# External influences

> **In the exam:** Appears in most series, often as the last question in Paper 1 or in Paper 2. Typical questions: "Define recession" [2], "Identify two government economic objectives" [2], "Outline two effects of an increase in interest rates on the business" [4], "Explain two effects of an appreciation of the exchange rate" [6], "Do you think the business should become more environmentally friendly? Justify your answer" [6]. Paper 2 often asks you to evaluate how an external change (higher tax, recession, new law, currency change) will affect the case-study business [12].

## What you need to know

- The business cycle (growth, boom, recession, slump) and the impact of each stage on businesses (production, employment, prices, sales).
- Government economic objectives: economic growth (GDP), low inflation, low unemployment, balance of payments stability; how businesses are affected when these are not achieved.
- Government economic policies: fiscal policy (taxes and government spending), monetary policy (interest rates), supply-side policies; how they affect businesses and how businesses might respond.
- Environmental and ethical issues: externalities (private and external costs and benefits); sustainable development; how and why businesses respond to environmental pressures (pressure groups, government controls, consumer pressure); ethical decisions and their impact.
- Business and the international economy: globalisation — opportunities and threats; protectionism (tariffs, import quotas); multinational companies; exchange rates — appreciation and depreciation and their effects on exporters and importers.

## Notes

### The business cycle

**Gross domestic product (GDP)** is the total value of output of goods and services in a country in one year. **Economic growth** is an increase in GDP.

The **business cycle** — the regular rise and fall in GDP over time:

| Stage | What happens | Impact on businesses |
|---|---|---|
| Growth | GDP rising, unemployment falling | Rising sales and profits; businesses expand and recruit |
| Boom | GDP very high; demand exceeds what economy can produce; inflation rises; labour shortages | Costs rise (wages, materials); hard to recruit; prices rise |
| Recession | GDP falls for six months or more; unemployment rises | Lower sales and profits; workers made redundant; some businesses fail |
| Slump | Serious, long recession; very high unemployment, prices may fall | Many businesses close; survival becomes main objective |

Then recovery/growth begins again.

### Government economic objectives

| Objective | Why it matters to business | If not achieved |
|---|---|---|
| Economic growth (rising GDP) | Higher incomes → higher sales | Recession: falling demand, job losses |
| Low and stable inflation | Stable costs and prices, easier planning | High **inflation** (general rise in prices): rising costs, consumers' real incomes fall, exports less competitive |
| Low unemployment | Higher spending; less government spending on benefits | High unemployment: lower demand (especially for luxury goods); but easier to recruit cheaply |
| Balance of payments stability | Exports and imports roughly balanced | Large deficit can lead to currency depreciation and government action |

### Government economic policies

**Fiscal policy** — changes in taxes and government spending.

| Tax / spending | Effect of an increase |
|---|---|
| Income tax | Consumers have less disposable income → lower demand (especially for luxury goods); may reduce incentives to work |
| Profit tax (corporation tax) | Less profit kept after tax → less finance for investment; less incentive to set up business |
| Indirect taxes (sales tax / VAT) | Prices rise → demand falls; consumers may switch to cheaper products |
| Import tariffs | Imported materials cost more; but domestic producers protected from foreign competition |
| Government spending | More spending (e.g. on roads, schools, defence) → more demand for businesses that supply the government; better infrastructure |

**Monetary policy** — changes in the **interest rate** (usually set by the central bank, e.g. the European Central Bank for the eurozone).

An increase in interest rates:
- Businesses with loans/overdrafts pay more interest → higher costs, lower profit (e.g. €200,000 loan: 4% = €8,000 a year; 6% = €12,000 a year).
- Less likely to borrow to expand.
- Consumers with loans/mortgages have less to spend; consumers save more and borrow less → lower demand, especially for goods bought on credit (cars, houses, furniture).
- The exchange rate may appreciate → exports harder to sell.

**Supply-side policies** — aim to make the economy more efficient and productive: privatisation, improving education and training, increasing competition (fewer regulations), lowering taxes on profits to encourage investment. Benefits for business: better-trained workers, more efficient markets, lower costs.

**How businesses respond:** e.g. to a recession or higher taxes — cut costs, reduce prices, launch cheaper products, find new (export) markets, delay expansion.

### Environmental and ethical issues

**Externalities:**

| | Costs | Benefits |
|---|---|---|
| Private (paid/received by the business or its customers) | Costs of building a factory, wages, materials | Revenue, profit |
| External (paid/received by the rest of society) | Pollution, congestion, noise, damage to habitats | Jobs created, better roads/infrastructure, training, local suppliers gain |

**Social cost = private costs + external costs.** **Social benefit = private benefits + external benefits.** Governments want projects where social benefits exceed social costs.

**Sustainable development:** development that meets the needs of the present without reducing the ability of future generations to meet their needs. Businesses can: use renewable energy, recycle and reduce waste, use less packaging, use sustainable raw materials, produce more efficient products.

**Why businesses respond to environmental pressures:**
- **Consumer pressure** — customers buy from "green" businesses; boycotts of polluters.
- **Pressure groups** — organisations of people with a common interest who campaign for change (e.g. environmental groups) using publicity, protests, boycotts and lobbying. Powerful when they have public support and media attention, and when the business's sales are affected.
- **Government controls** — laws and fines (e.g. on pollution levels), pollution permits, environmental taxes, planning restrictions.
- Benefits to business: better brand image and sales, lower costs (less energy and waste), avoid fines. Costs: more expensive materials and equipment; higher prices may reduce competitiveness.

**Ethical decisions:** decisions based on moral principles — e.g. not using child labour, paying fair wages to suppliers in poorer countries (fair trade), not testing on animals, honest advertising, avoiding bribery. Benefits: better reputation, consumer loyalty, easier recruitment. Costs: higher costs, lower profit in the short run.

### Globalisation

**Globalisation** — the increasing worldwide trade and movement of people and capital between countries. Caused by: free-trade agreements, cheaper and faster transport, internet and communications technology, growth of developing economies.

| Opportunities for businesses | Threats for businesses |
|---|---|
| New export markets → more sales | More foreign competition in the home market |
| Import cheaper materials and components | Employees may leave for better-paid jobs abroad |
| Locate production in lower-cost countries | Foreign businesses may set up locally |
| Become multinational; spread risk | Exchange-rate risk |

**Protectionism** — when a government protects domestic businesses from foreign competition:
- **Tariff:** a tax on imported goods → imports more expensive.
- **Import quota:** a limit on the quantity of a product that can be imported.
- Effects: domestic firms protected; but higher prices for consumers, imported materials cost more, other countries may retaliate against exports.

**Multinational companies:** see *Objectives, stakeholders and growth* — benefits and drawbacks to the host country.

### Exchange rates

The **exchange rate** is the price of one currency in terms of another (e.g. €1 = USD 1.10).

- **Appreciation** — the value of a currency rises (each € buys more US dollars).
- **Depreciation** — the value of a currency falls (each € buys fewer US dollars).

Example — a Spanish company sells a product for €45 in the USA and buys a component for USD 33:

| | €1 = USD 1.10 | €1 = USD 1.25 (euro appreciated) |
|---|---|---|
| Price of export in USA | 45 × 1.10 = USD 49.50 | 45 × 1.25 = USD 56.25 (more expensive → lower sales in USA) |
| Cost of imported component in € | 33 ÷ 1.10 = €30.00 | 33 ÷ 1.25 = €26.40 (cheaper imports) |

Memory aid **SPICED**: **S**trong **P**ound (currency) → **I**mports **C**heaper, **E**xports **D**earer. A depreciation does the opposite: exports cheaper (more competitive), imports dearer.

How exporters might respond to an appreciation: keep the foreign price the same and accept a lower profit margin; cut costs; focus on quality/brand so demand is less price-sensitive; buy more inputs from abroad (now cheaper).

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Gross domestic product (GDP) | The total value of output of goods and services in a country in one year |
| Business cycle | The regular increases and decreases in economic activity (GDP) over time |
| Recession | A period of falling GDP lasting six months or more |
| Inflation | The general rise in prices in an economy |
| Unemployment | When people who are willing and able to work cannot find a job |
| Fiscal policy | Changes in government spending and taxes |
| Monetary policy | Changes in the interest rate (and money supply) |
| Supply-side policies | Policies that aim to make the economy more efficient and competitive |
| External costs | The costs of an activity paid by the rest of society, not by the business (e.g. pollution) |
| Sustainable development | Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their needs |
| Pressure group | A group of people with a common interest who act together to change the policies of businesses or the government |
| Globalisation | The increasing worldwide trade and movement of people and capital between countries |
| Import tariff | A tax on an imported product |
| Import quota | A physical limit on the quantity of a product that can be imported |
| Exchange rate appreciation | A rise in the value of a currency compared with other currencies |
| Exchange rate depreciation | A fall in the value of a currency compared with other currencies |

## Worked examples

**Case context:** *Olea SA* is a Spanish producer of premium olive oil. It exports 60% of its output to the USA and imports glass bottles from Turkey. It has a €500,000 bank loan to pay for a new bottling line. The euro has just appreciated against the US dollar, and the European Central Bank has raised interest rates.

**Q1. Identify two government economic objectives. [2]**

Economic growth (1); low inflation / low unemployment / balance of payments stability (1).

**Q2. Explain two effects on Olea SA of the increase in interest rates. [6]**

- Higher interest costs (K): Olea has a €500,000 bank loan (App), so if, for example, the rate rose by 1 percentage point, it would pay €5,000 more interest a year, reducing profit and possibly cash available for marketing (An).
- Lower demand at home (K): consumers in Spain with loans and mortgages have less disposable income (App), so they may switch from premium oil to cheaper supermarket brands, lowering domestic sales (An).

**Q3. Do you think the appreciation of the euro will reduce Olea SA's profit? Justify your answer. [6]**

- Exports: each bottle priced in euros now costs more in dollars (K). As 60% of output goes to the USA (App), US sales may fall, or Olea must cut its euro price to keep the dollar price the same, reducing its profit margin (An).
- Imports: the glass bottles imported from Turkey become cheaper in euros if the euro has also appreciated against the Turkish lira (App), reducing costs slightly (An).
- **Judgement (Ev):** Yes, profit is likely to fall, because exports are 60% of output and the extra cost to US buyers affects most of Olea's revenue, while the saving on bottles is a small part of total costs. The effect will be smaller if US customers are loyal to Olea's premium brand (price-inelastic demand), so Olea should emphasise quality in its US marketing rather than cutting prices.

## Common mistakes

- Getting exchange-rate effects the wrong way round — use SPICED and check with a numerical example.
- Saying a recession means "the business will close" — explain the effect on demand, and remember that producers of cheaper (inferior) goods may gain.
- Confusing **fiscal** (tax and spending) and **monetary** (interest rate) policy.
- Writing about interest rates without linking them to the business's loans, its customers' borrowing, or its products.
- Listing environmental actions without explaining the **cost/benefit to the business**.
- Treating protectionism as only positive or only negative — the effect depends on whether the business exports, imports or competes with imports.

## Practice questions

1. Define "globalisation". [2]
2. Identify two stages of the business cycle. [2]
3. Outline two ways a car manufacturer could be affected by an increase in sales tax. [4]
4. A US company sells a machine for USD 9,000. Calculate its price in euros when €1 = USD 1.20, and again when €1 = USD 1.00. [2]
5. Explain two external costs and one external benefit of building a new airport. [6]
6. A clothing retailer is under pressure from a pressure group to stop buying from factories that pay very low wages. Should it change its suppliers? Justify your answer. [6]

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. The increasing worldwide trade (1) and movement of people and capital between countries (1).
2. Any two: growth, boom, recession, slump (accept recovery).
3. Prices of cars rise (2), so demand falls — especially as cars are expensive and non-essential, and consumers may delay buying (2). Accept: lower profit if the manufacturer absorbs the tax.
4. 9,000 ÷ 1.20 = €7,500 (1); 9,000 ÷ 1.00 = €9,000 (1).
5. External costs: noise pollution for local residents; air pollution/carbon emissions; congestion on local roads; loss of land/habitats (2 × K + App/An). External benefit: jobs for local people / more tourism for local businesses / better transport links (K + App/An).
6. Change: protects brand image, avoids boycotts and negative media, ethical customers more loyal, may avoid future laws. Against: higher costs → higher prices or lower margins, competitors may stay cheaper, hard to check every factory. Justified decision, e.g. "Yes, gradually — because a public campaign can quickly damage sales among young customers who use social media; the retailer could pay slightly higher prices and use its promotion to tell customers, turning the cost into a selling point."
