# Chemical bonding

> **In the exam:** Chemistry is about one third of each 0654 paper. **Paper 2** (Multiple Choice, 45 min, 40 questions across biology, chemistry and physics) usually has 1–2 bonding questions (bond type from properties, dot-and-cross diagrams, diamond/graphite). **Paper 4** (Theory, 2 h, 120 marks) often includes a dot-and-cross diagram (2–3 marks) and an "explain why ... has a high melting point / conducts electricity" part (2–3 marks) inside a chemistry question. **Paper 6** (Alternative to Practical, 1 h 30 min, 60 marks) may ask you to interpret conductivity or melting-point results. The explanation marks are lost more often than any others in the topic.

## What you need to know

- Describe the formation of positive ions (cations) and negative ions (anions).
- State that an ionic bond is a strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
- Describe the formation of ionic bonds between elements from Group I and Group VII, including the use of dot-and-cross diagrams; *Supplement:* between ions of metallic and non-metallic elements, including the use of dot-and-cross diagrams.
- Describe the properties of ionic compounds: high melting points and boiling points; good electrical conductivity when aqueous or molten and poor when solid; generally soluble in water.
- *Supplement:* explain in terms of structure and bonding the high melting and boiling points and the electrical conductivity of ionic compounds; describe the giant lattice structure of ionic compounds as a regular arrangement of alternating positive and negative ions, exemplified by sodium chloride.
- State that a covalent bond is formed when a pair of electrons is shared between two atoms leading to noble gas electronic configurations.
- Describe the formation of covalent bonds in H₂, Cl₂, H₂O, CH₄, NH₃ and HCl using dot-and-cross diagrams; *Supplement:* also CH₃OH, C₂H₄, O₂, CO₂ and N₂.
- Describe in terms of structure and bonding the properties of simple molecular compounds: low melting and boiling points; poor electrical conductivity. *Supplement:* explain these, low melting and boiling points in terms of weak intermolecular forces (specific types of intermolecular forces are not required).
- Describe the giant covalent structures of graphite and diamond.
- *Supplement:* relate the structures and bonding of graphite and diamond to their uses, limited to graphite as a lubricant and as an electrode, and diamond in cutting tools.
- *Supplement:* describe metallic bonding as the electrostatic attraction between the positive ions in a giant metallic lattice and a "sea" of delocalised electrons; explain in terms of structure and bonding the good electrical conductivity and malleability of metals.

*Not in 0654:* silicon(IV) oxide, SiO₂ (separate Chemistry 0620 only).

## Notes

### Ions

- Metals **lose** outer-shell electrons to form **positive ions** (cations): Na (2,8,1) → Na⁺ (2,8) + e⁻.
- Non-metals **gain** electrons to form **negative ions** (anions): Cl (2,8,7) + e⁻ → Cl⁻ (2,8,8).
- Charges: Group I +1, Group II +2, Group III (Al) +3, Group V −3, Group VI −2, Group VII −1.
- Compound ions to know: NH₄⁺, OH⁻, NO₃⁻, CO₃²⁻, SO₄²⁻ (useful for formulae).

### Ionic bonding

**Ionic bond:** a strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.

Dot-and-cross diagram for NaCl: Na⁺ drawn with **no** outer electrons shown (or a full 2,8 inner shell) in square brackets with + outside; Cl⁻ with **eight** outer electrons (seven of one symbol, one of the other) in square brackets with − outside. For MgO: [Mg]²⁺ and [O]²⁻ with 8 outer electrons on oxygen (6 dots + 2 crosses). For MgCl₂: one Mg²⁺ and **two** separate Cl⁻ ions. For extended questions, only the outer shell is usually required, but charges and brackets are essential.

**Giant ionic lattice (Supplement):** a regular arrangement of alternating positive and negative ions in three dimensions (e.g. NaCl: each Na⁺ surrounded by six Cl⁻ and vice versa).

| Property | Explanation (Supplement wording) |
|---|---|
| High melting and boiling points | Strong electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions in a giant lattice; a lot of energy is needed to overcome them |
| Do not conduct when solid | Ions are in fixed positions, cannot move |
| Conduct when molten or aqueous | Ions are free to move (and carry charge) |
| Generally soluble in water | (Core: state only; not explained) |

### Covalent bonding

**Covalent bond:** formed when a pair of electrons is shared between two atoms leading to noble gas electronic configurations.

| Molecule | Bonds | Lone pairs (non-bonding pairs) on central atom |
|---|---|---|
| H₂ | H–H (1 single) | – |
| Cl₂ | Cl–Cl (1 single) | 3 on each Cl |
| HCl | H–Cl | 3 on Cl |
| H₂O | 2 × O–H | 2 on O |
| NH₃ | 3 × N–H | 1 on N |
| CH₄ | 4 × C–H | 0 |
| CH₃OH *(Supp)* | 3 C–H, 1 C–O, 1 O–H | 2 on O |
| C₂H₄ *(Supp)* | C=C double (2 shared pairs), 4 C–H | 0 |
| O₂ *(Supp)* | O=O double | 2 on each O |
| CO₂ *(Supp)* | 2 × C=O double | 2 on each O |
| N₂ *(Supp)* | N≡N triple (3 shared pairs) | 1 on each N |

Checking a diagram: count electrons around every atom — H must have 2, every other atom 8. Shared pairs sit in the overlap of the circles.

### Simple molecular substances

- Strong covalent bonds **within** molecules; **weak intermolecular forces** (attractive forces between molecules).
- Low melting and boiling points: only the weak intermolecular forces need to be overcome, which takes little energy. (Covalent bonds are **not** broken on melting or boiling.)
- Poor electrical conductivity: no ions or electrons that are free to move (molecules are uncharged).
- Larger molecules have stronger intermolecular forces → higher boiling points (links to alkanes and fractional distillation).

### Giant covalent structures

| | Diamond | Graphite |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | each C covalently bonded to 4 others, tetrahedral, giant 3D lattice | each C bonded to 3 others in hexagonal layers; weak forces between layers; one delocalised electron per C |
| Melting point | very high (many strong covalent bonds) | very high |
| Hardness | very hard | soft, slippery (layers slide) |
| Conductivity | does not conduct (no free electrons) | conducts (delocalised electrons move along layers) |
| Uses *(Supplement)* | cutting tools | lubricant; electrodes |

*Supplement — linking structure to use:* diamond is used in cutting tools because it is very hard (every atom held by four strong covalent bonds in a rigid 3D lattice). Graphite is a lubricant because the layers are held by weak forces and slide over each other; it is used as an electrode because it conducts electricity (delocalised electrons) and has a very high melting point.

### Metallic bonding (Supplement)

**Metallic bond:** the electrostatic attraction between the positive ions in a giant metallic lattice and a "sea" of delocalised electrons.

| Property | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Good electrical conductivity | delocalised electrons are free to move through the structure (and carry charge) |
| Malleable | layers of positive ions can slide over each other without the bonding being broken (the delocalised electrons still hold them) |

(The 0654 syllabus limits the explanation to electrical conductivity and malleability.)

### Identifying structure from data

| Melting point | Conducts solid? | Conducts molten/aq? | Structure |
|---|---|---|---|
| high | no | yes | giant ionic |
| low | no | no | simple molecular |
| very high | no (except graphite) | no | giant covalent |
| usually high | yes | yes (molten) | metallic |

### Paper 6 skills on this topic

- Testing conductivity: a simple circuit with a power supply (cell), lamp or ammeter and two electrodes dipped into the solid, molten or aqueous substance. Conclusion: lamp lights = conducts.
- Deducing the type of structure from a table of results (melting point, conductivity, solubility).

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Ionic bond | A strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions |
| Covalent bond | A pair of electrons shared between two atoms leading to noble gas electronic configurations |
| Metallic bond | The electrostatic attraction between the positive ions in a giant metallic lattice and a "sea" of delocalised electrons |
| Ionic lattice | A regular arrangement of alternating positive and negative ions |
| Cation / anion | A positive ion / a negative ion |
| Intermolecular forces | Weak attractive forces between molecules |

## Worked examples

**1.** Draw a dot-and-cross diagram for calcium chloride, CaCl₂. Show outer-shell electrons only. [3]

- One Ca ion shown as [Ca]²⁺ with no outer electrons (1)
- Two chloride ions each [Cl]⁻ with 8 outer electrons: 7 dots + 1 cross (1)
- Correct charges on all ions (1)

**2.** Explain why magnesium oxide has a high melting point. [3]

- Giant ionic lattice / giant structure (1)
- Strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions (1)
- A lot of (thermal) energy is needed to overcome these attractions (1)

**3.** Graphite and diamond are both forms of carbon. Explain why graphite conducts electricity but diamond does not. [3]

- In graphite each carbon atom is bonded to three other atoms (1)
- so each atom has one delocalised electron / delocalised electrons that are free to move (1)
- in diamond all four outer electrons are used in covalent bonds, so there are no free electrons (1)

**4.** Draw a dot-and-cross diagram of a nitrogen molecule, N₂. [2]

- Three shared pairs between the two N atoms (1)
- One non-bonding pair on each N atom (total 8 electrons around each N) (1)

## Common mistakes

- Saying ionic compounds conduct when molten "because electrons are free to move" — it is **ions** that move. Metals and graphite: electrons.
- Saying simple molecular substances have low melting points because "covalent bonds are weak" — the covalent bonds are strong; the **intermolecular forces** are weak.
- Using "intermolecular forces" for giant structures (diamond, NaCl). Giant covalent → many strong covalent bonds must be broken.
- Forgetting square brackets and charges on ionic dot-and-cross diagrams, or drawing a single Cl⁻ for MgCl₂.
- Showing a covalent bond between ions, or showing electrons "transferred" with arrows but no final charges.
- In metallic bonding, writing "positive **atoms**" or "nuclei" instead of positive **ions**, or forgetting the word **delocalised**.
- Saying layers in graphite are held by "weak bonds" — say **weak forces** (intermolecular forces) between layers.

## Practice questions

1. State the type of bonding in (a) potassium bromide, (b) ammonia, (c) copper. [3]
2. Draw a dot-and-cross diagram for a molecule of water. Show outer-shell electrons only. [2]
3. Substance X melts at 801 °C. It does not conduct electricity when solid but does when molten. Substance Y melts at −23 °C and does not conduct when solid or liquid. Identify the type of structure of X and Y. Explain why X conducts only when molten. [4]
4. Draw a dot-and-cross diagram for carbon dioxide. Show outer-shell electrons only. [2]
5. Explain why copper is malleable. [2]
6. Explain why diamond is used in cutting tools. [2]
7. Explain why methane has a low boiling point. [2]
8. Give one use of graphite and link it to its structure. [2]

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. (a) ionic (b) covalent (c) metallic.
2. Two O–H shared pairs (each shared pair = one dot + one cross) (1); two non-bonding pairs on O, 8 electrons around O (1).
3. X: giant ionic (1). Y: simple molecular (1). X: in the solid, ions are in fixed positions / cannot move (1); when molten, ions are free to move (1).
4. Two shared pairs between C and each O (two double bonds) (1); two non-bonding pairs on each O (8 around each atom) (1).
5. Layers of positive ions (1) can slide over each other while still held by the sea of delocalised electrons / without breaking the metallic bonding (1).
6. Diamond is very hard (1); each carbon atom is covalently bonded to four others in a giant (3D) lattice of strong covalent bonds (1).
7. Simple molecular / weak intermolecular forces between molecules (1); little energy needed to overcome them (1).
8. Lubricant: layers held by weak forces so they slide over each other (2); or electrodes: delocalised electrons free to move so it conducts and has high melting point (2).
