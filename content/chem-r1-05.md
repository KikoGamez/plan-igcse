# The mole: empirical formula and yield

> **In the exam:** Paper 4 regularly includes an empirical formula calculation (3–4 marks), a percentage yield or percentage purity step (1–2 marks) and a limiting-reactant question (2–4 marks), usually inside a longer structured question. Paper 2 has 1–2 questions. Paper 6 may ask you to find the formula of a hydrated salt or an oxide from mass readings and to suggest sources of error.

## What you need to know

- Define empirical formula as the simplest whole number ratio of the different atoms or ions in a compound.
- Define molecular formula as the number and type of different atoms in one molecule.
- Deduce the formula of a compound from given data.
- *Extended:* calculate empirical formulae and molecular formulae, given appropriate data.
- *Extended:* calculate percentage yield, percentage composition by mass and percentage purity, given appropriate data.
- *Extended:* calculate limiting reactants (identify which reactant is in excess).
- *Extended (Topic 7):* define hydrated substance, anhydrous substance and water of crystallisation; calculate the number of molecules of water of crystallisation from experimental data.

## Notes

### Empirical formula — the table method

| Step | C | H | O |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mass or % | 40.0 | 6.7 | 53.3 |
| ÷ Ar | 40.0 ÷ 12 = 3.33 | 6.7 ÷ 1 = 6.7 | 53.3 ÷ 16 = 3.33 |
| ÷ smallest | 1 | 2.01 | 1 |
| Ratio | 1 | 2 | 1 |

Empirical formula = CH₂O.

- If a ratio is close to x.5 (e.g. 1.5), multiply all by 2; if close to x.33 or x.67, multiply by 3.
- If only some masses are given, find the missing element by subtraction (e.g. mass of oxygen = mass of oxide − mass of metal).

### Molecular formula from empirical formula

n = Mr ÷ (empirical formula mass), then multiply every subscript by n.
CH₂O has formula mass 30; if Mr = 60, n = 2 → C₂H₄O₂.

### Percentage composition

% of element = (Ar × number of atoms ÷ Mr) × 100. Fe in Fe₂O₃: 112 ÷ 160 × 100 = 70.0%.

### Percentage yield

percentage yield = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100

- Theoretical yield is calculated from the equation, using the limiting reactant.
- Yields are below 100% because: the reaction is reversible / does not go to completion; product lost during transfer, filtration or crystallisation; side reactions.

### Percentage purity

percentage purity = (mass of pure substance ÷ mass of impure sample) × 100

Usually you find the mass of pure substance from a product (gas volume, precipitate mass or titration). Assume impurities do not react.

### Limiting reactant

1. Convert each reactant to moles.
2. Divide each by its coefficient in the equation (or compare with the ratio needed).
3. The one that runs out first is **limiting**; the other is **in excess**.
4. Use the **limiting** reactant to calculate the products.

Example: 2.40 g Mg with 50.0 cm³ of 2.00 mol/dm³ HCl. Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂.
n(Mg) = 0.100 mol; n(HCl) = 0.100 mol. 0.100 mol Mg would need 0.200 mol HCl, so **HCl is limiting**. n(H₂) = 0.100 ÷ 2 = 0.0500 mol → 1.20 dm³ at r.t.p.

### Water of crystallisation (Extended)

Heat a hydrated salt to constant mass (heat, cool, weigh, repeat until two readings are the same) so all water is removed.
- mass of water = mass of hydrated salt − mass of anhydrous salt
- moles of anhydrous salt and moles of water → ratio → x in MX·xH₂O.

### Paper 6 skills on this topic

- Heating a solid in a crucible: weigh empty crucible, crucible + sample, heat, cool, reweigh; **heat to constant mass** to make sure the reaction/dehydration is complete.
- Burning magnesium in a crucible with a lid: lift the lid occasionally to let oxygen in, but not so much that MgO smoke escapes (a source of error).
- Sources of error: incomplete reaction (not heated to constant mass), loss of solid, absorbing water from air while cooling. Improvement: heat to constant mass; cool in a desiccator (or with lid on).
- Use a balance reading to 0.01 g; record masses consistently to 2 d.p.

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Empirical formula | The simplest whole number ratio of the different atoms or ions in a compound |
| Molecular formula | The number and type of different atoms in one molecule |
| Limiting reactant | The reactant that is completely used up and so determines the amount of product formed |
| Hydrated substance | A substance that is chemically combined with water |
| Anhydrous substance | A substance containing no water |
| Water of crystallisation | Water molecules present in crystals, e.g. CuSO₄·5H₂O and CoCl₂·6H₂O |

## Worked examples

**1.** A compound contains 85.7% carbon and 14.3% hydrogen by mass. Its Mr is 56. Find (a) its empirical formula, (b) its molecular formula. [4]

- C: 85.7 ÷ 12 = 7.14; H: 14.3 ÷ 1 = 14.3 (1)
- ratio 1 : 2 → CH₂ (1)
- empirical formula mass = 14; 56 ÷ 14 = 4 (1)
- C₄H₈ (1)

**2.** 10.0 g of calcium carbonate is heated and produces 4.80 g of calcium oxide. CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. Calculate the percentage yield. [3]

- n(CaCO₃) = 10.0 ÷ 100 = 0.100 mol (1)
- theoretical mass CaO = 0.100 × 56 = 5.60 g (1)
- % yield = 4.80 ÷ 5.60 × 100 = 85.7% (1)

**3.** 2.50 g of hydrated magnesium sulfate, MgSO₄·xH₂O, is heated to constant mass. 1.22 g of anhydrous MgSO₄ remains. Calculate x. [3]

- n(MgSO₄) = 1.22 ÷ 120 = 0.0102 mol (1)
- mass of water = 1.28 g; n(H₂O) = 1.28 ÷ 18 = 0.0711 mol (1)
- ratio 0.0711 ÷ 0.0102 ≈ 7, so x = 7 (1)

**4.** A 5.00 g sample of impure calcium carbonate reacts with excess hydrochloric acid to give 1.08 dm³ of CO₂ at r.t.p. Calculate the percentage purity. [3]

- n(CO₂) = 1.08 ÷ 24 = 0.0450 mol = n(CaCO₃) (1)
- mass CaCO₃ = 0.0450 × 100 = 4.50 g (1)
- purity = 4.50 ÷ 5.00 × 100 = 90.0% (1)

## Common mistakes

- Dividing by the Mr of a diatomic element (O₂ = 32) instead of Ar (O = 16) in empirical formula work.
- Rounding ratios like 1.5 to 2 instead of doubling everything.
- Calculating percentage yield the wrong way round (theoretical ÷ actual) — yields over 100% are a warning sign.
- Using the reactant in excess to calculate product mass.
- Forgetting to subtract to find the mass of water or oxygen.
- Not explaining why heating to constant mass matters (to ensure all the water has been removed / reaction complete).

## Practice questions

Ar: H = 1, C = 12, O = 16, Mg = 24, Al = 27, S = 32, Fe = 56, Cu = 64, Zn = 65.

1. 2.16 g of aluminium combines with 1.92 g of oxygen. Find the empirical formula of the oxide. [3]
2. Compound Z is 40.0% C, 6.7% H and 53.3% O by mass. Its Mr is 180. Find its molecular formula. [3]
3. Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂. 32.0 g of iron(III) oxide produces 19.6 g of iron. Calculate the percentage yield. [3]
4. 6.50 g of zinc is added to 40.0 cm³ of 2.00 mol/dm³ sulfuric acid. Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂.
   (a) Show which reactant is in excess. (b) Calculate the mass of zinc sulfate formed. [4]
5. 0.300 g of impure magnesium reacts with excess acid to produce 240 cm³ of hydrogen at r.t.p. Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂. Calculate the percentage purity of the magnesium. [3]
6. 5.00 g of hydrated copper(II) sulfate, CuSO₄·xH₂O, is heated to constant mass, leaving 3.20 g of anhydrous copper(II) sulfate. Calculate x. [3]
7. Calculate the percentage by mass of iron in iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃. [1]

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Al: 2.16 ÷ 27 = 0.0800; O: 1.92 ÷ 16 = 0.120 (1); ratio 1 : 1.5 = 2 : 3 (1); Al₂O₃ (1).
2. Empirical formula CH₂O (as in the notes) (1); 180 ÷ 30 = 6 (1); C₆H₁₂O₆ (1).
3. n(Fe₂O₃) = 32.0 ÷ 160 = 0.200 mol; n(Fe) = 0.400 mol (1); theoretical = 0.400 × 56 = 22.4 g (1); 19.6 ÷ 22.4 × 100 = 87.5% (1).
4. (a) n(Zn) = 6.50 ÷ 65 = 0.100 mol; n(H₂SO₄) = 2.00 × 0.0400 = 0.0800 mol (1); 1 : 1 ratio so zinc is in excess (1). (b) n(ZnSO₄) = 0.0800 mol (1); Mr = 161; mass = 0.0800 × 161 = 12.9 g (1).

6. n(CuSO₄) = 3.20 ÷ 160 = 0.0200 mol (1); water = 1.80 g, n = 0.100 mol (1); x = 0.100 ÷ 0.0200 = 5 (1).
