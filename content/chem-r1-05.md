# The mole: limiting reactants and problems

## Notes

### Limiting reactant

The **limiting reactant** is the one that is completely used up; it decides how much product forms. The other reactant is **in excess** (some is left over).

1. Convert each reactant to moles.
2. Use the equation to work out how much of the other reactant each would need.
3. The one that runs out first is **limiting**; the other is **in excess**.
4. Use the **limiting** reactant to calculate the products.

**Example 1 (masses):** 5.60 g of iron is heated with 4.00 g of sulfur. Fe + S → FeS.
n(Fe) = 5.60 ÷ 56 = 0.100 mol; n(S) = 4.00 ÷ 32 = 0.125 mol. Ratio 1 : 1, so **iron is limiting**, sulfur is in excess (0.025 mol left).
Mass of FeS = 0.100 × 88 = 8.80 g.

**Example 2 (gases):** 30 cm³ of methane is mixed with 50 cm³ of oxygen and ignited. CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.
30 cm³ CH₄ would need 60 cm³ O₂ — only 50 cm³ is present, so **oxygen is limiting**. CO₂ formed = 50 ÷ 2 = 25 cm³; methane left over = 30 − 25 = 5 cm³.

### Multi-step reacting masses and gas volumes

Chain the steps: mass → moles → ratio → moles → mass or volume.

Example: what volume of hydrogen at r.t.p. is formed when 1.30 g of zinc reacts with excess acid? Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂.
n(Zn) = 1.30 ÷ 65 = 0.0200 mol → n(H₂) = 0.0200 mol → V = 0.0200 × 24 = 0.480 dm³ = 480 cm³.

### Core: simple proportion

If 12 g of magnesium gives 20 g of magnesium oxide, then 3 g gives 5 g (divide both by 4). Doubling the mass of the limiting reactant doubles the mass of product.

### Hydrated and anhydrous substances

- **Hydrated substance:** a substance that is chemically combined with water, e.g. blue hydrated copper(II) sulfate, CuSO₄·5H₂O.
- **Anhydrous substance:** a substance containing no water, e.g. white anhydrous copper(II) sulfate, CuSO₄.
- Heating a hydrated salt drives off the water. Adding water to the anhydrous salt reverses the change (this is the chemical test for water — see the environment topic).

### Paper 6 skills on this topic

- **Mass-loss experiment:** a flask of acid and marble chips on a balance, with a cotton wool plug (lets CO₂ out but stops acid spray). The decrease in mass equals the mass of CO₂ given off.
- **Gas collection:** gas syringe (most precise) or upturned measuring cylinder over water. Read volumes at eye level.
- **Heating a solid in a crucible:** weigh empty crucible, crucible + sample, heat, cool, reweigh; **heat to constant mass** (heat, cool, weigh, repeat until two readings are the same) to make sure all the water has been removed or the reaction is complete.
- Sources of error: not heated to constant mass; solid lost (spitting); gas escaping before the bung is put in; anhydrous solid absorbing water from the air while cooling. Improvements: heat to constant mass; put the lid on while cooling; use a gas syringe; repeat and calculate a mean.

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Limiting reactant | The reactant that is completely used up and so determines the amount of product formed |
| In excess | More of a reactant is present than is needed to react with the limiting reactant |
| Hydrated substance | A substance that is chemically combined with water |
| Anhydrous substance | A substance containing no water |
| Molar gas volume | 24 dm³, the volume of one mole of any gas at r.t.p. |

## Worked examples

**1.** 6.50 g of zinc is added to a solution containing 3.65 g of hydrogen chloride. Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂.
(a) Show which reactant is limiting. (b) Calculate the volume of hydrogen formed at r.t.p. (c) Calculate the mass of zinc left over. [5]

- (a) n(Zn) = 6.50 ÷ 65 = 0.100 mol; n(HCl) = 3.65 ÷ 36.5 = 0.100 mol (1)
- 0.100 mol Zn would need 0.200 mol HCl, so HCl is limiting (1)
- (b) n(H₂) = 0.100 ÷ 2 = 0.0500 mol (1); V = 0.0500 × 24 = 1.20 dm³ (1)
- (c) Zn used = 0.0500 mol, so 0.0500 mol left = 0.0500 × 65 = 3.25 g (1)

**2.** 20 cm³ of hydrogen is mixed with 20 cm³ of oxygen and exploded. 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l). Which gas is in excess, and what volume of it remains? [2]

- 20 cm³ H₂ needs only 10 cm³ O₂, so oxygen is in excess (1)
- 20 − 10 = 10 cm³ of oxygen remains (1)

**3.** Calculate the mass of calcium oxide formed when 25.0 g of calcium carbonate is completely decomposed. CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ [3]

- n(CaCO₃) = 25.0 ÷ 100 = 0.250 mol (1)
- n(CaO) = 0.250 mol (1)
- mass = 0.250 × 56 = 14.0 g (1)

## Common mistakes

- Choosing the reactant with the smaller **mass** as limiting — compare **moles**, taking the equation ratio into account.
- Using the reactant in excess to calculate the product.
- Forgetting the ratio when it is not 1 : 1 (e.g. Zn + **2**HCl).
- Confusing cm³ and dm³ (24 dm³ = 24 000 cm³).
- Not explaining why heating to constant mass matters (to ensure all the water has been removed / the reaction is complete).
- Wasting revision time on empirical formula or percentage yield questions from 0620 books — they are not in 0654.

## Practice questions

Ar: H = 1, C = 12, O = 16, Mg = 24, S = 32, Cl = 35.5, Ca = 40, Fe = 56, Cu = 64, Zn = 65.

1. 2.40 g of magnesium is heated with 2.40 g of sulfur. Mg + S → MgS. (a) Show which reactant is in excess. (b) Calculate the mass of magnesium sulfide formed. [4]
2. 40 cm³ of carbon monoxide reacts with 30 cm³ of oxygen. 2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂. (a) Which gas is in excess? (b) What volume of carbon dioxide forms? [2]
3. Calculate the volume of hydrogen, in dm³ at r.t.p., formed when 0.720 g of magnesium reacts with excess dilute sulfuric acid. Mg + H₂SO₄ → MgSO₄ + H₂ [3]
4. Copper(II) oxide is reduced by hydrogen: CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O. Calculate the mass of copper formed from 4.00 g of copper(II) oxide. [3]
5. *(Core)* 64 g of copper reacts with oxygen to make 80 g of copper(II) oxide. What mass of copper(II) oxide is made from 16 g of copper? [1]
6. Blue crystals of copper(II) sulfate turn white when heated. (a) Name the white solid. (b) Use the words *hydrated* and *anhydrous* to explain the change. [3]
7. A student heats a hydrated salt in a crucible. Explain why the student should heat, cool and reweigh until the mass stays constant. [2]

1. (a) n(Mg) = 2.40 ÷ 24 = 0.100 mol; n(S) = 2.40 ÷ 32 = 0.0750 mol (1); 1 : 1 ratio so magnesium is in excess (1). (b) n(MgS) = 0.0750 mol (1); Mr MgS = 56; mass = 0.0750 × 56 = 4.20 g (1).

6. (a) Anhydrous copper(II) sulfate (1). (b) The blue crystals are hydrated — chemically combined with water (1); heating removes the water, leaving the anhydrous salt, which contains no water (1).
