# Electrochemistry

> **In the exam:** Electrolysis is a favourite chemistry context in 0654. **Paper 4** (Theory, 2 h, 120 marks across biology, chemistry and physics) often has an electrolysis part (typically 4–8 marks): naming products, observations, a cathode half-equation, the transfer of charge, or fuel cells. **Paper 2** (Multiple Choice, 45 min, 40 questions) usually has about one question (products at electrodes, identifying anode/cathode). **Paper 6** (Alternative to Practical, 1 h 30 min, 60 marks) may show an electrolysis apparatus and ask for observations, tests for the gases formed, or improvements.

## What you need to know

- Define electrolysis as the decomposition of an ionic compound, when molten or in aqueous solution, by the passage of an electric current.
- Identify in simple electrolytic cells: the anode as the positive electrode, the cathode as the negative electrode, the electrolyte as the molten or aqueous substance that undergoes electrolysis.
- Identify the products formed at the electrodes and describe the observations made during the electrolysis of: molten lead(II) bromide; concentrated aqueous sodium chloride; dilute sulfuric acid — using inert electrodes made of platinum or carbon/graphite.
- *Supplement:* describe the transfer of charge during electrolysis: (a) the movement of electrons in the external circuit, (b) the loss or gain of electrons at the electrodes, (c) the movement of ions in the electrolyte.
- *Supplement:* identify the products formed at the electrodes and describe the observations made during the electrolysis of aqueous copper(II) sulfate using carbon/graphite electrodes and when using copper electrodes.
- *Supplement:* state that metals or hydrogen are formed at the cathode and that non-metals (other than hydrogen) are formed at the anode.
- *Supplement:* predict the identity of the products at each electrode for the electrolysis of a binary compound in the molten state.
- *Supplement:* construct ionic half-equations for reactions at the cathode (showing gain of electrons as a reduction reaction).
- State that a hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell uses hydrogen and oxygen to produce electricity with water as the only chemical product.
- *Supplement:* describe the advantages and disadvantages of using hydrogen–oxygen fuel cells in comparison with gasoline/petrol engines in vehicles.

*Not in 0654 (separate Chemistry 0620 only):* electroplating; predicting products for dilute versus concentrated halide solutions in general; anode half-equations. Anode half-equations are shown below only to help you understand — the syllabus asks you to write **cathode** half-equations.

## Notes

### The cell

- **Anode** = positive electrode → attracts **anions** (negative ions) → **oxidation** (loss of electrons).
- **Cathode** = negative electrode → attracts **cations** (positive ions) → **reduction** (gain of electrons).
- Memory aid: **PANiC** — Positive Anode, Negative is Cathode. **OIL RIG**.
- Inert electrodes: carbon (graphite) or platinum — they do not react.
- Solid ionic compounds do not conduct: ions must be **free to move** (molten or aqueous).

### Transfer of charge (Supplement)

- **Electrons** flow through the external circuit (wires) from the anode to the cathode via the power supply.
- **Ions** move through the electrolyte: cations to the cathode, anions to the anode.
- At the electrodes, ions gain or lose electrons (are discharged).

### Molten binary compounds

Metal at the cathode, non-metal at the anode.

Molten lead(II) bromide, PbBr₂:
- Cathode: Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb — grey/silvery liquid metal forms (collects under the electrode)
- Anode: 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻ — red-brown fumes/vapour
- Heat in a fume cupboard (bromine is toxic).

Molten NaCl: Na at cathode, Cl₂ at anode. Molten ZnCl₂: Zn at cathode, Cl₂ at anode. Aluminium is extracted from its ore (bauxite) by electrolysis (see Metals).

### Aqueous solutions — why hydrogen or the metal?

Water provides H⁺ and OH⁻ ions as well as the ions of the salt. The syllabus names the solutions you must know (table below); this explains the pattern.

**At the cathode:**
- If the metal is **less reactive than hydrogen** (e.g. copper), the **metal** is deposited.
- Otherwise (e.g. Na, K, Mg, Ca, Al, Zn) **hydrogen** is produced: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂

**At the anode (inert electrodes):** concentrated sodium chloride gives **chlorine**; dilute sulfuric acid and copper(II) sulfate solution give **oxygen**.

### Electrolyses to know

| Electrolyte (inert electrodes) | Cathode | Anode | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Molten PbBr₂ | lead | bromine | red-brown fumes |
| Concentrated NaCl(aq) | hydrogen (bubbles) | chlorine (bubbles, pale yellow-green) | solution left is NaOH(aq) — becomes alkaline |
| Dilute H₂SO₄ | hydrogen | oxygen | volume H₂ : O₂ = 2 : 1 |
| CuSO₄(aq), carbon electrodes *(Supp)* | copper (pink-brown deposit) | oxygen (bubbles) | blue colour fades; solution becomes acidic (H₂SO₄ left) |
| CuSO₄(aq), copper electrodes *(Supp)* | copper deposited; cathode gains mass | copper anode dissolves: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻; anode loses mass | blue colour stays the same — used to purify copper |

Cathode half-equations to learn (Supplement) — reduction, gain of electrons:

| Electrolyte | Cathode half-equation |
|---|---|
| Molten PbBr₂ | Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb |
| Conc. NaCl(aq), dilute H₂SO₄ | 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ |
| CuSO₄(aq) | Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu |
| Molten NaCl | Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na |
| Molten Al₂O₃ | Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al |

For understanding only (not required in 0654): at the anode, ions **lose** electrons, e.g. 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻, and a copper anode dissolves, Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻.

Balance half-equations for **charge** with electrons: total charge on the left must equal total charge on the right.

### Uses linked to conductivity

- Copper is used in electrical wiring because of its good electrical conductivity.
- Aluminium is used in overhead electrical cables because of its low density and good electrical conductivity.

### Hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell

A fuel cell uses hydrogen and oxygen to produce electricity; the only chemical product is water. Overall: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.

| Advantages (vs petrol engine) | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Only product is water — no CO₂, CO, oxides of nitrogen or particulates at the point of use | Hydrogen is a flammable/explosive gas — difficult and dangerous to store (high pressure tanks) |
| No carbon dioxide → does not contribute to climate change when used | Hydrogen is often made from fossil fuels (methane), which releases CO₂, or by electrolysis needing electricity |
| More efficient conversion of chemical energy | Few hydrogen filling stations; fuel cells are expensive |

### Paper 6 skills on this topic

- Collecting gases at each electrode in inverted test-tubes; comparing volumes (H₂ : O₂ = 2 : 1).
- Testing the gases: H₂ "pops" with a lighted splint; O₂ relights a glowing splint; Cl₂ bleaches damp litmus paper.
- Finding the mass change of copper electrodes: wash the cathode, dry (e.g. with propanone or in a warm oven), weigh. Errors: copper falling off the cathode, electrode not dry.
- Safety: chlorine and bromine are toxic — fume cupboard; low voltage d.c. supply.

## Worked examples

**1.** Concentrated aqueous sodium chloride is electrolysed using carbon electrodes. (a) Name the products at each electrode. (b) Explain why sodium is not formed at the cathode. (c) Write the half-equation at the cathode. [5]

- (a) Cathode: hydrogen (1); anode: chlorine (1)
- (b) Sodium is more reactive than hydrogen (1), so hydrogen ions are discharged / reduced instead (1)
- (c) 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ (1)

**2.** Aqueous copper(II) sulfate is electrolysed using carbon electrodes. Describe two observations and explain the change in colour of the electrolyte. [4]

- Pink-brown solid forms on the cathode (1)
- Bubbles (of colourless gas) at the anode (1)
- Blue colour fades (1) because Cu²⁺ ions are removed from solution as copper is deposited (1)

**3.** Molten lead(II) bromide is electrolysed. Describe how electric charge is transferred through the circuit and the electrolyte. [3]

- Electrons move through the external circuit / wires (from the anode to the cathode) (1)
- Ions move through the molten electrolyte: Pb²⁺ to the cathode, Br⁻ to the anode (1)
- At the cathode Pb²⁺ ions gain electrons; at the anode Br⁻ ions lose electrons (1)

## Common mistakes

- Swapping anode and cathode, or saying oxidation happens at the cathode.
- Saying "chloride gas" or "chlorine ions are formed" — chloride **ions** are discharged to form chlorine **gas**.
- Saying electrons move through the electrolyte — **ions** move in the electrolyte; electrons move in the wires.
- Writing half-equations that don't balance for charge, e.g. Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + e⁻.
- Forgetting the colour change of CuSO₄(aq) with inert electrodes, or claiming it fades with copper electrodes (it doesn't).
- Fuel cells: stating "no pollution at all" without specifying no CO₂ at the point of use; forgetting that hydrogen production may use fossil fuels.

## Practice questions

1. Define electrolysis. [2]
2. Molten potassium iodide is electrolysed. Name the products at the anode and cathode and write the half-equation at the cathode. [3]
3. Dilute sulfuric acid is electrolysed with platinum electrodes. 30 cm³ of gas is collected at the cathode. Name both gases and state the volume of gas at the anode. [3]
4. Predict the product at each electrode when molten magnesium chloride is electrolysed. Write the cathode half-equation. [3]
5. Explain why copper is deposited at the cathode in the electrolysis of aqueous copper(II) sulfate, but hydrogen is formed in the electrolysis of aqueous zinc sulfate. [2]
6. Copper is purified using copper electrodes in aqueous copper(II) sulfate. State and explain what happens to the mass of each electrode. [4]
7. Give two advantages and two disadvantages of a hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell compared with a petrol engine. [4]

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Decomposition of an ionic compound when molten or in aqueous solution (1) by the passage of an electric current (1).
2. Anode: iodine (1). Cathode: potassium (1); K⁺ + e⁻ → K (1).
3. Cathode: hydrogen (1); anode: oxygen (1); 15 cm³ (1).
4. Cathode: magnesium (1); anode: chlorine (1); Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Mg (1).
5. Copper is less reactive than hydrogen so Cu²⁺ is discharged/reduced (1); zinc is more reactive than hydrogen so H⁺ is discharged instead (1).
6. Anode loses mass (1) as copper atoms form Cu²⁺ ions which go into solution / Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ (1). Cathode gains mass (1) as Cu²⁺ ions gain electrons and copper is deposited (1).
7. Advantages: only product is water; no CO₂ (or CO/NOx) produced at point of use; more efficient (any two). Disadvantages: hydrogen hard to store / explosive; hydrogen made from fossil fuels or using electricity; few refuelling stations; expensive (any two).
