# Energetics and rates of reaction

> **In the exam:** **Paper 4** (Theory, 2 h, 120 marks across biology, chemistry and physics) often has a rates part (graph interpretation and collision-theory explanations, typically 4–8 marks) and sometimes an energetics part (exothermic/endothermic, reaction pathway diagram; 2–4 marks). **Paper 2** (Multiple Choice, 45 min, 40 questions) usually has 1–2 questions on these topics. Rates experiments are one of the most common chemistry contexts in **Paper 6** (Alternative to Practical, 1 h 30 min, 60 marks): reading a gas syringe or stop-clock, plotting a graph, explaining and improving the method; temperature changes during reactions are another listed practical context.

## What you need to know

**Energetics**
- State that an exothermic reaction transfers thermal energy to the surroundings leading to an increase in the temperature of the surroundings.
- State that an endothermic reaction takes in thermal energy from the surroundings leading to a decrease in the temperature of the surroundings.
- *Supplement:* interpret reaction pathway diagrams showing exothermic and endothermic reactions.
- *Supplement:* state that the transfer of thermal energy during a reaction is called the enthalpy change, ΔH, of the reaction; ΔH is negative for exothermic reactions and positive for endothermic reactions.
- *Supplement:* define activation energy, Ea, as the minimum energy that colliding particles must have to react.
- *Supplement:* draw and label reaction pathway diagrams for exothermic and endothermic reactions using information provided, to include reactants, products, overall energy change of the reaction, ΔH, and activation energy, Ea.
- *Supplement:* state that bond breaking is an endothermic process and bond making is an exothermic process.

*Not in 0654:* calculating ΔH from bond energies (separate Chemistry 0620 only).

**Rates**
- Describe the effect on the rate of reaction of: changing the concentration of solutions; changing the pressure of gases; changing the surface area of solids; changing the temperature; adding or removing a catalyst.
- State that a catalyst increases the rate of a reaction and is unchanged at the end of a reaction.
- Describe practical methods for investigating the rate of a reaction including change in mass of a reactant or product and the formation of a gas.
- Interpret data, including graphs, from rate of reaction experiments.
- *Supplement:* explain the effect on the rate of reaction of each of these changes using collision theory.
- *Supplement:* state that a catalyst decreases the activation energy, Ea, of a reaction.
- *Supplement:* describe collision theory in terms of: number of particles per unit volume; frequency of collisions between particles; kinetic energy of particles; activation energy, Ea.

## Notes

### Exothermic and endothermic

| | Exothermic | Endothermic |
|---|---|---|
| Thermal energy | transferred **to** surroundings | taken **in** from surroundings |
| Temperature of surroundings | increases | decreases |
| ΔH | negative | positive |
| Examples | combustion, neutralisation, respiration, metals + acids | thermal decomposition, photosynthesis, dissolving ammonium nitrate, electrolysis |
| Pathway diagram | products **lower** than reactants | products **higher** than reactants |

### Reaction pathway diagrams (Supplement)

Axes: energy (y) against progress of reaction (x). Draw reactants and products as horizontal lines with formulae, a hump between them.
- **Ea**: arrow from the reactant level **up** to the top of the hump.
- **ΔH**: arrow from the reactant level to the product level (pointing down for exothermic, up for endothermic).
- A catalyst gives a **lower hump**; ΔH is unchanged.

Reading values from a diagram: Ea = (energy at the top of the hump) − (energy of reactants); ΔH = (energy of products) − (energy of reactants). Example: reactants at 50 kJ, peak at 200 kJ, products at 120 kJ → Ea = 150 kJ, ΔH = +70 kJ (endothermic).

### Bond breaking and bond making (Supplement)

- Breaking bonds **takes in** energy (endothermic).
- Making bonds **releases** energy (exothermic).
- If more energy is released making new bonds than is taken in breaking bonds, the reaction is exothermic overall (ΔH negative); if less, it is endothermic.

(0654 does not ask you to calculate ΔH from bond energy values.)

### Factors affecting rate and collision theory

Particles must **collide** with energy **at least equal to the activation energy** to react (a successful collision).

| Change | Effect on rate | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Increase concentration (solutions) | increases | more particles per unit volume → more frequent collisions |
| Increase pressure (gases) | increases | more particles per unit volume → more frequent collisions |
| Increase surface area (smaller pieces / powder) | increases | more particles exposed → more frequent collisions |
| Increase temperature | increases | particles have more kinetic energy, move faster → more frequent collisions **and a greater proportion of particles have energy ≥ Ea** → more frequent successful collisions |
| Add a catalyst | increases | *(Supp)* decreases the **activation energy**, so a greater proportion of collisions are successful |

Temperature has the biggest effect because it increases both collision frequency and the proportion of collisions with enough energy. (Link to biology: enzymes are biological catalysts; they are denatured at high temperatures.)

Risks: fine powders (flour, coal dust) in factories and mines can explode because of their huge surface area.

### Measuring rate

| Method | What you measure | Apparatus |
|---|---|---|
| Gas volume | volume of gas at intervals | gas syringe or upturned measuring cylinder/burette in water, stop-clock |
| Mass loss | decrease in mass as gas escapes | balance, cotton wool plug in the flask (lets gas out but stops acid spray) |
| Precipitate / colour change | time for a cross under the flask to disappear (e.g. sodium thiosulfate + hydrochloric acid) | stop-clock; rate ∝ 1/time |

**Interpreting graphs** (volume of gas or mass loss against time):
- **Steepest at the start** — rate is highest because concentration of reactants is highest.
- **Gradient decreases** as reactants are used up (fewer particles per unit volume, collisions less frequent).
- **Levels off** when one reactant (the limiting reactant) is used up — reaction has stopped.
- Faster reaction → steeper initial gradient; same final volume if the same amount of limiting reactant.
- If the amount of limiting reactant is halved, the final volume halves.
- Average rate = change in volume ÷ time (cm³/s). Rate at a point = gradient of a tangent drawn to the curve (a mathematical skill listed in the 0654 syllabus).

**Evaluating methods (Paper 6 skill):** gas syringes are more precise than upturned cylinders; some CO₂ dissolves in water (lower readings); gas escapes before the bung is inserted; the "disappearing cross" end point is subjective — use a light sensor or the same observer.

### Paper 6 skills on this topic

- Fair test: change one variable (independent), measure one (dependent), keep others constant (control variables: volume and concentration of acid, mass of solid, temperature, particle size).
- Table headings with units, e.g. "time / s", "volume of gas / cm³".
- Plotting: smooth curve through points, ignore anomalies; read values from graphs showing your working lines.
- Suggest improvements: repeat and take an average; use a gas syringe; use a water bath to control temperature; use a burette instead of a measuring cylinder for more accurate volumes.

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Exothermic reaction | Transfers thermal energy to the surroundings, leading to an increase in the temperature of the surroundings |
| Endothermic reaction | Takes in thermal energy from the surroundings, leading to a decrease in the temperature of the surroundings |
| Enthalpy change, ΔH | The transfer of thermal energy during a reaction (negative for exothermic, positive for endothermic) |
| Activation energy, Ea | The minimum energy that colliding particles must have to react |
| Catalyst | Increases the rate of a reaction and is unchanged at the end of the reaction |
| Collision theory | Particles must collide, with energy at least equal to the activation energy, to react |

## Worked examples

**1.** For a reaction, the energy of the reactants is 100 kJ, the top of the energy hump is 250 kJ and the energy of the products is 30 kJ. (a) Calculate Ea and ΔH. (b) State whether the reaction is exothermic or endothermic, and explain using bond breaking and bond making. [4]

- (a) Ea = 250 − 100 = 150 kJ (1); ΔH = 30 − 100 = −70 kJ (1) (sign required)
- (b) Exothermic (1); more energy is released making bonds (exothermic) than is taken in breaking bonds (endothermic) (1)

**2.** Explain, using collision theory, why increasing the temperature increases the rate of reaction between magnesium and hydrochloric acid. [3]

- Particles have more kinetic energy / move faster (1)
- Collide more frequently (1)
- A greater proportion of particles / collisions have energy greater than or equal to the activation energy (1)

**3.** A student adds 1.0 g of calcium carbonate lumps to excess hydrochloric acid and measures the volume of CO₂ every 10 s. After 60 s, 48 cm³ has been collected. (a) Calculate the average rate over 60 s. (b) The experiment is repeated with 1.0 g of calcium carbonate powder. State how the graph would differ. [3]

- (a) 48 ÷ 60 = 0.80 cm³/s (1)
- (b) Steeper initial gradient / levels off sooner (1); same final volume (1)

## Common mistakes

- Saying particles collide "more" — write "**more frequently**" or "more collisions **per unit time**".
- Saying a catalyst "gives particles more energy" — it **lowers the activation energy** (alternative pathway).
- Saying increased concentration makes particles "move faster" — that is temperature.
- Giving ΔH without a sign, or with the wrong sign (exothermic = negative).
- Drawing the ΔH arrow from the top of the hump, or Ea from the products.
- Saying a reaction "stops because the catalyst is used up" or "because the acid is used up" when acid was in excess — identify the **limiting** reactant.
- Calling an exothermic reaction one that "gives out heat" without referring to the surroundings' temperature rising.

## Practice questions

1. Ammonium nitrate dissolving in water lowers the temperature from 21 °C to 14 °C. State whether this is exothermic or endothermic and give the sign of ΔH. [2]
2. Explain, in terms of bond breaking and bond making, why the combustion of methane is exothermic. [2]
3. On a reaction pathway diagram, the reactants are at 40 kJ, the peak is at 180 kJ and the products are at 90 kJ. Calculate Ea and ΔH, and state whether the reaction is exothermic or endothermic. [3]
4. Sketch and label a reaction pathway diagram for an exothermic reaction, showing Ea and ΔH and the effect of a catalyst. [4]
5. Explain why powdered zinc reacts faster with dilute sulfuric acid than a single lump of zinc of the same mass. [2]
6. Explain why the rate of reaction decreases as the reaction proceeds. [2]
7. Describe how you could use a balance to follow the rate of reaction between marble chips and hydrochloric acid. [4]

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Endothermic (1); ΔH positive (1).
2. Bond breaking is endothermic and bond making is exothermic (1); more energy is released when the bonds in CO₂ and H₂O are made than is taken in to break the bonds in CH₄ and O₂ (1).
3. Ea = 180 − 40 = 140 kJ (1); ΔH = 90 − 40 = +50 kJ (1); endothermic (1).
4. Axes labelled energy and progress of reaction (1); products below reactants (1); Ea from reactants to peak and ΔH from reactants to products (downward) (1); catalysed pathway with lower peak (1).
5. Powder has larger surface area (1); more frequent collisions between acid particles and zinc (1).
6. Reactants are used up / concentration decreases (1); so fewer particles per unit volume and collisions are less frequent (1).
7. Flask with acid on a balance, add marble chips and start timer (1); cotton wool plug in neck (1); record mass at regular intervals (1); mass decreases as CO₂ escapes; plot mass loss against time (1).
