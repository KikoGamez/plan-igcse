# Acids, bases and salts

> **In the exam:** Paper 4 almost always has a salt preparation question (describe a method, 4–6 marks) and questions on acid/base reactions and pH. Ion and gas tests appear in Paper 2 (2–3 questions every series) and are **central to Paper 6** (one long question is usually a series of tests on unknown substances, with observations to record or interpret). Learn the test tables word for word — the syllabus notes on qualitative analysis use exactly this wording.

## What you need to know

- Describe the typical properties of acids: reactions with metals, bases and carbonates; effect on litmus and methyl orange.
- State that bases are oxides or hydroxides of metals and that alkalis are soluble bases; describe the properties of bases: reaction with acids and ammonium salts; effect on litmus, thymolphthalein and methyl orange.
- State that aqueous solutions of acids contain H⁺ ions and aqueous solutions of alkalis contain OH⁻ ions; describe neutrality, relative acidity and alkalinity in terms of pH (whole numbers only) measured using universal indicator.
- *Extended:* define acids as proton donors and bases as proton acceptors; define strong acids as completely dissociated in aqueous solution and weak acids as partially dissociated (e.g. hydrochloric acid and ethanoic acid); describe the effect of H⁺ concentration on pH.
- Classify oxides as acidic (including SO₂ and CO₂) or basic (including CuO and CaO), related to metallic and non-metallic character. *Extended:* amphoteric oxides (Al₂O₃ and ZnO) react with acids and with bases to form a salt and water.
- Describe the preparation, separation and purification of soluble salts by reaction of an acid with an alkali, a metal, an insoluble base or a carbonate.
- Describe the general solubility rules for salts.
- *Extended:* describe the preparation of insoluble salts by precipitation; define hydrated, anhydrous and water of crystallisation.
- Describe tests to identify the aqueous cations, anions and gases listed in the syllabus (Topic 12.5), and flame tests for metal ions.

## Notes

### Acids and bases

| | Acids | Alkalis |
|---|---|---|
| Ions in solution | H⁺(aq) | OH⁻(aq) |
| pH | below 7 | above 7 |
| Litmus | red | blue |
| Methyl orange | red | yellow |
| Thymolphthalein | colourless | blue |
| Universal indicator | red (pH 1–2), orange/yellow (pH 3–6) | blue (pH 8–11), purple (pH 12–14) |

Neutral = pH 7 (universal indicator green).

**Reactions of acids** (learn the general equations):
- acid + metal → salt + hydrogen   Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂
- acid + base → salt + water   CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O
- acid + alkali → salt + water   NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O (neutralisation: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O)
- acid + carbonate → salt + carbon dioxide + water   CaCO₃ + 2HNO₃ → Ca(NO₃)₂ + CO₂ + H₂O

**Bases + ammonium salts:** an alkali warmed with an ammonium salt gives ammonia: NH₄Cl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O + NH₃.

### Proton donors and strong/weak acids (Extended)

- Acid = **proton donor** (H⁺ donor). Base = **proton acceptor**.
- **Strong acid**: completely dissociated in aqueous solution: HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻.
- **Weak acid**: partially dissociated: CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺.
- The higher the H⁺ concentration, the lower the pH. Same concentration: strong acid has lower pH, reacts faster (more H⁺ ions), conducts better.
- Strong ≠ concentrated. Concentration is how much acid per dm³; strength is how much it dissociates.

### Oxides

| Type | Examples | Reacts with | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acidic | CO₂, SO₂ (non-metal oxides) | bases/alkalis | non-metallic |
| Basic | CuO, CaO, MgO (metal oxides) | acids | metallic |
| Amphoteric *(Ext)* | Al₂O₃, ZnO | **both** acids and bases | |

ZnO + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂O and ZnO + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + H₂O (the second equation would be given if needed).

### Solubility rules

| Soluble | Insoluble (exceptions) |
|---|---|
| All sodium, potassium and ammonium salts | — |
| All nitrates | — |
| Chlorides, bromides, iodides | silver and lead(II) |
| Sulfates | barium, calcium and lead(II) |
| Sodium, potassium, ammonium carbonates | all other carbonates |
| Sodium, potassium, ammonium hydroxides | most other hydroxides (calcium hydroxide is partially soluble) |

### Preparing salts — choose the method

1. **Is the salt soluble?** No → **precipitation** (Extended).
2. Yes → is the salt a sodium, potassium or ammonium salt? Yes → **titration** (acid + alkali or soluble carbonate).
3. No → **excess insoluble solid** (metal, insoluble base or insoluble carbonate) + acid.

**Method A: acid + excess insoluble solid** (e.g. copper(II) sulfate from copper(II) oxide and dilute sulfuric acid)
1. Warm the acid; add the solid a little at a time with stirring until **in excess** (no more dissolves / solid remains / no more fizzing for carbonates).
2. **Filter** to remove the excess solid.
3. Heat the filtrate to **evaporate some of the water** until the solution is saturated (crystals form on a cold glass rod dipped in it).
4. Leave to **cool and crystallise**.
5. Filter off the crystals, wash with a little cold distilled water, **dry** between filter papers (or in a low-temperature oven).

Why excess? To make sure all the acid is used up, so the salt is not contaminated with acid.

**Method B: titration** (e.g. sodium chloride from NaOH and HCl)
1. Pipette 25.0 cm³ of alkali into a conical flask, add a few drops of indicator (methyl orange or thymolphthalein).
2. Add acid from a burette until the indicator just changes colour; record the volume.
3. **Repeat without indicator** using the same volumes (so the salt is not contaminated with indicator).
4. Evaporate and crystallise as above.

**Method C: precipitation** (Extended) (e.g. barium sulfate)
1. Mix solutions of two soluble salts: BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq).
2. Filter to collect the precipitate.
3. Wash the residue with distilled water (removes soluble impurities).
4. Dry in a warm oven or between filter papers.

### Tests for aqueous cations

| Cation | Effect of aqueous sodium hydroxide | Effect of aqueous ammonia |
|---|---|---|
| Aluminium, Al³⁺ | white ppt., soluble in excess giving a colourless solution | white ppt., insoluble in excess |
| Ammonium, NH₄⁺ | ammonia produced on warming | — |
| Calcium, Ca²⁺ | white ppt., insoluble in excess | no ppt. or very slight white ppt. |
| Chromium(III), Cr³⁺ | green ppt., soluble in excess | green ppt., insoluble in excess |
| Copper(II), Cu²⁺ | light blue ppt., insoluble in excess | light blue ppt., soluble in excess, giving a dark blue solution |
| Iron(II), Fe²⁺ | green ppt., insoluble in excess | green ppt., insoluble in excess |
| Iron(III), Fe³⁺ | red-brown ppt., insoluble in excess | red-brown ppt., insoluble in excess |
| Zinc, Zn²⁺ | white ppt., soluble in excess, giving a colourless solution | white ppt., soluble in excess, giving a colourless solution |

How to tell the white precipitates apart: Al³⁺ and Zn²⁺ both dissolve in excess NaOH; only **Zn²⁺** dissolves in excess NH₃. Ca²⁺ is insoluble in excess NaOH and gives little or no precipitate with NH₃.

### Tests for anions

| Anion | Test | Test result |
|---|---|---|
| Carbonate, CO₃²⁻ | add dilute acid, then test for carbon dioxide gas | effervescence, carbon dioxide produced |
| Chloride, Cl⁻ | acidify with dilute nitric acid, then add aqueous silver nitrate | white ppt. |
| Bromide, Br⁻ | acidify with dilute nitric acid, then add aqueous silver nitrate | cream ppt. |
| Iodide, I⁻ | acidify with dilute nitric acid, then add aqueous silver nitrate | yellow ppt. |
| Nitrate, NO₃⁻ | add aqueous sodium hydroxide, then aluminium foil; warm carefully | ammonia produced |
| Sulfate, SO₄²⁻ | acidify with dilute nitric acid, then add aqueous barium nitrate | white ppt. |
| Sulfite, SO₃²⁻ | add a small volume of acidified aqueous potassium manganate(VII) | the acidified aqueous potassium manganate(VII) changes colour from purple to colourless |

Why acidify with nitric acid? To remove carbonate ions, which would also give a precipitate. (Don't use hydrochloric acid for the halide test — it adds Cl⁻; don't use sulfuric acid for the sulfate test — it adds SO₄²⁻.)

### Tests for gases

| Gas | Test and test result |
|---|---|
| Ammonia, NH₃ | turns damp red litmus paper blue |
| Carbon dioxide, CO₂ | turns limewater milky |
| Chlorine, Cl₂ | bleaches damp litmus paper |
| Hydrogen, H₂ | "pops" with a lighted splint |
| Oxygen, O₂ | relights a glowing splint |
| Sulfur dioxide, SO₂ | turns acidified aqueous potassium manganate(VII) from purple to colourless |

### Flame tests for metal ions

| Metal ion | Flame colour |
|---|---|
| Lithium, Li⁺ | red |
| Sodium, Na⁺ | yellow |
| Potassium, K⁺ | lilac |
| Calcium, Ca²⁺ | orange-red |
| Barium, Ba²⁺ | light green |
| Copper(II), Cu²⁺ | blue-green |

Method: dip a clean (acid-washed) platinum or nichrome wire loop in concentrated hydrochloric acid, then into the solid, and hold it in a blue (roaring) Bunsen flame.

### Paper 6 skills on this topic

- Record observations exactly: "white precipitate", "precipitate dissolves in excess to give a colourless solution", "effervescence; gas turns limewater milky". "No change" is a valid observation — write it, never leave a blank.
- When asked to identify, name the ion **and** give the evidence.
- Planning a salt preparation: name reagents, quantities, apparatus, the step "add until in excess", and how to obtain dry crystals.
- Testing for water: anhydrous copper(II) sulfate (white → blue) or anhydrous cobalt(II) chloride (blue → pink); purity: boiling point 100 °C / melting point 0 °C.

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Acid (Ext) | A proton donor |
| Base (Ext) | A proton acceptor |
| Alkali | A soluble base |
| Strong acid (Ext) | An acid that is completely dissociated in aqueous solution |
| Weak acid (Ext) | An acid that is partially dissociated in aqueous solution |
| Amphoteric oxide (Ext) | An oxide that reacts with acids and with bases to produce a salt and water |
| Neutralisation | H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l) |
| Hydrated substance | A substance that is chemically combined with water |
| Anhydrous substance | A substance containing no water |
| Water of crystallisation | Water molecules present in crystals, e.g. CuSO₄·5H₂O and CoCl₂·6H₂O |

## Worked examples

**1.** Describe how to prepare pure, dry crystals of zinc sulfate from zinc carbonate and dilute sulfuric acid. [5]

- Add zinc carbonate to (warm) dilute sulfuric acid until in excess / no more effervescence (1)
- Filter to remove excess zinc carbonate (1)
- Heat the filtrate to evaporate some water / until saturated (1)
- Leave to cool so crystals form (1)
- Filter/remove crystals and dry between filter papers (1)

**2.** Solution X gives a light blue precipitate with aqueous sodium hydroxide. When aqueous barium nitrate is added to acidified X, a white precipitate forms. Identify X. [2]

- Copper(II) (1) sulfate (1) — CuSO₄

**3.** Ethanoic acid and hydrochloric acid of the same concentration are tested with universal indicator. Explain why the pH of hydrochloric acid is lower. [3]

- Hydrochloric acid is a strong acid; it is completely dissociated (1)
- Ethanoic acid is weak / partially dissociated (1)
- So hydrochloric acid has a higher concentration of H⁺ ions (1)

## Common mistakes

- Writing "clear" instead of "colourless" (copper sulfate solution is clear but blue).
- Forgetting to say "in excess" in salt preparations, or filtering in the titration method (no solid to remove).
- Heating to dryness instead of evaporating until saturated and crystallising.
- Mixing up the NaOH and NH₃ results for zinc and aluminium.
- Gas tests: "limewater goes cloudy" is accepted as milky; but "a splint pops" or "glowing splint pops" are wrong — a **lighted** splint pops with hydrogen; a **glowing** splint relights with oxygen.
- Using "dry" litmus — gas tests need **damp** litmus paper.
- For the nitrate test, forgetting aluminium foil or the warming step; for sulfite, forgetting that the manganate(VII) must be **acidified**.

## Practice questions

2. Write a balanced equation for the reaction between calcium carbonate and dilute hydrochloric acid. [2]

4. Describe a test to show that a solution contains iodide ions. Give the result. [3]
5. A solid gives a lilac flame. When it is warmed with aqueous sodium hydroxide and aluminium foil, a gas that turns damp red litmus blue is given off. Identify the solid. [2]

7. Zinc oxide is amphoteric. Explain what this means. [2]
8. Describe a test for sulfur dioxide and its result. [2]
