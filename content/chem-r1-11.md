# Metals and the environment

> **In the exam:** This is a large, fact-heavy area. Paper 4 nearly always has a metals question (reactivity, blast furnace or aluminium extraction, alloys, rusting; 6–12 marks) and often an environment part (pollutants, water treatment, greenhouse effect; 3–6 marks). Paper 2 has 4–6 questions across these topics. Paper 6 often sets up rusting experiments or reactivity investigations (temperature rise in displacement, rate of bubbles with acid).

## What you need to know

**Metals**
- Compare the general physical properties of metals and non-metals; describe the general chemical properties of metals (reactions with dilute acids, cold water/steam, oxygen).
- Describe the uses of metals related to their properties: aluminium (aircraft — low density; food containers — resistance to corrosion); copper (electrical wiring — good conductor, ductile).
- Describe an alloy as a mixture of a metal with other elements, including brass (copper and zinc) and stainless steel (iron with other elements such as chromium, nickel and carbon); state that alloys can be harder and stronger than pure metals and are more useful. *Extended:* explain in terms of structure how alloying changes properties, including diagrams.
- State the order of the reactivity series: potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium, carbon, zinc, iron, hydrogen, copper, silver, gold.
- Describe the reactions of potassium, sodium and calcium with cold water; magnesium with steam; magnesium, zinc, iron, copper, silver and gold with dilute hydrochloric acid, and explain these in terms of position relative to hydrogen.
- Deduce an order of reactivity from experimental results, including displacement reactions. *Extended:* describe reactivity in terms of the tendency of a metal to form positive ions; explain the apparent unreactivity of aluminium in terms of its oxide layer.
- State the conditions required for the rusting of iron and steel (oxygen and water); state that rust is hydrated iron(III) oxide; describe barrier methods (painting, greasing, coating with plastic). *Extended:* describe galvanising and explain sacrificial protection in terms of the reactivity series and in terms of electron loss.
- Describe the ease of obtaining metals from their ores, related to the reactivity series; describe the extraction of iron from hematite in the blast furnace; state that aluminium is extracted from bauxite by electrolysis. *Extended:* symbol equations for the blast furnace; extraction of aluminium from purified bauxite/aluminium oxide including the role of cryolite, why the carbon anodes are replaced, and electrode half-equations.

**Chemistry of the environment**
- Describe chemical tests for water; describe how to test for the purity of water using melting and boiling points; explain that distilled water is used in practical chemistry rather than tap water because it contains fewer chemical impurities.
- State that water from natural sources may contain substances (dissolved oxygen, metal compounds, plastics, sewage, harmful microbes, nitrates and phosphates from fertilisers and detergents) and describe whether these are beneficial or harmful.
- Describe the treatment of the domestic water supply: sedimentation and filtration, use of carbon, chlorination.
- State that elements needed by plants are N, P and K and describe the use of NPK fertilisers.
- State the composition of clean, dry air.
- State the source of each pollutant (carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide and particulates, methane, oxides of nitrogen, sulfur dioxide) and its adverse effects.
- State and explain strategies to reduce the effects of climate change and acid rain. *Extended:* explain how greenhouse gases cause global warming; explain how oxides of nitrogen form in car engines and are removed by catalytic converters.
- Describe photosynthesis as the reaction between carbon dioxide and water to produce glucose and oxygen in the presence of chlorophyll and using energy from light; *Extended:* state the symbol equation.

## Notes

### Properties of metals and non-metals

| Metals | Non-metals |
|---|---|
| good conductors of heat and electricity | poor conductors (except graphite) |
| malleable and ductile | brittle when solid |
| high melting and boiling points (mostly) | low melting and boiling points (mostly) |
| shiny; usually high density | dull |
| basic oxides | acidic oxides |

Chemical: metals react with oxygen to form oxides (2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO), with acids to form salt + hydrogen, and (reactive ones) with water.

### Alloys

An **alloy** is a mixture of a metal with other elements. Brass = copper + zinc. Stainless steel = iron + chromium, nickel and carbon (resistant to rusting — cutlery, sinks).

*Extended:* in a pure metal, atoms/ions are the same size in regular layers that slide easily. In an alloy, **different-sized atoms** disrupt the regular layers, so layers **cannot slide** over each other as easily → harder and stronger. (Diagram: regular grid of equal circles, with a few larger or smaller circles breaking up the rows.)

### Reactivity series

K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > (C) > Zn > Fe > (H) > Cu > Ag > Au

| Metal | Cold water | Steam | Dilute HCl |
|---|---|---|---|
| K, Na, Ca | react: metal hydroxide + hydrogen (K most vigorous) | — | dangerous (violent) |
| Mg | very slow | reacts: MgO + H₂ (bright white glow) | rapid fizzing |
| Zn, Fe | no | — | fizzing (Zn faster than Fe) |
| Cu, Ag, Au | no | no | **no reaction** (below hydrogen) |

Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂   Mg + H₂O(g) → MgO + H₂   Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂

Metals **above hydrogen** displace hydrogen from acids; those below do not.

**Displacement:** a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from a solution of its salt (or from its oxide when heated). Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu (grey iron gets a pink-brown coating; blue solution fades to pale green). The **bigger the gap** in reactivity, the bigger the temperature rise.

*Extended:* more reactive metals have a **greater tendency to form positive ions** (lose electrons). Aluminium seems less reactive than expected because it has a thin, unreactive, protective **oxide layer** (Al₂O₃).

### Rusting and its prevention

Rusting needs **both oxygen and water**. Rust = **hydrated iron(III) oxide**. Salt water speeds up rusting.

Classic experiment: three tubes of iron nails — (1) air + water → rusts; (2) boiled water (removes dissolved air) under a layer of oil → no rust; (3) dry air with anhydrous calcium chloride (drying agent) → no rust.

- **Barrier methods:** painting, greasing/oiling, coating with plastic — stop oxygen and water reaching the iron.
- **Galvanising** *(Ext)*: coating iron with **zinc**. Acts as a barrier, and if scratched, the zinc still protects.
- **Sacrificial protection** *(Ext)*: a more reactive metal (zinc, magnesium) is attached; it **loses electrons more readily** than iron, so it is oxidised (corrodes) instead of the iron. Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻. Used on ship hulls and pipelines.

### Extraction of metals

- Metals **below carbon** (Zn, Fe, Cu…) can be extracted by **reduction with carbon** (heating the oxide with carbon).
- Metals **above carbon** (K → Al) must be extracted by **electrolysis** (more expensive, needs lots of electricity).
- Unreactive metals (Au, Ag) are found uncombined (native).

**Iron from hematite (blast furnace)** — raw materials: hematite (Fe₂O₃), coke (carbon), limestone (CaCO₃), hot air.

| Stage | Equation |
|---|---|
| Coke burns, provides heat | C + O₂ → CO₂ |
| Carbon monoxide forms | CO₂ + C → 2CO |
| Iron(III) oxide reduced by CO | Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂ |
| Limestone decomposes | CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ |
| Slag forms (removes sand impurity) | CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃ |

Molten iron and molten slag (calcium silicate) are tapped off at the bottom; slag floats on iron.

**Aluminium from bauxite** (Extended detail):
- Bauxite is purified to aluminium oxide, Al₂O₃.
- Al₂O₃ is dissolved in **molten cryolite** — this **lowers the operating temperature** (saves energy/cost) and the mixture is a better conductor.
- Cathode: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al. Anode: 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻.
- **Carbon anodes must be replaced** because oxygen produced at the anode reacts with the hot carbon to form carbon dioxide, so the anodes burn away.

### Water

- Tests for water: **anhydrous copper(II) sulfate** white → blue; **anhydrous cobalt(II) chloride** blue → pink. Pure water: boils at 100 °C, melts at 0 °C.
- Distilled water is used in practical chemistry because it contains **fewer chemical impurities** than tap water.

| Substance in natural water | Beneficial or harmful |
|---|---|
| Dissolved oxygen | beneficial — needed by aquatic life |
| Some metal compounds | some provide essential minerals; some are toxic |
| Plastics | harm aquatic life |
| Sewage | contains harmful microbes that cause disease |
| Nitrates and phosphates (fertilisers, detergents) | lead to deoxygenation of water and damage to aquatic life |

**Water treatment:** sedimentation and filtration (remove solids) → carbon (removes tastes and odours) → chlorination (kills microbes).

**Fertilisers:** NPK fertilisers supply nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium for plant growth; ammonium salts and nitrates are used as fertilisers.

### Air and pollutants

Clean, dry air: approximately **78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen**, the remainder a mixture of noble gases (mainly argon) and carbon dioxide.

| Pollutant | Source | Adverse effect |
|---|---|---|
| Carbon dioxide | complete combustion of carbon-containing fuels | higher levels lead to increased global warming, which leads to climate change |
| Carbon monoxide | incomplete combustion of carbon-containing fuels | toxic gas |
| Particulates (soot) | incomplete combustion of carbon-containing fuels | increased risk of respiratory problems and cancer |
| Methane | decomposition of vegetation; waste gases from digestion in animals | higher levels lead to increased global warming → climate change |
| Oxides of nitrogen | car engines | acid rain, photochemical smog, respiratory problems |
| Sulfur dioxide | combustion of fossil fuels containing sulfur compounds | acid rain |

**Greenhouse effect (Extended):** the Sun's radiation passes through the atmosphere and warms the Earth; the Earth's surface **emits thermal (infrared) energy**; greenhouse gases (CO₂, CH₄) **absorb** this thermal energy and **re-emit** it in all directions, including back to the Earth, so **less thermal energy is lost to space** → global warming.

**Strategies — climate change:** planting trees; reducing livestock farming; decreasing use of fossil fuels; increasing use of hydrogen and renewable energy (wind, solar).
**Strategies — acid rain:** catalytic converters in vehicles; low-sulfur fuels; flue gas desulfurisation using calcium oxide.

**Oxides of nitrogen and catalytic converters (Extended):** at the high temperature in a car engine, nitrogen and oxygen from the air react: N₂ + O₂ → 2NO (then 2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂). Catalytic converters (containing transition metals such as platinum/rhodium) remove them: **2NO + 2CO → N₂ + 2CO₂**; they also oxidise CO: 2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂.

**Photosynthesis:** carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (chlorophyll, light energy). *Extended:* 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂.

### Paper 6 skills on this topic

- Rusting experiment: identify control variables (same type and size of nail, same time); explain the purpose of boiled water, oil layer and drying agent.
- Displacement/temperature-rise experiments: use a polystyrene cup (insulation), same volume and concentration of solution, same mass of powdered metal, stir, record the maximum temperature.
- Comparing reactivity by rate of bubbling with acid: same surface area and mass of metal.

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Alloy | A mixture of a metal with other elements |
| Rust | Hydrated iron(III) oxide |
| Sacrificial protection (Ext) | A more reactive metal is oxidised (loses electrons) in preference to iron, protecting it |
| Galvanising (Ext) | Coating iron or steel with zinc |
| Ore | A rock containing a metal compound from which the metal can be extracted |
| Slag | Calcium silicate, CaSiO₃, formed in the blast furnace from impurities |

## Worked examples

**1.** Explain why zinc blocks attached to a steel ship's hull stop the hull from rusting. [3]

- Zinc is more reactive than iron (1)
- Zinc loses electrons / is oxidised more readily than iron (1)
- so zinc corrodes instead of iron (sacrificial protection) (1)

**2.** In the extraction of aluminium, explain (a) why the aluminium oxide is dissolved in molten cryolite, (b) why the anodes have to be replaced. [4]

- (a) Lowers the operating temperature / melting point (1); saves energy / reduces cost (1)
- (b) Oxygen is produced at the anode (1) and reacts with the carbon to form carbon dioxide (so the anode burns away) (1)

**3.** Explain how carbon dioxide in the atmosphere causes global warming. [3]

- The Earth's surface emits thermal energy (1)
- CO₂ absorbs the thermal energy and re-emits it (back towards the Earth) (1)
- so less thermal energy is lost to space (1)

## Common mistakes

- "Rusting needs air **or** water" — it needs **both** oxygen **and** water.
- Saying galvanising works because zinc "doesn't rust" — explain sacrificial protection (zinc is more reactive / loses electrons).
- In the blast furnace, saying carbon reduces iron oxide directly — the main reducing agent is **carbon monoxide**. Limestone's job is to **remove impurities** (forms slag), not to reduce the ore.
- Mixing pollutant sources: CO comes from **incomplete** combustion; NOx from **car engines** (N₂ and O₂ reacting at high temperature), not from nitrogen in the fuel.
- Confusing acid rain with global warming; or ozone depletion (not on the syllabus) with the greenhouse effect.
- Saying chlorine "cleans" water — it **kills microbes**.
- Writing "carbon reduces aluminium oxide" — aluminium is above carbon, so electrolysis is needed.
