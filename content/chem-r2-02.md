# Past paper · Paper 4 timed

> **Goal:** Sit one complete Paper 4 (Theory, Extended) in exactly 1 hour 15 minutes under exam conditions, so you can mark it properly in the next session.

## Before you start

- **Paper:** a Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry 0620 **Paper 4 (Theory, Extended)** from a recent series — ideally 2024 or 2025, May/June or Oct/Nov — variant 41, 42 or 43. Choose one you have **not** seen. Don't look at the mark scheme or examiner report yet; you will use them in the next session ("Mark Paper 4").
- **Timer** set to 1 h 15 min. This session is longer than usual — block out 80 minutes.
- **Equipment:** black pen, pencil (for diagrams and graphs), ruler, eraser, **calculator** (allowed). The Periodic Table and the molar gas volume (24 dm³ at r.t.p.) are on the paper.
- Quiet room, phone in another room, no notes.

## Exam format reminder

- 1 hour 15 minutes, **80 marks**, 50% of the qualification — the most important paper.
- About **6–7 structured questions**, each 8–16 marks, answered in the spaces on the paper. Questions mix topics and often build from easy recall to harder explanation or calculation within the same question.
- **Timing: about 1 minute per mark.** A 12-mark question should take about 11–12 minutes. Aim to be roughly halfway through the marks at 37 minutes.
- The number of marks and the lines given show how much to write.

## How to do it

1. **0–2 min:** flick through the paper; note the mark total of each question.
2. **2–72 min:** answer in order. If you are stuck for more than a minute on a part, write something sensible, mark it with a star and move on.
3. **72–75 min:** go back to starred parts; check every calculation has units and every equation is balanced.
4. **After the timer:** stop immediately. Note in the margin anything you did after time (it doesn't count). Write how long each question took if you can, then put the paper away until the marking session.

## Exam technique for this paper

**Command words**
- **State / Give / Name:** short answer, no explanation. "Name" means a name, not a formula (unless asked).
- **Describe:** say what happens or what you would see — observations, steps, trends.
- **Explain:** give reasons — use "because" and link to particles, bonding, electrons or collisions.
- **Suggest:** apply knowledge to an unfamiliar context; more than one answer may be credited.
- **Calculate:** show working; final answer with unit; usually 3 significant figures.
- **Deduce:** draw a conclusion from given information.
- **Predict:** give an expected result without needing to explain.

**How marks are awarded**
- One mark per **creditable point**; for a 3-mark "explain" you need three distinct points.
- **Error carried forward (ecf):** in multi-step calculations, a mistake early on is penalised once if later steps are correct — so always show every step.
- Contradictions cancel marks: "white precipitate, which may be cream" scores zero.
- Equations: correct formulae first, then balancing; state symbols only when asked.
- Dot-and-cross diagrams: correct number of electrons, charges and brackets for ions.

**What examiners repeatedly report**
- Answers to "explain" questions that only describe.
- Vague words: "it reacts more" (say "more frequent collisions"); "clear" instead of "colourless"; "it" with no subject.
- Explanations using the wrong particle: electrons moving in molten ionic compounds, intermolecular forces in giant structures.
- Mole calculations with no working, unit errors (cm³/dm³) and premature rounding.
- Wrong conditions for Haber/Contact or organic reactions.
- Incomplete displayed formulae (missing H atoms or the O–H bond).

## Checklist while marking

You will mark in the next session, but before you finish today, write a quick self-assessment:

- Which questions felt confident, uncertain or blank?
- Did you run out of time? On which question?
- Did any question include a topic you hadn't revised?

This helps you compare your expectations with the real mark and spot overconfidence.

## Quick-fire revision

- Ionic: high melting point — strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions; conduct when molten/aqueous — ions free to move.
- Simple molecular: low melting point — weak intermolecular forces.
- Giant covalent (diamond, SiO₂): many strong covalent bonds to break.
- Metals: positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons; layers slide (malleable).
- Collision theory: more frequent collisions; higher temperature → greater proportion of particles with energy ≥ Ea.
- ΔH = bonds broken − bonds made.
- Equilibrium: increase T → shifts in endothermic direction; increase p → side with fewer moles of gas.
- Half-equations: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻; 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻; Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu; 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂.
- Salt preparation: excess solid, filter, evaporate to saturation, cool and crystallise, dry.
- Blast furnace: Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂; CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃.
- Organic conditions: UV light (substitution), nickel (hydrogenation), acid catalyst/300 °C/6000 kPa (hydration), yeast/25–35 °C/anaerobic (fermentation), acid catalyst (esterification).
- Condensation polymers: nylon (amide link), PET (ester link); water is also formed.
