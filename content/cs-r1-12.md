# Issues and impact: environment, ethics, law and cybersecurity

> **In the exam:** Paper 1 (2 hours, 80 marks). Usually one extended question of 6 marks (discuss, evaluate or "assess" an issue, with points for and against and a conclusion), plus several 1–3 mark questions: name a threat and say how it works (2 marks), give ways to protect a system (2–4 marks), say what a licence or patent is (1–2 marks), and name an emerging technology and its impact. The spec is for an international exam, so name general ideas (data protection, copyright, computer misuse) rather than relying on one country's law.

## What you need to know

- **Environmental impact** of technology: health, energy use, resources.
- **Ethical impact**: privacy, inclusion, professionalism.
- **Legal impact**: intellectual property, patents, licensing and cyber-security.
- **Cyber-attacks** (technical weaknesses and behaviour): social engineering (phishing, shoulder surfing, pharming), unpatched software, USB devices, other digital devices, eavesdropping; malware.
- Identifying vulnerabilities: penetration testing, ethical hacking, commercial analysis tools, reviewing network and user policies.
- Protecting systems: software design stage, audit trails, securing operating systems, secure coding, code reviews, modular testing, network security.
- **Emerging trends**: quantum computing, DNA computing, artificial intelligence (AI), nanotechnology.

## Notes

### Environmental impact

| Area | Negative effects | Positive effects |
|---|---|---|
| **Energy use** | Data centres, networks and devices use huge amounts of electricity (and cooling); many are powered by fossil fuels, producing CO₂ | Smart meters, smart heating and efficient route planning save energy; working from home reduces travel |
| **Resources** | Devices need **rare and finite materials** (e.g. lithium, cobalt, gold) that are mined, damaging land; short device lifespans | Paperless offices, e-books and streaming use fewer physical resources; recycling and refurbishing |
| **Waste (e-waste)** | Old devices thrown away contain toxic substances (lead, mercury) that **pollute soil and water**; much is exported to poorer countries | Recycling schemes recover metals; reuse and repair |
| **Health** | Eye strain, back and neck problems, repetitive strain injury (RSI), inactivity, poor sleep, anxiety linked to social media; sedentary lifestyles | Health apps, fitness trackers, telemedicine, remote monitoring |

Ways to reduce harm: more energy-efficient hardware, renewable energy for data centres, **recycle** or donate old devices, repair and upgrade rather than replace, power-saving settings, take breaks and use ergonomic equipment.

### Ethical impact

**Ethics** are principles about what is right and wrong. Technology raises questions:

- **Privacy:** companies and governments collect large amounts of personal data (location, browsing, purchases). Concerns: **surveillance** (CCTV, facial recognition), **tracking and targeted advertising**, data being **sold, leaked or hacked**, loss of anonymity. Laws on data protection (such as the **GDPR** in the EU, including Spain) say organisations must collect only what they need, keep it secure, use it for stated purposes and let people see and correct it.
- **Inclusion (digital divide):** the gap between people who have access to technology and internet skills and those who do not (because of cost, location, age, disability, education). Also **bias** in algorithms and AI trained on unrepresentative data, and software that is not **accessible** (e.g. no screen reader support).
- **Professionalism:** computer professionals should be **honest, competent and responsible**: protect user data, respect copyright, test software properly, avoid harm, be open about errors, and follow a professional **code of conduct** (such as that of the British Computer Society).
- Other issues: **automation** replacing jobs; responsibility when **AI or self-driving cars** make mistakes; misinformation and fake content; cyberbullying.

For a discussion question: give **two or three developed points on each side**, link to the scenario, then **conclude** with a judgement.

### Legal impact

**Intellectual property (IP)** means creations of the mind (software, music, designs, writing) that belong to their creator.
- **Copyright:** automatically protects original works (code, music, films, text). Copying, sharing or selling without permission is **illegal (piracy)**.
- **Patent:** protects an **invention** or process for a limited time (e.g. 20 years), letting the owner stop others from making or selling it without permission.
- **Licensing:** the terms under which software may be used.

| Licence type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Proprietary (commercial)** | Paid for; source code is **not** available; you may not copy or change it; limited installs |
| **Open source** | **Source code freely available**; users can **view, modify and share** it (e.g. Linux); community can find bugs and improve it |
| **Freeware** | Free to use but source code **not** available, and you cannot modify it |
| **Shareware** | Free to try for a limited time or with limited features, then you pay |

**Cyber-security law:** most countries have laws against **unauthorised access** to computer systems, **modifying data** without permission, and creating or spreading malware (in the UK, the Computer Misuse Act 1990). Data protection laws (such as GDPR) require organisations to protect personal data; they can be **fined** if there is a breach.

### Cybersecurity threats

**Malware** (malicious software):
- **Virus:** attaches to a file/program and spreads when that file is opened; can corrupt or delete data.
- **Worm:** spreads by itself across networks without the user doing anything.
- **Trojan:** appears to be useful software but contains something harmful.
- **Ransomware:** encrypts your files and demands payment to unlock them.
- **Spyware:** secretly records what you do (e.g. keystrokes, passwords) and sends it to the attacker.

**Attacks that use people (social engineering)**: tricking people into giving away information or access.
- **Phishing:** fake emails or messages that look genuine, asking for passwords or bank details, or containing a malicious link/attachment.
- **Pharming:** malicious code redirects you from a real website to a **fake one** (even if you type the correct address), to steal your details.
- **Shoulder surfing:** watching someone enter a password or PIN by looking over their shoulder (or recording them).
- **Blagging / pretexting:** inventing a story to get information from someone.

**Technical weaknesses and other attack routes:**
- **Unpatched software:** known security flaws not fixed because updates were not installed.
- **Weak or reused passwords** and **brute-force attacks** (trying many passwords).
- **USB devices:** a found or infected USB stick may carry malware.
- **Eavesdropping / interception:** reading data as it travels over a network (especially public Wi-Fi) if it is not encrypted.
- **Denial of service (DoS/DDoS):** flooding a server with requests so real users cannot use it.
- **SQL injection / poor coding:** attackers enter code into forms to access databases.
- **Other digital devices:** poorly secured smart devices (cameras, IoT) used as entry points.

### Protecting systems

| Protection | How it helps |
|---|---|
| **Strong passwords and two-factor authentication** | Harder to guess or steal; a second proof is needed |
| **Access control / permissions** | Users only reach what they need |
| **Firewall** | Blocks unauthorised traffic |
| **Anti-malware** | Detects and removes malicious software |
| **Software updates / patches** | Closes known vulnerabilities |
| **Encryption** | Data is unreadable if intercepted or stolen |
| **Backups** | Data can be restored after ransomware or damage |
| **Audit trails (logs)** | Record who did what and when, to detect and investigate misuse |
| **User training and policies** | People spot phishing, don't plug in unknown USBs, use screen privacy |
| **Physical security** | Locks, key cards, CCTV |

**Finding weaknesses before attackers do:**
- **Penetration testing:** authorised experts attack a system on purpose to find weak points, then report them.
- **Ethical hacking:** hackers with permission who try to break in so the problems can be fixed (unlike criminal "black hat" hackers).
- **Commercial analysis tools:** software that scans a network or code for known vulnerabilities.
- **Review of network and user policies:** check that password rules, access rights and procedures are up to date and followed.

**Secure software development:** think about security at the **design stage**; follow **secure coding** practices (validate all input, avoid known bad practices); **code reviews** by other programmers; **modular testing** of each part; secure the operating system; keep audit trails.

### Emerging trends

| Technology | Idea | Possible impacts |
|---|---|---|
| **Artificial intelligence (AI)** | Computers that learn from data to make decisions or recognise patterns (e.g. voice assistants, image recognition, recommendations) | Faster diagnosis, automation, personalisation; **job loss, bias, privacy, accountability, deepfakes** |
| **Quantum computing** | Uses **quantum bits (qubits)** that can be in several states at once, to solve some problems much faster | Could speed up drug design and simulation; could **break current encryption**, so new methods are needed |
| **DNA computing** | Uses DNA molecules to store and process data | Huge storage in tiny space and massively parallel; still experimental and slow |
| **Nanotechnology** | Engineering at an extremely small (atomic/molecular) scale | Tiny medical devices, stronger materials, smaller faster chips; **health, safety and environmental risks** not fully known |

## Key terms and definitions

- **Digital divide:** the gap between people who have access to modern technology and those who do not.
- **E-waste:** discarded electronic devices.
- **Intellectual property:** creations of the mind that belong to their creator and are protected by law.
- **Copyright:** the legal right of the creator to control copying and distribution of their work.
- **Patent:** a legal right that stops others from making or selling an invention without permission for a limited period.
- **Open-source software:** software whose source code is freely available to view, modify and share.
- **Malware:** software designed to damage a system or steal data.
- **Phishing:** sending fake messages that appear to come from a trusted source to trick people into revealing personal information.
- **Pharming:** redirecting users from a genuine website to a fake website to steal their data.
- **Social engineering:** manipulating people into giving away information or access.
- **Penetration testing:** an authorised attempt to break into a system in order to find weaknesses.
- **Ethical hacking:** hacking with permission in order to find and fix security weaknesses.
- **Audit trail:** a record of the actions carried out on a system and who carried them out.

## Worked examples

**1. Describe two ways in which technology harms the environment. [4]**

Data centres and devices use large amounts of electricity, often from fossil fuels, which adds to carbon emissions [2]. Old devices become e-waste containing toxic chemicals that can pollute soil and water if not recycled [2]. (Also: mining of rare materials.)

**2. A user receives an email that appears to be from their bank asking them to click a link and enter their password. (a) Name this type of attack. [1] (b) State two ways the user could tell it is fake. [2] (c) State one way they can reduce the risk. [1]**

(a) **Phishing** [1].
(b) Any two: the sender's address does not match the bank; generic greeting; spelling mistakes; urgent threats; link address does not match the bank's real domain [2].
(c) Don't click the link — go to the bank's website directly; use two-factor authentication; report it [1].

**3. Explain the difference between open-source and proprietary software. [2]**

With open-source software the source code is freely available and users can modify and share it [1]. Proprietary software is bought with a licence, the source code is not available and users cannot copy or change it [1].

**4. "Facial recognition cameras should be used in all public places." Discuss. [6]**

For: helps police identify criminals and find missing people quickly; deters crime; could improve safety [up to 2]. Against: invades privacy as people are tracked without consent; data could be hacked or misused; algorithms can be biased and wrongly identify people; may cause people to feel watched [up to 2]. Conclusion with a reasoned judgement, e.g. only acceptable with clear laws, limits on storing data and oversight [up to 2].

## Common mistakes

- Writing "hackers" with no detail. Name the **method** (phishing, brute force, malware) and the **effect** (data stolen, files encrypted).
- Mixing up virus and worm: a worm spreads **by itself**; a virus needs a host file to be run.
- Confusing phishing (fake message) with pharming (fake website via redirection).
- Saying "antivirus stops all attacks". It only protects against known malware, and cannot stop social engineering.
- Saying "ethical hacking is illegal". It is **authorised**.
- Saying copyright is "the right to copy" instead of the creator's right to **control** copying.
- One-sided answers to "discuss". Give both sides and a conclusion.
- Forgetting to link answers to the scenario (a school, a hospital, a shop).
