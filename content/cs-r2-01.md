# Past paper · Paper 1 timed (1/2)

> **Goal:** Sit the first half of a real Pearson Paper 1 (Principles of Computer Science) under exam conditions, so you practise pacing at about 1.5 minutes per mark and find out which topics lose you marks.

## Before you start

- **Paper to use:** a real Pearson Edexcel International GCSE Computer Science **Paper 1 (4CP0/01)** with its mark scheme. Download a recent series from the Pearson website (qualifications.pearson.com: International GCSE Computer Science, past papers/exam materials), or ask your teacher for the paper. Use the Sample Assessment Materials only if you have already used all real papers. Choose a paper you have **not seen** before.
- Print it if you can. Do **not** open the mark scheme yet.
- Have ready: a black pen, a pencil and ruler (for diagrams), a calculator only if the front page of the paper allows one, and a timer or clock. Keep your notes closed.
- Find a quiet desk and put your phone away.
- Today's job is the **first half**: roughly the first 40 marks of the paper (about the first 40–45 minutes of the real 2 hours). Stop at the 60-minute mark if you are not finished; draw a line where you stopped. Tomorrow's session completes the paper.
- If your paper has the questions in a different order from your revision, do them in the paper's order.

## Exam format reminder

- **Paper 1: Principles of Computer Science**, code 4CP0/01. **2 hours, 80 marks, 50% of the qualification.** All questions are compulsory.
- Question types: **multiple choice, short open-response, open-response and extended open-response**.
- It tests **all the topics**: algorithms and problem solving (flowcharts, pseudocode, trace tables, searching and sorting), programming (data types, constructs, subprograms, testing), data (binary, hex, images, sound, compression, encryption), computers (CPU, memory, storage, software, languages, logic and truth tables), networks and the internet, and the bigger picture (environment, ethics, law, security, emerging technology).
- Pseudocode and algorithm questions are written in **Pearson's pseudocode style** (the paper shows the style; Paper 2 has a reference document).
- Marks per question are shown in brackets. About **1.5 minutes per mark** is the pace for the whole paper.

## How to do it

1. **0–3 min:** read the front page instructions. Look at the whole paper quickly: how many pages, where the long questions are. Write the **time budget**: 80 marks in 120 min means 1 mark ≈ 1.5 min.
2. **3–50 min:** start the timer and answer questions in order. Write start and end time on the paper for each question. Aim for about 40 marks in this stretch. If a question takes more than twice its mark value in minutes, move on and put a star next to it.
3. **50–55 min:** go back to the starred questions and have a second go.
4. **55–60 min:** check your answers: every binary answer has 8 bits, every calculation has a unit, every "explain" has a reason. Write on the first page the question number where you stopped and how long it took.
5. **Do not mark yet.** Put the paper away for the second session. Write on a sticky note: "3 questions I felt unsure about" and list them.

## Exam technique for this paper

- Read the **command word** first: **State/Name/Give** = just the fact. **Describe** = give an account with detail. **Explain** = a point plus a reason ("because…", "so that…"). **Discuss/Evaluate** = both sides and a conclusion. **Calculate** = show working and the unit.
- The marks tell you how many points to make. A 2-mark "explain" needs two linked points; a 1-mark "state" needs one only.
- **Use the scenario.** If the question mentions a school, a hospital or a shop, your answer should mention it. Generic answers lose marks.
- **Multiple choice:** read all four options, cross out wrong ones, never leave blank. Only one answer is allowed per question.
- **Trace tables and algorithms:** work line by line and fill every row; show intermediate values. Do not skip a step in your head.
- **Binary and hex:** write all 8 bits; check by converting back to denary.
- **Extended answers (6 marks):** plan in 30 seconds: 2–3 points for, 2–3 points against, then a conclusion that picks a side and gives a reason.
- Use correct vocabulary: "volatile", "packet", "lossy", "validation". Avoid "it" without saying what "it" is.
- Never write "faster", "better", "cheaper" alone. Say **what** is faster and **why**.
- If you do not know, write something sensible: there are no negative marks.

## Checklist while marking

This is for tomorrow when you mark, but know it now:

- Mark in **a different colour pen**. Follow the mark scheme exactly: marks are for the points given; "accept" and "do not accept" lines matter.
- For each lost mark, write a **reason code** next to it: **K** = knowledge gap, **T** = technique (didn't use command word or scenario), **C** = careless (e.g. forgot a bit or unit), **R** = ran out of time.
- Count marks per **topic** so you can see where to revise.
- Note any question where you gained the mark by guessing, and revise that point too.

## Quick-fire revision

- Binary place values: 128 64 32 16 8 4 2 1. Hex digit = 4 bits.
- Image size = width × height × colour depth (bits) ÷ 8; sound size = rate × bit depth × seconds × channels ÷ 8.
- KiB/MiB/GiB go up in 1024s; kB/MB/GB go up in 1000s.
- Lossless keeps all data (RLE, ZIP, PNG); lossy removes data (JPEG, MP3).
- CPU: control unit decodes, ALU calculates, registers store, buses carry (address, data, control).
- Fetch-decode-execute: PC → MAR, fetch via data bus into MDR/CIR, PC increments, decode, execute.
- RAM is volatile and in use; ROM is non-volatile and boot program; cache is small, fast, near the CPU.
- Compiler: whole program, executable, fast to run. Interpreter: line by line, stops at the first error.
- Router joins networks; switch joins devices in a LAN; DNS turns names into IP addresses.
- Packets have a header with source, destination and packet number; reassembled at the destination.
- Truth tables: AND needs both true; OR needs at least one; NOT flips.
- Three error types: syntax, logic, runtime. Test data: normal, boundary, erroneous.
- Threats: phishing (fake message), pharming (fake site), ransomware, worm; defences: firewall, updates, backups, two-factor authentication.
