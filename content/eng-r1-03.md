# Reading: summary technique

> **In the exam:** Paper 1, the final part of Question 1, based on Text B. 15 marks: 10 for Reading (selecting relevant ideas) and 5 for Writing (concise, own words, well organised). You must write continuous prose of **no more than 120 words**. It comes up on every paper.

## What you need to know

- R1: demonstrate understanding of explicit meanings
- R5: select and use information for specific purposes
- W1–W5 apply to the 5 writing marks: organise ideas, use your own words, write accurately and concisely
- The summary must be in **continuous writing** (not note form) and in your **own words as far as possible**

## Notes

### What the question looks like

"Write a summary of *[focus]*, according to Text B. You must use continuous writing (not note form). Use your own words as far as possible. Your summary should include all the relevant ideas and be well organised. Your summary should not be more than 120 words."

The **focus** is the key. Text B usually contains material that is *not* relevant to it (anecdotes, examples, background, the writer's personal story).

### Step-by-step method (about 25 minutes)

1. **Underline the focus** (e.g. "the benefits of urban rooftop gardens"). If there are two parts ("reasons for… and problems with…"), you need both.
2. **Read Text B once** for overall sense.
3. **Highlight** each separate relevant idea. Aim for **10–15 ideas**. Ignore examples, statistics, quotations and repetition – keep the *point* the example illustrates.
4. **Make a quick plan**: list each idea in 2–4 words of your own. Group similar ideas.
5. **Write** the summary from your plan, not from the text. Use connectives to join ideas.
6. **Count words** and cut anything not essential.

### Generalising: the key skill

Turn specific examples into the general point they make.

| Text B says | Summary point |
|---|---|
| "Bees, butterflies and even kestrels have been spotted on roofs in Rotterdam." | they attract wildlife |
| "Office workers report taking lunch among the tomatoes, returning calmer." | they reduce stress |
| "The building's heating bill fell by a fifth." | they insulate buildings, cutting energy costs |

### Own words without wasting words

- Replace key vocabulary, but keep it short. "Mitigate the urban heat island effect" → "cool cities".
- Do not add your opinions, introductions ("This text is about…") or conclusions.
- Combine ideas in one sentence: "They cool cities, absorb rainwater and attract wildlife."

### What the 5 writing marks reward (plain words)

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Top (5) | Clear, concise, well-organised; ideas grouped; consistently own words; fluent |
| Middle (3) | Mostly own words but some lifting; some repetition or slightly wordy |
| Low (1) | Heavily copied, list-like, or far over the limit; hard to follow |

## Worked examples

**Text B (original practice text) – Rooftop farms**

> Walk along almost any city street and look up: most roofs are empty grey squares. A growing number of architects think this is a waste. When a roof is covered with soil and plants, it soaks up heavy rain that would otherwise rush into overloaded drains, reducing the risk of floods. The plants also shade and insulate the building – one hospital in Toronto found its heating bill fell by a fifth after planting its roof. In summer, green roofs are measurably cooler than tar ones, which helps lower the temperature of the whole neighbourhood. Bees, butterflies and even kestrels have been spotted on rooftop gardens in Rotterdam, giving wildlife a foothold in the concrete. Some roofs grow vegetables, which are sold in shops just a few floors below, cutting the miles food travels. Office workers report spending lunch breaks among the tomatoes and returning to their desks calmer. My own uncle, who has never grown anything, now talks about little else. Critics point out, however, that strengthening an old roof to carry wet soil can be very expensive, and that leaks are costly to repair.

**Question:** Write a summary of the **benefits** of rooftop gardens, according to Text B. [15]

**Plan (ideas):** absorb rain → fewer floods · insulate → lower energy bills · cooler in summer → cooler neighbourhood · habitat for wildlife · grow local food → less transport · reduce stress for workers.
*Not included:* the uncle (anecdote), the critics (drawbacks – not the focus), the hospital and Rotterdam names (examples).

**Model answer (90 words):**
> Rooftop gardens offer several advantages. The soil and plants absorb heavy rainfall, so less water floods the drains and streets below. They also protect buildings from heat loss, which can significantly reduce the cost of heating, and in hot weather they stay cooler than ordinary roofs, helping to keep the surrounding area at a lower temperature. In addition, they provide a habitat for insects and birds in otherwise built-up places. Food can be grown close to where it is sold, reducing transport, and employees who relax there feel less stressed.

*Why it scores highly:*
- **Reading (10/10 range):** all six relevant ideas, each clearly understood; examples converted into general points; drawbacks and anecdote rightly left out.
- **Writing (5):** consistently own words ("protect buildings from heat loss" for "shade and insulate"); connectives ("also", "in addition") organise the ideas; well under 120 words with no padding.

**Weak version (for comparison):**
> The text is about rooftop gardens. It soaks up heavy rain that would otherwise rush into overloaded drains. One hospital in Toronto found its heating bill fell by a fifth. Bees, butterflies and even kestrels have been spotted. My uncle now talks about little else. But strengthening an old roof can be very expensive.

*Why it scores poorly:* lifted phrases, an empty opening sentence, specific examples not generalised, irrelevant anecdote and a drawback included, several ideas missed.

## Common mistakes

- Including examples, names, numbers or quotations instead of the point behind them.
- Including material outside the focus (e.g. drawbacks when asked for benefits).
- Copying chunks of the text ("lifting") – costs writing marks and can hide understanding.
- Writing an introduction or conclusion that adds no ideas.
- Exceeding 120 words.
- Repeating the same idea in different words.
- Writing in note form or bullet points.

## Practice questions

**Text B2 (original practice text) – Why we should walk more**

> Doctors have long recommended walking as the simplest form of exercise. It needs no equipment beyond a pair of shoes, and no gym membership. Regular walkers have stronger hearts and lower blood pressure, and studies suggest that a brisk thirty-minute walk each day can lower the risk of type 2 diabetes. Walking is also kind to the joints, which makes it suitable for older people who find running painful. Researchers in Japan found that people who walked in forests had lower levels of stress hormones afterwards. Many writers, from Wordsworth to Dickens, walked for hours to solve problems in their work, and modern studies confirm that walking boosts creative thinking. Walking to school or work instead of driving cuts traffic and pollution. My grandmother walked to the market every day until she was ninety. Of course, in some cities pavements are narrow, dangerous or simply missing, which discourages people from walking at all.

2. List every relevant idea in note form (own words). [6]
3. Which sentences should you leave out, and why? [2]
4. Write the summary in no more than 120 words. [15]
5. Rewrite this lifted sentence in your own words, concisely: "It needs no equipment beyond a pair of shoes, and no gym membership." [2]
