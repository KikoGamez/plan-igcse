# Writer’s effect: practice

> **In the exam:** Paper 1, final part of Question 2 (Text C): 15 marks, about 25 minutes, roughly 200–300 words. Two named paragraphs, three choices from each, including imagery. Today is a full timed attempt followed by marking with level descriptors.

## What you need to know

- R4: demonstrate understanding of how writers achieve effects and influence readers
- Select words and phrases precisely from each named paragraph
- Explain meaning in context and the effect on the reader
- Show how images combine to create an overall impression

## Notes

### Which question to use

- **Best:** the writer's effect question from a Cambridge 0500 Paper 1 you have **not** done (e.g. a recent May/June or Oct/Nov series from your teacher), marked with its official mark scheme. The mark scheme lists "possible choices" with comments – these are examples, not a checklist; other well-explained choices also earn credit.
- **Otherwise:** use the original Text C extract below and the marking guide on this page.

### Quick reminder of the method

1. Read the two paragraphs; decide each paragraph's **main idea/mood** in a few words.
2. Underline 4–5 candidate choices in each; keep the best **three** (at least one image each).
3. For each: quote → meaning → effect (connotations of specific words).
4. End each paragraph of your answer with one sentence on the overall impression.

### Timing

3 min read · 4 min select · 16 min write · 2 min check that every choice has an *effect* sentence.

### How to self-mark (plain-words levels)

| Level | Ask yourself |
|---|---|
| Top | Did I choose precise, interesting words from **both** paragraphs? Did I explain the effect of individual words, not just the whole phrase? Did I explore images and link them into an overall impression? |
| Upper middle | Are most effects explained, even if one or two are general? Are meanings clear? |
| Lower middle | Did I mostly explain meanings? Are effects vague ("makes it vivid")? |
| Low | Did I copy long quotations or just name techniques? |

## Worked examples

**Text C extract (original practice text) – "The Climb"**

> (4) Above the last hut the path gave up pretending to be a path. It became a staircase of loose stones that shifted and complained under our boots. The wind arrived without warning, shouldering us towards the edge as if it wanted the mountain to itself. Far below, the valley shrank to a green thumbprint.
> (7) Then, quite suddenly, we were above the cloud. It lay beneath us like a frozen sea, white and motionless, broken only by the black islands of other summits. The sun poured over everything, and the silence was so complete that I could hear my own heartbeat, steady at last, like a clock in an empty house.

**Question:** Re-read paragraphs 4 and 7. Explain how the writer uses language to convey meaning and to create effect in these paragraphs. Choose three examples of words or phrases from each paragraph to support your answer. Your choices should include the use of imagery. [15]

**Model answer (top level, about 280 words):**

> In paragraph 4 the climb becomes hostile. "The path gave up pretending to be a path" means the track disappears, but personifying it as something that had been "pretending" suggests the mountain was deceptive, and "gave up" implies the climbers are now abandoned without help. The "staircase of loose stones that shifted and complained" shows the ground is unstable; "complained" gives the stones a grumbling voice, so even the rocks seem to resent the climbers, and the sound of grinding stone adds tension. The wind "shouldering us towards the edge as if it wanted the mountain to itself" means strong gusts push them sideways; "shouldering" suggests a rough, deliberate shove from a bully, and the idea that it "wanted the mountain to itself" makes the danger seem personal and jealous. Together these images create a sense that nature itself is trying to drive the walkers away.
>
> Paragraph 7 is a contrast of calm and reward. The cloud "like a frozen sea" means it is spread flat below them; "frozen" suggests stillness and perfect smoothness, transforming the climb into a magical, almost Arctic landscape, and "black islands" makes the other peaks seem isolated and dramatic against the white. "The sun poured" means light covered everything; "poured" suggests a generous liquid flood, conveying warmth and abundance after the cold struggle. The simile "like a clock in an empty house" shows the narrator's heartbeat is calm and regular, but the "empty house" also emphasises the vast, slightly eerie silence and the narrator's solitude. Overall the language shifts from threat to peaceful wonder, making the summit feel earned.

*Why it scores in the top level:*
- three precise choices per paragraph, each including imagery;
- meaning **and** effect every time, with individual words unpacked ("gave up", "complained", "shouldering", "frozen", "poured");
- links between choices and a clear overall impression for each paragraph;
- recognises the contrast between paragraphs;
- concise: no introduction, no device-naming without explanation.

**A middle-level sentence – and how to lift it:**
> *"The valley shrank to a green thumbprint" is a metaphor which creates a vivid image.*
> Improved: *"Shrank to a green thumbprint" shows how high they have climbed: the whole valley now looks tiny, the size of a fingertip mark, which emphasises the height and their distance from safety.*

## Common mistakes

- Choosing all six examples from one paragraph.
- Writing a general essay about the passage instead of focusing on the named paragraphs.
- Explaining the simile as a whole but not the key word inside it.
- Repeating "this shows" without saying *what* it shows about mood, character or situation.
- Running over time – keep to about 25 minutes so Q3 is not rushed.

## Practice questions

Timed task (25 minutes). Use a past-paper question if you have one; otherwise answer the question below.

> **Text C extract (original practice text) – "The Old Cinema"**
> (2) The Regal had been closed for thirty years. Its neon sign hung crookedly, missing three letters, like a smile with teeth knocked out. Pigeons had colonised the balcony, and the velvet seats had faded to the colour of old bruises. Dust lay so thick on the stage that each footstep left a fossil.
> (5) On opening night, the restored cinema blazed. Light spilled out of every doorway onto the wet pavement, turning puddles into pools of gold. Inside, the audience murmured like a sea before a storm, and when the curtain lifted, a hush fell so suddenly that it felt like a held breath.

1. Re-read paragraphs 2 and 5. Explain how the writer uses language to convey meaning and to create effect in these paragraphs. Choose three examples of words or phrases from each paragraph to support your answer. Your choices should include the use of imagery. [15]

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

Possible choices (other well-explained choices are equally valid):

**Paragraph 2 – neglect and decay**
- "like a smile with teeth knocked out" – the sign has missing letters; makes the building seem battered, sad, perhaps beaten up by time; once cheerful, now damaged and slightly grotesque.
- "colonised" – pigeons have taken over; suggests an invasion, the building now belongs to the birds, humans have abandoned it.
- "the colour of old bruises" – the seats have faded to dull purple/yellow; connects to injury, as if the cinema has been hurt; reinforces the "knocked-out teeth" image.
- "each footstep left a fossil" – dust is so deep footprints remain; "fossil" suggests extreme age, as if the cinema belongs to prehistory, frozen in time.
Overall: a wounded, abandoned building, almost a victim.

**Paragraph 5 – rebirth, energy, anticipation**
- "blazed" – shines brightly; suggests fire, energy, intensity, contrasting with the dead building before.
- "Light spilled out … pools of gold" – light overflows; gold suggests wealth, magic and value; even dirty puddles are transformed.
- "murmured like a sea before a storm" – the audience talks quietly; the simile suggests a large mass of people and growing excitement about to break.
- "like a held breath" – sudden silence; suggests tension and anticipation, everyone waiting for something special.
Overall: the building has come back to life, glamorous and full of expectation.

Self-mark using the level table above. Top level needs: 6 precise choices, effects explained word by word, overall impressions, and the contrast between neglect and revival.
