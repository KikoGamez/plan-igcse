# Extended response to reading

> **In the exam:** Paper 1, Question 3, based on Text C. 25 marks: 15 for Reading, 10 for Writing. You write 250–350 words in a set form (letter, report, journal, speech, interview or article), usually in the role of a character or someone connected with the text, covering the bullet points in the task. Allow about 35 minutes. It appears on every paper.

## What you need to know

- R1, R2: understand explicit and implicit meanings and attitudes
- R3: analyse, evaluate and **develop** facts, ideas and opinions, using support from the text
- R5: select and use information for a specific purpose
- W1–W5: write in an appropriate voice and register, organised, with varied vocabulary and sentences, accurately

## Notes

### What the task looks like

"Imagine you are [character]. Write a [letter/journal entry/speech…] to [audience]. In your [form] you should: • [bullet 1 – usually explicit content] • [bullet 2 – feelings/attitudes, needs inference] • [bullet 3 – opinion/prediction, needs development]. Base your [form] on what you have read in Text C, but do not copy from it. Be careful to use your own words. Address each of the bullet points. Begin your [form]: '…' Write about 250 to 350 words."

### How the 15 reading marks work (plain words)

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Top | Uses many ideas from the text, **develops** them with convincing inferences, stays in role; all bullets covered fully; ideas fit together into a coherent whole |
| Middle | Uses a fair range of ideas, mostly explained; some development; bullets covered but unevenly |
| Low | Retells the text; little inference; bullets missing; may copy |

"Develop" means going beyond what the text states: explaining *why*, imagining consequences, linking details, giving the character's reaction – always **grounded** in the text, never invented freely.

### How the 10 writing marks work

- **Voice and register** suit the character and audience (a journal is private and reflective; a speech addresses listeners directly; a report is formal and neutral).
- **Structure**: clear paragraphs, usually one per bullet, with a fitting opening and close.
- **Language**: own words, varied vocabulary and sentences, accurate.

### Form conventions

| Form | Key conventions |
|---|---|
| Letter | Greeting ("Dear…"), purpose in first paragraph, sign-off matching register |
| Journal / diary | First person, past and present mixed, reflective, honest feelings |
| Speech | Address audience ("Good morning, everyone"), direct appeals, rhetorical questions, memorable ending |
| Report | Title, formal tone, perhaps subheadings, objective, recommendations at end |
| Interview | Question-and-answer layout with names; answers developed, in voice |
| Article | Headline, engaging opening, third person or first person, aimed at readers |

### Method (35 minutes)

1. **5 min:** reread Text C; label each relevant detail B1, B2 or B3 according to the bullet it fits.
2. **5 min:** plan – for each bullet, 3–4 text details + one developed idea (inference).
3. **22 min:** write about 300 words, one or two paragraphs per bullet.
4. **3 min:** check own words, register, accuracy.

## Worked examples

**Text C (original practice text, shortened) – "The Last Ferry"**

> When the bridge to Holm Island opened, the ferry that had crossed the strait for ninety years was withdrawn. Its captain, Ewan Reid, had worked on it for thirty-one of those years. He knew which passengers were seasick, which children were going to the dentist, which farmers would want to talk about sheep prices. On the last crossing, islanders filled the deck. Some cried; a few brought flags. A teenage girl filmed everything on her phone and said, "My kids won't even know this existed." Ewan sounded the horn three times as they left harbour, which he had never done before. Afterwards he sat in the empty cabin for an hour. The bridge, everyone agreed, would make life easier: no more cancelled crossings in storms, no more racing for the 6 p.m. boat. But the old man in the ticket office said, "We'll get to the mainland quicker. Whether we'll still talk to each other is another matter."

**Task:** Imagine you are Ewan Reid. That evening you write a journal entry. In it you should write about:
- what happened on the final crossing
- your thoughts and feelings about the ferry and its passengers
- what you think the bridge will mean for the island in future.

Begin: *"Well, it's done."* Write about 250 to 350 words. [25]

**Model answer (extract – first two sections, about 210 words):**

> *Well, it's done. Thirty-one years, and it ended with a ticket stub and a bit of bunting.*
> *I didn't expect so many people. The deck was packed tighter than on market day – people who haven't needed the boat for years came just to say goodbye. Some had flags, one or two wept openly, and young Isla Morrison recorded every second, as if she were collecting evidence that we had ever existed. When we cleared the harbour I pulled the horn three times. I've never done that in my life. I'm not sure why I did it today – I think I wanted the island to hear that we were going.*
> *The ferry was never just transport. I knew who couldn't stomach a swell and kept them near the rail; I knew which nervous child was off to have a tooth pulled and told them about the seals. The farmers would grumble to me about prices as if I could fix them. I suppose I was a sort of noticeboard and confessor rolled into one. Sitting alone in the cabin afterwards, with the seats empty, it felt like being in a house after a funeral.*

*Why it scores highly:*
- **Reading:** uses most details (crowd, flags, tears, filming, horn, knowing passengers, sitting alone) **in own words**; develops them – *why* he sounded the horn, his role as "noticeboard and confessor" (inferred from what he knew about passengers), the funeral comparison (inferred from the hour in the empty cabin).
- **Writing:** convincing private voice (short sentences, dry humour, honest uncertainty); one paragraph per bullet; varied sentence lengths; vocabulary chosen for effect ("bunting", "confessor"). The third bullet would weigh the advantages (no cancellations, speed) against the loss of community suggested by the ticket-office man.

## Common mistakes

- Retelling the text in order instead of answering the bullets.
- Copying phrases or whole sentences from Text C.
- Inventing new events not supported by the text (a car crash on the bridge, a secret love affair).
- Losing the role – writing as a student analysing the text.
- Wrong register: chatty slang in a formal report, or a stiff, formal diary.
- Giving bullet 3 one sentence; it usually carries the most "development" marks.
- Writing 500+ words and losing control and accuracy.

## Practice questions

1. Plan bullet 3 of the Ewan Reid task: list two text details and two developed ideas. [4]
2. Write the final paragraph of Ewan's journal (about 100 words). [8]
3. Rewrite the task as a **speech** by the teenage girl at a farewell event. Write the first 80 words, showing speech conventions. [5]
4. Which details from the text would a **report** for the island council use, and in what tone? [4]
5. Explain the difference between "developing" an idea and "inventing" one, with an example from Text C. [2]
