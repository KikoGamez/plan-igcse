# Directed writing: argue and evaluate

> **In the exam:** Paper 2, Section A (compulsory). 40 marks: 15 for Reading, 25 for Writing. You read two short texts on the same topic, then write an argumentative or discursive piece – usually a letter, article or speech – of 250–350 words. Allow about 1 hour including reading and planning. On every paper.

## What you need to know

- R2: understand implicit meanings and attitudes
- R3: analyse, **evaluate** and develop facts, ideas and opinions, using support from the texts
- R5: select and use information for a specific purpose
- W1–W5: express a clear viewpoint; structure for effect; varied vocabulary and sentences; register appropriate to audience; accuracy

## Notes

### What the task looks like

"Write a [letter to your headteacher / article for a magazine / speech to your class] giving your views on [issue]. In your [form] you should: • evaluate the ideas and opinions in both texts • give your own views, based on what you have read. Base your answer on what you have read in both texts, but be careful to use your own words. Address both of the bullet points. Write about 250 to 350 words."

### What "evaluate" means

Evaluating = **judging** how strong, fair, reliable or convincing an idea is – not just reporting it.

| Reporting (lower level) | Evaluating (top level) |
|---|---|
| Text A says homework helps students. | Text A claims homework builds independence, but its only evidence is one teacher's experience – hardly enough to convince parents. |
| Text B says it causes stress. | Text B's survey of 2,000 students is more persuasive, though it was run by a campaign group, which may affect its objectivity. |

Evaluation questions to ask of each idea:
- What **evidence** supports it (statistics, expert, anecdote)? Is it enough?
- Who is the writer – could they be **biased**?
- Is the argument **exaggerated**, one-sided or emotional?
- Does the other text **contradict** or **support** it?
- Does it apply to **everyone**, or only some situations?

### Reading marks (15) in plain words

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Top | Evaluates ideas from **both** texts thoroughly; reads between the lines (implicit attitudes); combines ideas into a developed, convincing argument |
| Middle | Uses a range of ideas from both texts; some evaluation, but often just summarises and agrees/disagrees |
| Low | Repeats or copies a few ideas; little sense of argument |

### Writing marks (25) in plain words

Top level: highly effective style, register exactly right for the audience, carefully structured paragraphs that build an argument, wide vocabulary, varied sentences, very few errors.

### Structure (about 300–350 words)

1. **Opening (3–4 sentences):** greet/address audience, state the issue and your overall position.
2. **Body 1:** first set of ideas from the texts, evaluated.
3. **Body 2:** opposing ideas, evaluated (concede what is strong, challenge what is weak).
4. **Body 3:** your own developed view, linking ideas from both texts.
5. **Conclusion:** clear final judgement or recommendation; sign off / closing appeal.

### Register by form

| Form | Register |
|---|---|
| Letter to headteacher / council | Formal, polite, respectful; "Dear Ms…"; "Yours sincerely" |
| Article for school magazine | Semi-formal, lively; headline; engaging opening |
| Speech to classmates | Direct, persuasive; "you", "we"; rhetorical questions |

### Useful evaluative phrases

"The writer claims…, yet offers little evidence…" · "This argument is strengthened by…" · "While this is true for…, it ignores…" · "Both writers agree that…, but they draw opposite conclusions." · "Perhaps the most convincing point is…"

## Worked examples

**Text A (original) – from a school newsletter, by the Head of Sport**
> Every student should play a team sport. In my twenty years of coaching, I have watched shy eleven-year-olds become confident leaders. Sport teaches you to lose with dignity and win without arrogance. Schools that cut PE time to make room for exam preparation are making a terrible mistake: active students concentrate better in class.

**Text B (original) – from a student blog**
> I hated compulsory rugby. Being shouted at in the rain while the "sporty" kids ignored me taught me nothing except how to hide in the changing rooms. A survey at our school found that 40% of students in Year 10 would rather do individual activities like swimming, climbing or dance. Forcing everyone into competitive team games puts some people off exercise for life.

**Task:** Write a letter to your headteacher giving your views on whether team sport should be compulsory for all students. [40]

**Model answer (extract – opening and first two body paragraphs, about 220 words):**

> *Dear Mrs Navarro,*
> *I am writing in response to the recent discussion about making team sport compulsory for every student. While I agree that exercise is essential, I do not believe forcing everyone into team games is the best way to achieve it.*
>
> *The Head of Sport makes a persuasive case that team sport builds confidence and teaches students to handle both victory and defeat. Twenty years of coaching gives his view real authority. However, his evidence comes from the students he has seen flourish; it is natural that he remembers successes, and he may not notice those who quietly withdraw. His point about concentration is more convincing, since most of us feel more alert after exercise – but that benefit comes from being active, not specifically from being in a team.*
>
> *The student blogger, by contrast, describes the opposite experience: humiliation and exclusion. Her account is emotional and based on one person's experience, so it cannot prove that team sport is harmful for everyone. Yet her survey suggests she is far from alone: if four in ten Year 10 students would prefer individual activities, a compulsory policy risks alienating a large minority.*

*Why it scores highly:*
- **Reading:** both texts used; ideas **evaluated** (authority vs bias, anecdote vs survey, distinguishing activity from team sport) rather than repeated; implicit point drawn (coaches remember successes).
- **Writing:** formal register for a headteacher; clear position in the opening; topic sentences signal the structure; balanced concessions ("persuasive", "more convincing", "Yet"); accurate, varied sentences. The rest would develop the student's own view (e.g. a choice of activities including team sport) and close with a recommendation and "Yours sincerely".

## Common mistakes

- Summarising Text A, then Text B, with no judgement – capped in the middle reading levels.
- Using only one text.
- Copying phrases from the texts.
- Forgetting the audience: slang to a headteacher, or no greeting/sign-off.
- Giving your opinion without linking it to the texts.
- Writing a narrative or description instead of an argument.
- No clear overall position, or changing position halfway without explanation.

## Practice questions

1. Find two weaknesses in the evidence of Text A. [2]
2. Find one strength and one weakness in Text B's argument. [2]
3. Write an opening paragraph for a **speech to your class** on the same issue (about 60 words). [5]
4. Rewrite this reporting sentence as evaluation: "Text B says 40% of students prefer individual activities." [2]
5. Write a conclusion (about 60 words) for the letter, ending with a recommendation. [5]
6. List three changes you would make if the task were an article for a school magazine instead of a letter. [3]
