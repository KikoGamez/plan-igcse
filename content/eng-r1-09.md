# Directed writing: timed practice

> **In the exam:** Paper 2, Section A: one compulsory directed writing task worth 40 marks (15 Reading + 25 Writing), 250–350 words, about 1 hour including reading. Today you write one full answer under exam conditions.

## What you need to know

- R2, R3, R5: identify, evaluate and use ideas and opinions from both texts, including implicit attitudes
- W1–W5: clear viewpoint, deliberate structure, varied language, appropriate register, accuracy
- Address **both** bullet points: evaluate the texts **and** give your own view

## Notes

### Timing for the hour

| Minutes | Step |
|---|---|
| 0–10 | Read the task (form, audience, purpose). Read both texts, annotating: + (strong point), – (weak point), ? (bias/assumption). |
| 10–20 | Plan: decide your position; group ideas into 3–4 paragraphs; for each, note which text ideas you will evaluate. |
| 20–50 | Write about 300–350 words. |
| 50–60 | Check: both texts used? evaluation in every body paragraph? register consistent? spelling, punctuation, paragraphs? |

### Task to use

- **Best:** Section A of a Cambridge 0500 Paper 2 you have not done (recent May/June or Oct/Nov series), with its mark scheme.
- **Otherwise:** the original task below.

### Self-check before you finish

- Did I state my position in the first paragraph?
- Is there at least one evaluative comment (evidence, bias, exaggeration, limitation) per body paragraph?
- Did I use ideas from **both** texts, in my own words?
- Does my conclusion make a clear judgement or recommendation?
- Is the tone right for the audience all the way through?

## Worked examples

**Text A (original) – article by a city councillor**
> Our city centre is choking. Cars account for most of the air pollution on our high street, and asthma rates among children here are among the highest in the region. Banning private cars from the centre would make the streets safer and quieter, and cities such as Pontevedra in Spain show that shops can thrive when people walk. Change is uncomfortable, but our children's lungs matter more than our convenience.

**Text B (original) – letter from a shop owner to a local newspaper**
> I have run a hardware shop on the high street for twenty-two years. Most of my customers are older people and tradespeople who cannot carry paint tins and timber onto a bus. When the council closed one road for "trial pedestrianisation" last summer, my takings fell by a third. Councillors who work in offices have no idea what it costs to keep a small business alive. Clean air is important, but not if the high street becomes a museum of empty shops.

**Task:** Your city council is considering banning private cars from the city centre. Write a speech to be given at a public meeting, giving your views. In your speech you should: • evaluate the views given in both texts • give your own views, based on what you have read. Write about 250 to 350 words. [40]

**Model answer (about 340 words):**

> *Good evening, everyone. I am sixteen, I live two streets from the high street, and I would like to tell you why I believe we should ban cars from the centre – but carefully, and not overnight.*
>
> *The councillor's case is powerful. If cars cause most of the pollution on our high street and children here suffer more asthma than elsewhere, then doing nothing is not a neutral choice: it is a choice to accept harm. The example of Pontevedra suggests shops can survive without cars, although one Spanish town is not proof that every city will succeed, and we are not told how long it took or what support businesses received.*
>
> *The shop owner's letter deserves to be taken seriously too. A third of his takings lost in one summer is not a small inconvenience; it is the difference between staying open and closing. He also makes a fair point that his customers are often elderly or need to carry heavy goods. However, his evidence comes from a single short trial on one road, perhaps before people had changed their habits, and his comment that councillors "have no idea" is more frustration than argument.*
>
> *So who is right? I think both are – and that is why the answer is not a simple yes or no. Our health must come first: you cannot run a business, or a city, with sick children. But a ban will only work if it protects the people the shop owner describes. That means delivery and loading permits for traders, free shuttle buses and parking at the edge of town, and help for shops during the first year while customers adjust.*
>
> *Imagine a high street where you can hear conversations instead of engines, where children cross without fear, and where the hardware shop is still there because we planned for it. That is not a museum. That is a living city.*
>
> *I urge the council to vote for the ban – and to vote, at the same time, for the support that will make it fair. Thank you.*

*Why it scores highly:*
- **Reading (top level):** both texts used extensively; every claim weighed (statistics vs a single trial; one example town; emotional language); implicit point drawn (the trial may have been too short for habits to change); ideas combined into a new solution grounded in both texts.
- **Writing (top level):** speech conventions (greeting, direct address, rhetorical question, imperative "Imagine", closing "Thank you"); clear position stated early and confirmed at the end; deliberate structure (for → against → synthesis → vision → appeal); varied sentences, including short ones for emphasis ("That is not a museum."); the shopkeeper's own image turned back in the conclusion; accurate throughout.

## Common mistakes

- Spending 25 minutes reading and planning, then rushing the writing.
- Forgetting the form: a "speech" with no audience address reads like an essay.
- Evaluating only one text.
- Copying the most quotable phrases ("our children's lungs matter more than our convenience").
- Ending without a clear judgement.
- Not leaving time to proofread – accuracy is part of the 25 writing marks.

## Practice questions

**Timed task (1 hour).** Use a past-paper Section A if you have one; otherwise:

**Text A (original) – school website, from the Head of Year 11**
> From January, mobile phones will be locked away during the school day. Teachers report that students are distracted in lessons, and break times have become silent, with everyone staring at screens. Research published by several countries' education departments links phone bans with better concentration. Students will be able to call home from the school office.

**Text B (original) – student newspaper, Year 11 student**
> Nobody asked us. Many of us use our phones for timetables, homework apps and dictionaries. Some students have parents who work abroad and message during the day. Yes, some people overuse their phones – but punishing everyone for a few people's habits is lazy. Teach us to manage technology, because the adult world won't lock it away for us.

1. Write a letter to your Head of Year giving your views on the phone ban. Evaluate the ideas in both texts and give your own views. Write 250–350 words. [40]
2. After writing, mark yourself: reading /15, writing /25, using the self-check list. [—]

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

Points a top-level answer is likely to evaluate:
- Text A: teacher reports of distraction (observations, may be generalised); silent break times (vivid, but are some students simply relaxing?); research from several countries (strongest evidence, but details are vague: which research, what age group?); calls via office (practical, but slow for urgent family contact).
- Text B: lack of consultation (fair point about student voice); educational uses (valid, but could teachers provide alternatives?); parents abroad (emotional and genuine, affects some students only); "punishing everyone" (persuasive, though the ban is not presented as a punishment in Text A); "teach us to manage technology" (strong long-term argument, links to adult life).
- Possible own view: a compromise – phones away in lessons but allowed for set purposes, a digital-skills programme, a quick-contact system for families.

Writing: formal letter to Head of Year ("Dear Mr/Ms…", "Yours sincerely"), respectful tone even when disagreeing, clear position, paragraphs that build, varied vocabulary, accuracy.
