# Composition: descriptive writing

> **In the exam:** Paper 2, Section B. You choose **one** of four titles: two descriptive and two narrative. 40 marks: 16 for content and structure, 24 for style and accuracy. Write 350–450 words in about 1 hour (10 min planning, 40 min writing, 10 min checking). A descriptive option is on every paper.

## What you need to know

- W1: articulate experience and express what is thought, felt and imagined
- W2: organise and structure ideas for deliberate effect
- W3: use a range of vocabulary and sentence structures appropriate to context
- W4: use register appropriate to context
- W5: make accurate use of spelling, punctuation and grammar

## Notes

### What descriptive titles look like

Typical styles: "Describe a place at two different times of day." · "Describe the scene at a busy station as a train arrives." · "Describe a person who is important to a community." · "Write a description with the title 'The Abandoned Garden'."

### Description is not a story

- The aim is to create a **picture and an atmosphere**, not to tell what happens next.
- Some movement or a small change is fine (time passing, weather changing, the viewer walking through a place), but there should be no plot with a problem and a resolution.
- Examiners' reports repeatedly note candidates who drift into narrative; this limits the content and structure mark.

### Content and structure (16 marks) in plain words

Top level: the description is **original and absorbing**, full of well-chosen detail, with a clear, deliberate structure that holds the reader's interest; the atmosphere is consistent and developed. Lower levels: lists of details, clichés, repetitive structure (every paragraph starts "I see…"), or story-telling.

### Style and accuracy (24 marks) in plain words

Top level: precise, ambitious vocabulary used **correctly**; varied sentence structures used for effect; almost error-free spelling, punctuation and grammar. A simple word used precisely beats a long word used wrongly.

### Structural frameworks

| Framework | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Zoom (camera)** | Wide panorama → mid-shot → close-up on one telling detail → wide again |
| **Time shift** | Same place at dawn / noon / night, or before / after an event |
| **Walk-through** | The observer moves through the place; each paragraph a new area |
| **Senses sequence** | Each paragraph led by a different sense (sound, smell, touch…) – use with care to avoid a list |
| **Circular** | End by echoing the opening image, changed |

### Techniques that score

- **Sensory detail:** not just sight – sounds, smells, textures, temperature, taste.
- **Precise nouns and verbs:** "a gull wheeled" not "a bird flew"; "the gate groaned" not "the gate made a noise".
- **Imagery:** fresh similes and metaphors linked to the mood; extended metaphor across a paragraph.
- **Personification and pathetic fallacy:** setting reflects mood.
- **Sentence variety:** a long, flowing sentence for a panorama; a one-word or minor sentence for impact ("Silence.").
- **Contrast:** light/dark, noise/stillness, past/present.
- **Focus on one small detail** to suggest a bigger story (a child's shoe on an empty platform).

### Avoid

Clichés ("as quiet as a mouse", "the sun shone brightly"), lists of adjectives ("the big, tall, dark, scary house"), over-writing with words you are unsure of, and repetitive openings.

## Worked examples

**Title:** Describe a fish market early in the morning. [40]

**Plan (zoom + time shift, 4–5 paragraphs):** (1) wide: harbour before dawn, cold, lights · (2) mid: market hall noise, auction, workers · (3) close-up: the fish on ice, one detail · (4) the old auctioneer's hands · (5) wide: sun rises, market empties, gulls take over.

**Model answer (extract, about 190 words):**

> *Before the sun has bothered to rise, the harbour is already awake. Floodlights hang over the quay like small, cold moons, and the water beneath them is black and oily, slapping against the hulls with the patience of something that has done this for centuries.*
>
> *Inside the hall, the noise hits you like a wall. Boots squeal on wet concrete. Forklifts reverse, beeping, through lanes of polystyrene boxes. Somewhere a radio is playing a song nobody is listening to. Over all of it rises the auctioneer's voice, a fast, rhythmic chant that sounds less like English than like a machine counting.*
>
> *And then the fish. Row after row of them lie on crushed ice, silver as spoons, their eyes clear and astonished. A monkfish gapes, all teeth and disappointment. Scales cling to everything – to gloves, to aprons, to the rim of a coffee cup abandoned on a crate – catching the light like loose sequins.*

*Why it scores highly:*
- **Content and structure:** clear zoom structure (outside → hall → close-up); a sustained, slightly humorous but atmospheric tone; carefully selected details (abandoned coffee cup) that suggest people without telling a story.
- **Style and accuracy:** precise verbs ("squeal", "gapes", "cling"); fresh similes ("silver as spoons", "like loose sequins") linked to the setting; sound described in detail; sentence variety, including the minor sentence "And then the fish."; present tense for immediacy; accurate, controlled punctuation (commas in lists, dashes for an added detail).

## Common mistakes

- Turning the description into a story with a plot twist.
- Listing sights with "I can see… I can hear…".
- Using clichés or a chain of adjectives.
- Mixing tenses accidentally (choose present or past and stick to it).
- Writing fewer than 350 words with thin detail, or far more than 450 with lost control.
- Paragraphs that do not move anywhere – no sense of structure.
- Vocabulary used inaccurately in an attempt to impress.

## Practice questions

1. Improve each phrase with precise vocabulary or imagery: (a) "the wind was strong"; (b) "the old house was scary"; (c) "people were walking fast". [3]
2. Write a one-sentence opening and a one-sentence ending (circular structure) for "Describe a playground at night". [4]
3. Plan "Describe a busy airport departure lounge" using the zoom framework (5 paragraph headings). [5]
4. Write one paragraph (about 100 words) describing a storm approaching a beach, using at least three senses and one extended metaphor. [10]
5. Rewrite to vary the sentence openings: "I see the trees. I hear the birds. I smell the flowers. I feel the sun." [4]

1. (a) "the wind tore at the shutters, rattling them like loose teeth"; (b) "the house leaned over the lane, its broken windows watching every passer-by"; (c) "commuters streamed past, heads down, umbrellas jostling". (Any precise, effective version.)
2. Opening: *"By day the swings scream with children; by night they creak alone in the wind."* Ending: *"By morning the swings will scream again, but for now they creak, alone, keeping the dark company."*
3. (1) Wide: the vast glass hall, planes on the runway at dusk · (2) mid: crowds, queues, announcements · (3) close: a family saying goodbye / a child asleep on a suitcase · (4) detail: a departure board flickering, names of cities · (5) wide: a plane lifts off, the lounge refills.
