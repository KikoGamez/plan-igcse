# Composition: narrative writing

> **In the exam:** Paper 2, Section B: choose **one** of four titles (two descriptive, two narrative). 40 marks: 16 for content and structure, 24 for style and accuracy. 350–450 words in about 1 hour. Narrative titles appear on every paper and are the most popular choice – which means examiners read many predictable stories. Originality and control stand out.

## What you need to know

- W1: articulate experience and express what is thought, felt and imagined
- W2: organise and structure ideas for deliberate effect
- W3: use a range of vocabulary and sentence structures appropriate to context
- W4: use register appropriate to context
- W5: make accurate use of spelling, punctuation and grammar

## Notes

### What narrative titles look like

"Write a story that begins: 'The letter had been under the door all week.'" · "Write a story called 'The Choice'." · "Write a story in which a character returns to a place after many years." If a title gives an opening line or a required event, you **must** use it.

### Content and structure (16 marks) in plain words

Top level: a complex, engaging story with a **deliberate** structure; credible characters; tension built and released; a satisfying, well-prepared ending. Lower levels: simple chronological events, too many characters, plots that rush, endings that come from nowhere ("and then I woke up").

### Style and accuracy (24 marks) in plain words

As in descriptive writing: precise, ambitious vocabulary; varied sentences used for effect; accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar – including **correctly punctuated dialogue**.

### Keep it small

In 350–450 words you can handle:
- **1–2 main characters**, maybe one minor character
- **1 main setting**
- **a short time span** (an evening, an hour, a single conversation)
- **one central problem or decision**

Big plots (wars, alien invasions, murders with five suspects) collapse into summary.

### Structures that work

| Structure | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Linear with a turn** | Opening → build-up → crisis → resolution, but with a surprise or shift at the crisis |
| **In medias res** | Start at a dramatic moment, then fill in background briefly |
| **Flashback frame** | Present moment → memory → back to present, changed |
| **Circular** | Ending mirrors the opening with new meaning |
| **Withheld information** | Reader knows less than the narrator; reveal at the end (must be prepared by clues) |

### Building tension

- **Pace:** slow down at key moments (more detail, shorter sentences); speed up through unimportant time ("Three days passed.").
- **Short sentences and fragments** at the crisis: "The handle turned. Slowly."
- **Sensory focus** on small details: a ticking clock, a dripping tap.
- **Delay:** make the reader wait for the answer.
- **Foreshadowing:** an early detail that matters later.

### Characters

- Show, don't tell: "She folded the letter into smaller and smaller squares" rather than "She was nervous".
- Use a little dialogue to reveal character – not long conversations.
- Give the main character a **want** and an **obstacle**.

### Endings

- Prepared by earlier clues; feels earned.
- Can be open or ambiguous, but deliberate.
- Avoid: "it was all a dream", sudden deaths with no meaning, a final sentence that explains the moral.

### Dialogue punctuation

- New speaker, new line.
- Punctuation inside the speech marks: *"I'm not going," she said.* / *"Why not?" he asked.*
- Lower case after the speech when the tag follows: *"Wait," she whispered.*

## Worked examples

**Title:** Write a story with the title "The Last Bus". [40]

**Plan:** Character: Leo, 16, missed the last bus after a party, phone dead. Want: get home before his mother finds out. Obstacle: dark country road, 5 km. Structure: in medias res → tension on the road → turn: a car stops; it's his mother, who has been driving around looking for him → ending: silence in the car, then she hands him his coat (she brought it) – echo of the opening (he was cold).

**Model answer (extract – opening and crisis, about 210 words):**

> *The tail-lights of the 11:40 shrank to two red pinpricks, then vanished round the bend. Leo stood in the bus shelter, breathing hard, his phone a cold black rectangle in his hand. Dead. Of course it was dead.*
>
> *Five kilometres. He had done it on a bike in daylight. He started walking.*
>
> *The road had no pavement, only a strip of gravel and, beyond it, fields so dark they seemed to have been rubbed out. Every few minutes a car came over the hill, its headlights sweeping across him like a searchlight, and he flattened himself against the hedge until it had roared past. He tried not to think about the text he had promised to send at eleven.*
>
> *He was halfway up the long hill when he heard an engine slow behind him. Not pass. Slow.*
>
> *Gravel crunched. A door opened. Leo did not turn round. He fixed his eyes on the white line and kept walking, counting his steps – twelve, thirteen, fourteen –*
>
> *"Leo."*
>
> *He knew the voice before he knew he knew it. His mother stood beside the car in her dressing gown and trainers, keys still in her hand, his school coat folded over her arm.*

*Why it scores highly:*
- **Content and structure:** small, believable situation; opens in the middle of action; tension built through delay ("Not pass. Slow."), the dark setting and the counting; a turn that is surprising but prepared (the promised text at eleven); the coat sets up a circular ending about care and cold.
- **Style and accuracy:** precise details (dressing gown and trainers – shows panic without saying it); short sentences and fragments at the crisis; a simile that fits the fear ("like a searchlight"); correctly punctuated dialogue on its own line; consistent past tense.

## Common mistakes

- Plot too big; the story becomes a summary.
- Starting with the alarm clock and breakfast – slow, predictable openings.
- Too much dialogue, badly punctuated.
- Endings that are rushed in the last two lines or rely on "it was a dream".
- Ignoring the given opening line or title.
- Telling feelings ("I was very scared") instead of showing them.
- Tense switching between past and present.

## Practice questions

1. Rewrite as "show, don't tell": (a) "He was angry."; (b) "She was excited about the result." [4]
2. Punctuate correctly: *where are you going she asked nowhere he said* [3]
3. Plan a story for "Write a story that ends with the words: 'and that was the last time I saw it.'" Give: character, want, obstacle, structure, ending. [5]
4. Write a tension paragraph (about 80 words) in which a character waits for an exam result to load on a screen. Use short sentences, sensory detail and delay. [8]
5. Identify the problem in this plan and fix it: "A spy story across three countries with a betrayal, a car chase and a wedding." [3]
