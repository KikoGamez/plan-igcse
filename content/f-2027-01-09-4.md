## Business 0450

### Formulae

| Item | Formula |
|---|---|
| Revenue | price × quantity sold |
| Total cost | fixed costs + variable costs |
| Average cost (per unit) | total cost ÷ output |
| Profit (or loss) | total revenue − total cost |
| Contribution per unit | selling price − variable cost per unit |
| **Break-even output** | fixed costs ÷ (selling price − variable cost per unit) |
| Margin of safety | actual (or planned) output − break-even output |
| Gross profit | revenue − cost of sales |
| Gross profit margin | (gross profit ÷ revenue) × 100 |
| Profit margin | (profit ÷ revenue) × 100 |
| Return on capital employed (ROCE) | (profit ÷ capital employed) × 100 |
| Current ratio | current assets ÷ current liabilities |
| Acid test ratio | (current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities |
| Net cash flow | cash inflows − cash outflows |
| Closing balance | opening balance + net cash flow (closing balance of one month = opening balance of the next) |
| Percentage change | (new − old) ÷ old × 100 |

### Worked example: break-even

A café sells jugs of smoothie for parties. Fixed costs are €12 000 per year. Each jug sells for €25 and has variable costs of €10. It plans to sell 1100 jugs.

- Contribution per unit = 25 − 10 = **€15**
- Break-even output = 12 000 ÷ 15 = **800 jugs**
- Margin of safety = 1100 − 800 = **300 jugs**
- Profit at 1100 jugs = (1100 × 25) − (12 000 + 1100 × 10) = 27 500 − 23 000 = **€4500** (check: 300 × 15 = 4500 ✓)

On a **break-even chart**: fixed cost line horizontal; total cost line starts at fixed costs; revenue line starts at 0; break-even where total cost and revenue cross; profit is the gap to the right, loss to the left. Label axes (output; costs/revenue in €) and lines.

**Evaluation points:** break-even assumes all output is sold, a constant price and constant variable cost per unit; useful for planning and for applying for a bank loan; the result changes if costs or prices change.

### Worked example: ratios

Current assets €45 000 (of which inventory €15 000); current liabilities €30 000.
- Current ratio = 45 000 ÷ 30 000 = **1.5** (often written 1.5 : 1)
- Acid test ratio = (45 000 − 15 000) ÷ 30 000 = **1.0**

Interpretation: a current ratio between about 1.5 and 2 and an acid test ratio of about 1 are usually considered comfortable; below 1 for the acid test means the business may struggle to pay its short-term debts without selling inventory. Always **compare** (with last year or with a competitor) — a ratio alone means little.

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Fixed costs | costs that do not change with the level of output in the short run (e.g. rent) |
| Variable costs | costs that change directly with the level of output (e.g. raw materials) |
| Break-even point | the level of output at which total revenue equals total costs (no profit, no loss) |
| Margin of safety | the amount by which sales exceed the break-even level of output |
| Profit | total revenue minus total costs |
| Liquidity | the ability of a business to pay its short-term debts |
| Cash flow forecast | an estimate of future cash inflows and outflows, usually month by month |
| Current assets / liabilities | assets likely to be turned into cash within a year (inventory, trade receivables, cash) / debts due within a year |
| Capital employed | the total long-term finance invested in the business |

**Exam technique:** show the formula, the substitution and the answer with **units** (€, %, units). If you get a calculation wrong but the formula is right, you still earn the method mark.

## Statistics 4ST1

### Spearman's rank correlation coefficient

**rₛ = 1 − 6Σd² ÷ (n(n² − 1))**, where d = difference between the two ranks, n = number of pairs.
- −1 ≤ rₛ ≤ 1. Near +1: strong positive (rank) correlation; near −1: strong negative; near 0: no correlation.
- Rank both variables in the **same direction** (both largest = 1, or both smallest = 1). Tied values share the mean of the ranks they would take.
- Check: Σd = 0 always.

**Example.** Six students' Maths and Science marks:

| Student | A | B | C | D | E | F |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Maths | 78 | 65 | 90 | 52 | 71 | 84 |
| Science | 72 | 60 | 88 | 58 | 75 | 80 |
| Rank Maths | 3 | 5 | 1 | 6 | 4 | 2 |
| Rank Science | 4 | 5 | 1 | 6 | 3 | 2 |
| d² | 1 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 0 |

Σd² = 2, n = 6: rₛ = 1 − (6 × 2) ÷ (6 × 35) = 1 − 12/210 = **0.943** (3 s.f.).
Interpretation: strong positive correlation — students who rank high in Maths tend to rank high in Science. (Correlation does not show causation.)

### Index numbers

| Type | Formula |
|---|---|
| Simple index | (value in given year ÷ value in base year) × 100 |
| Chain base index | (value this year ÷ value previous year) × 100 |
| Weighted index | Σ(index × weight) ÷ Σweight |

**Examples.**
- Price €2.40 in 2022 (base year), €2.70 in 2024: index = 2.70 ÷ 2.40 × 100 = **112.5** → 12.5% increase since 2022.
- Prices €50, €54, €58.32, €60 in four consecutive years: chain base indices **108, 108, 102.9**. Interpretation: prices rose 8% in each of the first two years and 2.9% in the third. A chain base index falling from 108 to 102.9 means prices are still **rising**, only more slowly.
- Items with indices 110, 104, 125 and weights 5, 3, 2: weighted index = (550 + 312 + 250) ÷ 10 = 1112 ÷ 10 = **111.2**.

### Moving averages and seasonal variation

- Use a moving average whose order matches the cycle: quarterly data → **4-point**; data for 7 days a week → 7-point.
- Each moving average is plotted at the **middle** of the periods it covers (a 4-point moving average of quarters 1–4 is plotted between quarters 2 and 3).
- Draw a **trend line** through the moving averages (not through the original data).
- **Seasonal variation** for a period = actual value − trend value. Mean seasonal variation for, say, quarter 3 = average of all quarter-3 seasonal variations.
- **Prediction** = trend value read from the extended trend line + mean seasonal variation.

**Example.** Quarterly sales (thousands): 42, 58, 66, 34, 46, 62, 70, 38.
4-point moving averages: (42+58+66+34)/4 = **50.0**, then **51.0, 52.0, 53.0, 54.0**.
Trend at quarter 3 of year 1 (halfway between 50.0 and 51.0) = 50.5, so seasonal variation = 66 − 50.5 = **+15.5**. Similarly quarter 4: 34 − 51.5 = **−17.5**.
Interpretation: the trend rises by about 1 thousand per quarter; sales are seasonally high in quarter 3 and low in quarter 4.
