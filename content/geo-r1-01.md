# Rivers: processes and landforms

> **In the exam:** Paper 1 (Physical geography, 1 hour 10 minutes, 70 marks, 40% of the qualification), Section A. You choose two of three topics: rivers, coasts or hazards. The rivers question is worth about 25 marks and mixes multiple choice (1), short answers (1–2 marks), a graph or diagram question (2–4 marks), and one longer 6-mark "explain how a landform forms" or case-study question. Labelled diagrams earn marks when you use them to explain.

## What you need to know

- The hydrological cycle: characteristics, stores and transfers (a closed system).
- Features of a drainage basin: source, watershed, channel network, mouth (plus tributary and confluence).
- Fluvial processes: erosion (vertical and lateral), weathering and mass movement, transportation and deposition, and the factors affecting them (climate, slope, geology, altitude, aspect).
- How channel shape (width, depth), valley profile (long and cross profiles), gradient, velocity, discharge and sediment size/shape change along a named river.
- Upland and lowland landforms: valleys, interlocking spurs, waterfalls, meanders, oxbow lakes, flood plains and levees.

## Notes

### The hydrological cycle

The water on Earth is a **closed system**: no water enters or leaves, it just moves between **stores** and **transfers** (also called flows).

| Stores | Transfers |
|---|---|
| Atmosphere (water vapour, clouds), oceans, ice, rivers and lakes, soil moisture, groundwater, vegetation | **Evaporation** (liquid to vapour), **transpiration** (water lost from plants), **condensation**, **precipitation**, **interception** (rain caught by leaves), **surface runoff / overland flow**, **infiltration** (water soaking into soil), **throughflow** (water moving sideways through soil), **percolation** (water moving down into rock), **groundwater flow**, **river flow** |

### Drainage basin

A **drainage basin** is the area of land drained by a river and its tributaries.

- **Source**: where the river starts (often in the hills).
- **Watershed**: the high land (ridge) that separates one drainage basin from the next.
- **Tributary**: a smaller stream that joins a larger river. **Confluence**: the point where they meet.
- **Channel network**: the main river and all its tributaries.
- **Mouth**: where the river ends, entering the sea or a lake.

Inputs: precipitation. Outputs: evaporation, transpiration, and river discharge into the sea. Stores: soil, groundwater, vegetation, lakes.

### River processes

**Erosion** (wearing away of the bed and banks):

| Process | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Hydraulic action** | The force of water pushes air into cracks in the bank and bed, breaking off pieces |
| **Abrasion** | Rocks carried by the river scrape and wear the bed and banks like sandpaper |
| **Attrition** | Rocks carried by the river knock against each other and become smaller and rounder |
| **Solution** | Slightly acidic river water dissolves soluble rock such as limestone |

**Vertical erosion** cuts downwards (dominant in the upper course, makes V-shaped valleys). **Lateral erosion** cuts sideways (dominant in the middle and lower course, makes meanders and wide valleys).

**Transportation** (how the load is moved):

| Process | Load moved |
|---|---|
| **Solution** | dissolved minerals carried in the water |
| **Suspension** | fine silt and clay carried within the water |
| **Saltation** | small pebbles and sand bounced along the bed |
| **Traction** | large boulders rolled along the bed (needs high energy, e.g. floods) |

**Deposition** happens when the river loses energy and drops its load: when discharge falls (after a flood, in a drought), on the inside of bends where water is slow and shallow, where the river enters the sea or a lake, and where the load is too much to carry. The heaviest material is dropped first.

**Weathering** (breakdown of rock in place, with no movement) and **mass movement** (downhill movement of material under gravity, e.g. soil creep, slumping and landslides) supply sediment to the river and widen the valley sides.

Factors affecting river processes: **climate** (heavy rain means more discharge and more erosion; freeze-thaw weathers rock), **slope** (steep means faster flow, more erosion), **geology** (hard rock such as granite resists erosion, soft rock such as clay erodes easily; permeable rock lowers runoff), **altitude** (higher land is steeper, with more rain and frost) and **aspect** (the direction a slope faces affects temperature and snowmelt).

### Changes along a river (long profile and cross profile)

The **long profile** is a cross-section from source to mouth: steep and concave (curving). The **cross profile** is a slice across the channel and valley.

| Feature | Upper course | Middle course | Lower course |
|---|---|---|---|
| Valley shape | narrow, steep V-shaped | wider, shallower sides | very wide, flat flood plain |
| Channel width and depth | narrow, shallow | wider, deeper | widest, deepest |
| Gradient | steep | gentler | very gentle |
| Velocity | slower (rough, shallow bed and large boulders cause friction) | increasing | fastest (smooth, deep channel, little friction) |
| Discharge | low | increasing | highest |
| Dominant process | vertical erosion | erosion and transport (lateral erosion) | deposition |
| Sediment size | large, angular | smaller | smallest, smooth and rounded |

Velocity rises downstream even though the gradient falls, because the channel becomes deeper and smoother and so there is less friction with the bed. **Discharge** = volume of water passing a point per second (cubic metres per second, cumecs). **Discharge = cross-sectional area × velocity.** It increases downstream because tributaries and runoff add water. Sediment gets smaller and rounder because of attrition.

### Upland landforms

**V-shaped valley:** in the upper course, the river cuts down (vertical erosion). Weathering and mass movement wear the valley sides back, so the valley is steep-sided and V-shaped.

**Interlocking spurs:** the river is not powerful enough to erode through hard ridges of land, so it winds around them. The spurs of land fit together like the teeth of a zip.

**Waterfall and gorge (6-mark favourite):**

1. A river flows over a band of **hard rock** lying above **soft rock**.
2. The soft rock is eroded faster (by hydraulic action and abrasion), so the hard rock is **undercut**.
3. Water falling at the base creates a **plunge pool**; swirling water and rocks erode it deeper (abrasion).
4. The overhanging hard rock is no longer supported and **collapses**.
5. This repeats and the waterfall **retreats upstream**, leaving a steep-sided **gorge**.

### Middle and lower course landforms

**Meanders** are bends in the river. Water flows faster on the **outside** of the bend, where the channel is deeper, so there is **erosion** and a steep **river cliff**. On the **inside** of the bend the water is slow and shallow, so there is **deposition** and a gently sloping **slip-off slope** (point bar). Over time the bends swing across the valley and migrate downstream.

**Oxbow lake:**

1. Erosion on the outsides of a meander makes the neck of land narrower.
2. During a flood the river breaks through the neck and takes the shortest route.
3. Deposition seals the ends of the old meander, cutting it off.
4. The cut-off loop is an **oxbow lake**. It eventually dries up into a meander scar.

**Flood plain:** the flat land either side of the river in the lower course. It is built up by **deposition** of silt when the river floods, and by sideways movement of meanders. Fertile soil makes it good farmland.

**Levees:** natural raised banks along the river. When the river floods, it loses energy as it spills over the bank. The heaviest material is dropped first, next to the channel, and repeated floods build up banks. Finer silt is spread further away on the flood plain.

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Hydrological cycle | The continuous movement of water between the atmosphere, land and oceans (a closed system) |
| Drainage basin | The area of land drained by a river and its tributaries |
| Watershed | The boundary of high land separating two drainage basins |
| Discharge | The volume of water passing a point in a river per second (cumecs) |
| Hydraulic action | Erosion by the force of water forcing air into cracks and breaking off rock |
| Abrasion | Erosion by rocks carried by the river wearing away the bed and banks |
| Attrition | Rocks carried by the river hitting each other and becoming smaller and rounder |
| Traction | Large stones rolled along the river bed |
| Saltation | Small stones bounced along the river bed |
| Lateral erosion | Sideways erosion of the river banks |
| Flood plain | The flat area either side of a river formed by flooding and deposition |
| Levee | A natural raised bank along a river formed by deposition during floods |

## Worked examples

**1. Describe how the velocity of a river changes along its course. [2]**
Velocity increases from the upper course to the lower course (1) because the channel becomes deeper and smoother so there is less friction (1). *Mark scheme style: a statement of the pattern plus a reason.*

**2. Explain how a waterfall forms. [4]**
Hard rock lies over soft rock (1). The softer rock is eroded faster by abrasion and hydraulic action, undercutting the hard rock (1). A plunge pool forms at the base and the overhang is left unsupported, so it collapses (1). The waterfall retreats upstream, leaving a gorge (1).

**3. Explain how a meander develops into an oxbow lake. [6]**
Water flows faster on the outside of the bend so lateral erosion forms a river cliff (1), while on the inside deposition forms a slip-off slope (1). Continued erosion on the outside bends makes the meander loop larger (1) and the neck of land narrower (1). In a flood the river cuts through the neck and takes the shortest route (1). Deposition then seals off the old loop, leaving an oxbow lake (1). *Use of key words (lateral erosion, deposition, neck) and a clear sequence earns the marks.*

## Common mistakes

- Saying water "evaporates from the ground" and "transpires from the sea". Transpiration is only from plants.
- Saying velocity is fastest in the upper course because it is steep. It is fastest where friction is lowest, in the lower course. Steep gradients do give high erosion power, but small, shallow channels with big boulders slow the flow.
- Mixing up erosion (wearing away) and transportation (moving). Also mixing up attrition (rocks hit each other) and abrasion (rocks scrape the bank).
- Writing "the water erodes the soft rock" with no process named. Always name hydraulic action or abrasion.
- Saying the meander's outside is deposition. Outside = erosion (river cliff); inside = deposition (slip-off slope).
- Describing the landform without explaining how it formed when the question says "explain".
- Forgetting that a levee is formed by deposition during floods, not by people.
