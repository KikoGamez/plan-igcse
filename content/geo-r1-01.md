# Population dynamics: growth, structure and policies

> **In the exam:** Paper 1 (Geographical Themes, 1 h 45 min, 75 marks), Theme 1 Population and settlement. Expect short questions on definitions and diagram reading (birth rate, natural increase, population pyramids) worth 1–4 marks, "describe" and "explain" questions of 3–4 marks, and a final extended question of about 7 marks that asks you to evaluate a population policy using a named country (usually China, but any country you have studied is fine). Population pyramids and graphs can also appear in Paper 2 (Geographical Skills).

## What you need to know

- Population growth and decline: birth rate, death rate, natural change, and how they are measured.
- Reasons for high and low birth rates and death rates, and why they differ between countries.
- Overpopulation, underpopulation and optimum population.
- Population structure shown by age-sex (population) pyramids: youthful, ageing, dependent population.
- The impacts of different population structures (youthful and ageing).
- Population policies: pro-natalist, anti-natalist and others, with a named example and its success.

## Notes

### Measuring population change

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Birth rate** | Number of live births per 1,000 people per year |
| **Death rate** | Number of deaths per 1,000 people per year |
| **Natural change** | Birth rate − death rate (shown as a % or per 1,000). Positive = natural increase; negative = natural decrease |
| **Fertility rate** | Average number of children a woman has in her lifetime. About 2.1 is the "replacement level" in a rich country |
| **Infant mortality** | Deaths of children under 1 per 1,000 live births per year |
| **Life expectancy** | Average number of years a person is expected to live |

Population **change** also depends on **migration** (see topic 02): total change = natural change + net migration.

Example: birth rate 30, death rate 10 per 1,000 → natural increase = 20 per 1,000 = 2.0% a year.

### Why birth rates are high or low

| High birth rate (e.g. Niger, Mali) | Low birth rate (e.g. Japan, Germany) |
|---|---|
| Little or no **contraception**/family planning | Wide access to contraception and family planning |
| **High infant mortality**: parents have more children so some survive | Low infant mortality: fewer children needed |
| Children work on farms and support parents in old age | Children are costly (education, housing); no need for them to work |
| Religious or cultural beliefs favouring big families | Women in education and careers; marry and have children later |
| Low status and education of women; early marriage | Pensions and welfare mean no need for children's support |

### Why death rates are high or low

- **High:** disease (malaria, HIV/AIDS, cholera), poor healthcare, dirty water and poor sanitation, famine and malnutrition, war and conflict.
- **Low:** good healthcare and vaccination, clean water and sanitation, enough food, clean and safe housing, peace.
- Death rates can be **high in rich countries with ageing populations** (more old people die) even though life expectancy is high. This is a favourite exam trap.

### Overpopulation, underpopulation and optimum population

- **Overpopulation:** too many people for the resources and technology available, so living standards are low or falling (unemployment, shortages of food and water, pressure on services).
- **Underpopulation:** too few people to use the resources fully, so the country could support a higher standard of living with more people (labour shortages, empty areas).
- **Optimum population:** the number of people that gives the highest standard of living using the available resources and technology.
- It is about the **balance between people and resources**, not about the number of people alone. The Netherlands is dense but wealthy; some countries with fewer people are poor.

### Population pyramids

A **population pyramid** (age-sex pyramid) shows the **percentage (or number) of males and females in each age group** (usually 5-year bands, 0–4 up to 100+). Males are on the left, females on the right.

| Feature | Youthful (e.g. Niger, Nigeria) | Ageing (e.g. Japan, Italy) |
|---|---|---|
| Base | Very wide (high birth rate) | Narrow (low birth rate) |
| Shape | Concave sides: steep drop-off with age | Top-heavy, near-rectangular or inverted base |
| Top | Narrow (high death rate, low life expectancy) | Wide (long life expectancy) |
| Women outnumber men in the oldest groups? | Rarely | Yes: women live longer |

How to read a pyramid in the exam:
1. Check the **base** (birth rate) and the **top** (life expectancy/death rate).
2. Look for **notches or bulges**: a narrow band might show a past war, famine or the effect of a policy (China's one-child policy shows a narrower base).
3. Work out the **dependent population**: children (0–14) and elderly (65+). The **working-age (economically active) population** is 15–64.
4. **Dependency ratio** = (number aged 0–14 + number aged 65+) ÷ number aged 15–64 × 100.

### Impacts of population structure

| | Youthful population | Ageing population |
|---|---|---|
| **Pressure on** | Schools, maternity services, jobs for school-leavers, food | Pensions, hospitals, care homes, healthcare costs |
| **Economy** | Many dependants; low incomes; may be a future workforce | Falling workforce; fewer taxpayers; labour shortages |
| **Positive** | Large future workforce; potential for economic growth | Older people have experience; grandparents help; "grey" spending |
| **Negative** | Unemployment; poverty; pressure on resources | Higher taxes or later retirement; fewer people to care |

### Population policies

Governments try to change population growth. The aim is to reach the optimum population.

| Type | Aim | Methods | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Anti-natalist** | Reduce birth rate | Limit family size, free contraception, education for girls, fines, later marriage | China (one-child policy, India, Iran earlier) |
| **Pro-natalist** | Increase birth rate | Payments for children, paid parental leave, subsidised childcare, tax cuts | Singapore, South Korea, France, Russia |
| **Immigration policy** | Fill labour shortages or reduce numbers | Work visas, quotas | Canada, Germany, Gulf states |

**Case study: China.**
- **1979:** one-child policy introduced because of fears of overpopulation and famine (population then nearly 1 billion).
- **Methods:** fines, loss of jobs and benefits for extra children, free contraception, later marriage; some forced abortions and sterilisations.
- **Successes:** fertility fell from about 5.8 (1970) to about 1.6; birth rate fell sharply; China says hundreds of millions of births were prevented; helped economic growth.
- **Problems:** **ageing population** (fewer workers to support old people), **gender imbalance** (preference for sons; more males than females), "**4-2-1** problem" (one child supporting two parents and four grandparents), unregistered children, human-rights concerns.
- **Changes:** limit raised to **two children in 2016** and **three in 2021**, because the population was ageing and the workforce shrinking. Births kept falling anyway.

Learn **one policy case study** with figures and dates, and be able to say whether it was successful.

## Key terms and definitions

- **Birth rate:** the number of live births per 1,000 people in a year.
- **Death rate:** the number of deaths per 1,000 people in a year.
- **Natural change:** the difference between the birth rate and the death rate.
- **Fertility rate:** the average number of children born to a woman in her lifetime.
- **Infant mortality rate:** the number of deaths of children under one year old per 1,000 live births per year.
- **Life expectancy:** the average number of years a person can expect to live.
- **Overpopulation:** when there are too many people for the resources available, so living standards fall.
- **Underpopulation:** when there are too few people to make full use of the resources available.
- **Optimum population:** the population that makes the best use of resources and gives the highest standard of living.
- **Dependent population:** people too young (under 15) or too old (over 64) to work, who rely on the working-age population.
- **Population policy:** a government plan to change the size or structure of its population.

## Worked examples

**1. A country has a birth rate of 36 per 1,000 and a death rate of 11 per 1,000. Calculate the natural change as a percentage. [2]**

Natural change = 36 − 11 = 25 per 1,000 (1 mark). 25 ÷ 10 = **2.5%** per year (1 mark). Remember to convert: per 1,000 ÷ 10 gives a percentage.

**2. Explain why many LICs have high birth rates. [4]**

Model answer: Many LICs have **high infant mortality** because of disease and poor healthcare, so parents have extra children in case some die (1 + 1 for the explanation). **Children work on farms** and look after parents in old age because there are no state pensions (1). **Contraception and family planning** are often not available or are not accepted for religious reasons (1). Marks go for a reason plus a development ("so…", "because…").

**3. Describe the population pyramid of a youthful country. [3]**

Wide base showing a high birth rate (1); sides narrow quickly with age, showing a high death rate/low life expectancy (1); few people aged over 65 (1). Quote a figure from the graph if possible (e.g. "35% are aged 0–4").

**4. "China's one-child policy was a success." How far do you agree? Use named examples. [7]**

Plan: PEEL paragraphs. For: birth rate and fertility fell (5.8 to about 1.6), pressure on resources eased, economic growth. Against: ageing population, 4-2-1 problem, more men than women, human-rights issues, policy relaxed in 2016/2021. Give a **judgement** at the end ("Overall, it succeeded in cutting the birth rate, but the long-term costs mean it was only partly a success"). Always include dates and figures for the top band.

## Common mistakes

- Giving birth rate as a total or as a percentage: it is **per 1,000**.
- Saying "overpopulation means too many people". Say "too many people for the **resources** available".
- Reading a pyramid by shape only. Quote **figures and age groups**, and mention both the base and the top.
- Forgetting that ageing populations can have a **high death rate** even in rich countries.
- Writing "China limits families to one child" with no dates. The policy changed in 2016 and 2021.
- Mixing up pro-natalist (encourages more babies) and anti-natalist (reduces them).
- Using the term "dependency" loosely: dependants are under 15 and over 64 only.
