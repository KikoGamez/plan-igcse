# Migration and population density

> **In the exam:** Paper 1 (Geographical Themes, 75 marks), Theme 1 Population and settlement. Typical questions: define migration terms [1–2], calculate population density [2], describe a world distribution map or a choropleth [3], explain push and pull factors [4], and a final question of about 7 marks on the impacts of a named migration on the source and destination areas. Distribution and density maps also appear in Paper 2 (Geographical Skills).

## What you need to know

- Population density and distribution: definitions, how to describe patterns, and the factors that make areas densely or sparsely populated.
- Types of migration: voluntary and forced; internal and international; rural–urban.
- Causes of migration: push and pull factors (economic, social, political, environmental).
- Impacts of migration on the source area and the receiving area, for people and the economy.
- A case study of a major migration (for example Syria to Europe, Mexico to the USA, or Eastern Europe to the UK).

## Notes

### Distribution and density

- **Population distribution:** how people are spread across an area (where they live).
- **Population density:** the number of people per square kilometre: **density = population ÷ area (km²)**.
- **Dense:** more than about 200 people per km² (e.g. Bangladesh, the Netherlands, Nile valley). **Sparse:** fewer than about 10 per km² (e.g. Mongolia, Sahara, Siberia, Amazon, Australia's interior).
- **Describing a distribution:** name areas of high and low density, give figures, mention clusters (along coasts, rivers), and give an anomaly. Use compass directions.

| Factor | Dense population | Sparse population |
|---|---|---|
| **Relief** | Flat, low-lying land (easy to farm and build) | Mountains and steep slopes |
| **Climate** | Warm, reliable rain, moderate temperatures | Very hot, very cold, very dry or very wet |
| **Soil** | Fertile (e.g. river floodplains, volcanic soils) | Thin, infertile, waterlogged or frozen |
| **Water** | Rivers and reliable supply | Deserts; no water |
| **Resources** | Minerals, energy, fish; port access | Few resources; remote |
| **Economy/politics** | Jobs, industry, trade, cities; stable government | Few jobs, isolated; conflict |
| **Accessibility** | Good roads, rail, ports | Remote; poor transport |

Example: the Ganges delta in Bangladesh is densely populated (flat, fertile alluvial soil, water, rice farming). The Sahara is sparsely populated (extreme heat, no water, poor soil).

### Migration

**Migration** is the **permanent or semi-permanent movement of people from one place to another**. Net migration = immigrants − emigrants.

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Voluntary** | People choose to move | Workers seeking better jobs |
| **Forced** | People have no choice because of danger | Refugees from war; people displaced by flooding |
| **Internal** | Within one country | Rural to urban migration in India or Brazil |
| **International** | Between countries | Mexico to USA |
| **Refugee** | Person forced to flee across a border because of persecution, war or violence | Syrian refugees |
| **Asylum seeker** | A person who has applied to be recognised as a refugee in a new country | |

### Causes: push and pull factors

**Push factors** drive people away from their home area. **Pull factors** attract them to a new area. Most moves are caused by a mix.

| | Push | Pull |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic** | Unemployment, low wages, poverty | Jobs, higher wages |
| **Social** | Poor services (health, schools), lack of housing | Better education, healthcare, family already there |
| **Political** | War, persecution, conflict | Safety, political freedom |
| **Environmental** | Drought, floods, earthquakes, famine | Safer environment, good climate, farmland |

- The strongest causes are usually **economic** (voluntary) and **political** (forced).
- **Intervening obstacles** can stop migration: cost, borders and visas, distance, language, danger of travel.
- **Rural–urban migration** is the main form in LICs: people leave farms (low income, few services) for cities (jobs, schools), causing **urbanisation** (see topic 03).

### Impacts of migration

| | Source area (emigration) | Receiving area (immigration) |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive** | **Remittances** (money sent home) boost income; less pressure on jobs and resources; migrants return with new skills | Fills **labour shortages**; migrants pay taxes; younger workforce, higher birth rate; **cultural diversity** (food, music, festivals) |
| **Negative** | **Brain drain** (skilled people leave, e.g. doctors); ageing population; fewer workers; villages abandoned; families split | **Pressure on housing, schools, healthcare**; competition for jobs and lower wages (arguments); social tension, racism, segregation; **overcrowding** |

### Case study: a major migration (learn one)

**Syria to Europe (2011 onwards).**
- **Cause (push):** civil war from 2011, bombing and destroyed homes, deaths, no safety, no jobs. Millions fled; most went to neighbouring Turkey, Lebanon, Jordan.
- **Pull:** peace and safety, jobs and welfare in Germany and Sweden; family links already there; Germany welcomed over a million asylum seekers in 2015.
- **Impacts on destination:** extra pressure on housing and services; cost of support; political tension and rise of anti-immigration parties; but also filled labour shortages and added cultural diversity.
- **Impacts on source:** loss of young, skilled people (doctors, engineers); families split; a damaged economy.

**Alternatives:** Mexico to the USA (jobs, higher wages; push: low wages; US border restrictions; remittances to Mexico); Poland and Romania to the UK after EU enlargement (jobs; labour shortages filled; pressure on housing). Use **named places, dates and figures** in the 7-mark answer.

## Key terms and definitions

- **Population distribution:** the way in which people are spread across an area.
- **Population density:** the average number of people living in each square kilometre.
- **Migration:** the movement of people from one place to another to live (permanent or semi-permanent).
- **Net migration:** the difference between the number of people who move into an area and the number who move out.
- **Push factor:** a negative aspect of the home area that encourages people to leave.
- **Pull factor:** a positive aspect of a place that attracts people to move there.
- **Refugee:** a person forced to leave their country because of war, persecution or natural disaster, and who has crossed an international border.
- **Remittances:** money sent home by migrants to family members.
- **Brain drain:** the loss of educated, skilled people from a country through emigration.
- **Rural–urban migration:** the movement of people from the countryside to towns and cities.

## Worked examples

**1. The population of an island is 4.5 million and its area is 15,000 km². Calculate the population density. [2]**

Density = population ÷ area = 4,500,000 ÷ 15,000 (1 mark) = **300 people per km²** (1 mark, with unit). Be careful with the zeros: write the numbers out fully.

**2. Explain why some areas have very low population densities. [4]**

Model answer: Deserts such as the Sahara have **very little water** so farming is impossible (1 + 1 development). Mountain areas such as the Himalayas have **steep slopes, thin soil and cold temperatures** so crops cannot grow and building is difficult (1 + 1). Marks need a factor and an explanation. Include one named place.

**3. Explain two push factors that cause people to leave rural areas. [4]**

Low incomes and unemployment because farms are small and drought can ruin harvests (2); poor services such as schools and hospitals, so families move to cities for better access (2).

**4. "Migration brings more benefits than problems to the receiving country." How far do you agree? Use a named example. [7]**

Cover **both sides** with a named case (e.g. Syrian refugees in Germany, Mexicans in the USA): benefits (fills jobs, taxes, diversity), problems (housing and school pressure, tension). End with a **judgement** and say "it depends on".

## Common mistakes

- Using "immigrant" and "emigrant" the wrong way round: emigrants **leave**, immigrants **arrive**.
- Calculating density without the unit **per km²** or dividing the wrong way round.
- Writing "there are no jobs" as the only push factor. Develop it ("so people cannot afford food and move to the city").
- Describing a map without data or place names.
- Saying all migrants are refugees. **Refugees are forced**; most migrants are voluntary economic migrants.
- Treating impacts as only negative. Remember **remittances** and **cultural diversity**.
- Mixing up source (origin) and destination in the impacts table.
