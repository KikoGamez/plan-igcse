# Rivers: flooding and management

## What you need to know

- Factors affecting river regimes: precipitation (including storm hydrographs), temperature, vegetation, land use, water abstraction, dams.
- Causes of river flooding: rainfall intensity, seasonal discharge (monsoon, snowmelt), relief, urbanisation.
- Prediction and prevention of flooding.
- Uses of water and variations in supply (shortage and surplus); water quality and pollution; dams, reservoirs, pipelines, treatment works.
- Case studies of river management in a developed country and a developing or emerging country (causes, impacts, responses, success).
- Hard and soft engineering: advantages and disadvantages.

## Notes

### Storm hydrographs

A **storm (flood) hydrograph** shows river **discharge** (cumecs) over time after a rainstorm, with rainfall shown as bars.

| Part | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Base flow** | normal, steady flow from groundwater |
| **Rising limb** | discharge increases as runoff reaches the river |
| **Peak discharge** | highest flow |
| **Lag time** | time between **peak rainfall** and **peak discharge** |
| **Falling (recessional) limb** | discharge decreases |

**Flashy** hydrograph: short lag time, steep rising limb, high peak (flood risk is high). **Gentle** hydrograph: long lag time, gradual limb, low peak.

How to read one: give the numbers with units. "Peak discharge was 80 cumecs at 1400 hours; lag time = 1400 − 1100 = 3 hours."

### Factors that affect flooding

| Factor | Effect on flood risk |
|---|---|
| **Intense / prolonged rainfall** | ground becomes saturated or can't absorb it, so more surface runoff, high peak |
| **Impermeable rock (clay, granite)** | little infiltration, so fast runoff |
| **Steep slopes (relief)** | water reaches channel quickly, short lag time |
| **Urbanisation** | tarmac and concrete are impermeable; drains take water quickly to the river, so short lag time, high peak |
| **Deforestation / little vegetation** | less interception and transpiration, so more runoff |
| **Saturated or frozen ground** | cannot infiltrate |
| **Snowmelt** | rapid melting in spring raises discharge (seasonal) |
| **Monsoon** | heavy seasonal rain, e.g. in South Asia, so seasonal floods |
| **Drainage basin shape** | circular basins bring water to one point at once, so higher peak |
| **Dams and abstraction** | dams can reduce flood peaks; abstraction lowers flow (affects regime) |

**River regime** = the pattern of a river's discharge over a year.

Human factors: urbanisation, deforestation, farming on slopes (compacts or leaves bare soil), building on flood plains. Climate change is increasing rainfall intensity.

### Impacts of flooding

- **Social:** deaths, injuries, homes damaged, people homeless, disease (cholera), schools closed.
- **Economic:** damage to property, roads and bridges; businesses closed; crops and livestock lost; insurance costs; clean-up costs.
- **Environmental:** erosion, deposited silt (can be fertile), pollution from sewage and chemicals, habitats damaged.

Richer countries: more insurance and money to rebuild, fewer deaths, higher costs in total value. Poorer countries: more deaths and long-term damage because of weaker defences, housing and healthcare.

### Prediction and prevention

**Prediction:** weather forecasting and satellite/radar data, river-level gauges and monitoring, flood warnings by text, TV and sirens, flood risk maps.
**Prevention / reduction:** hard and soft engineering, land-use planning (not building on the flood plain), flood-proof building design (raised floors, flood doors), education and evacuation plans.

### Hard vs soft engineering

| Strategy | How it works | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dams and reservoirs** (hard) | store water, release it slowly; also supply water and HEP | controls flow, multiple uses | very expensive; floods farmland and villages upstream; traps silt so less fertile land downstream |
| **Straightening/deepening channel (channelisation)** (hard) | water leaves quickly | reduces flooding locally | increases flood risk downstream; destroys habitats |
| **Embankments / flood walls** (hard) | raise bank height | protect an area | expensive; can fail; water pushed downstream; ugly |
| **Flood relief channel** (hard) | extra channel carries water around the town | protects town | costly; land use |
| **Afforestation / planting trees** (soft) | intercepts rain, slows runoff | cheap, sustainable, helps wildlife | slow to work, not enough in a severe flood |
| **Flood plain zoning** (soft) | no building on flood-prone land; use it for parks or farmland | cheap, effective | no help to existing homes; limits development |
| **Wetland and river restoration** (soft) | meanders put back, storage areas | natural, low cost, habitats | needs space, may flood farmland |
| **Flood warnings and preparation** (soft) | time to move belongings and evacuate | saves lives, low cost | cannot prevent damage |

Hard engineering = man-made structures, expensive, effective locally. Soft engineering = works with nature, cheaper, more sustainable, lower impact.

### Water supply and quality

- **Uses:** agriculture (irrigation), industry, domestic (drinking, washing, hygiene), leisure, energy (HEP).
- **Water shortage** occurs where demand is high and rainfall low, e.g. Middle East, North Africa. **Surplus** occurs in wet regions, e.g. Amazon basin, Northern Europe. Demand is rising with population, industry and living standards.
- **Pollution:** sewage (untreated waste causes disease), industrial waste (chemicals, heavy metals), agricultural runoff (fertiliser causes **eutrophication**, algae blooms that use up oxygen and kill fish).
- **Supply:** dams and reservoirs store water; pipelines move it; treatment works clean it.

### Case studies: what to learn

You need one developed and one developing/emerging country example from your own course. For each, learn: **where** (river, country), **causes** (physical and human), **impacts** (social, economic, environmental), **management** (hard and soft, who paid), and a **judgement** (success? who benefits?).

Examples you might have studied: **Boscastle, Cornwall (UK, 2004)** where about 200 mm of rain fell in a few hours on steep, saturated ground and a small river rose quickly; no one died; later a new channel, bridge and flood warning were installed. **Bangladesh** (monsoon rain, snowmelt in the Himalayas, low-lying delta, cyclone surges; solutions include embankments, flood shelters and warnings). **Pakistan 2022** (record monsoon rain and glacier melt; about a third of the country flooded, more than 1,700 deaths). Check the exact facts in your own notes and use data (numbers, dates, place names) because they gain marks.

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Discharge | The volume of water passing a point in a river each second (cumecs) |
| Storm hydrograph | A graph showing river discharge over time after a rainstorm |
| Lag time | The time between peak rainfall and peak discharge |
| Surface runoff | Water flowing over the ground surface into rivers |
| Interception | Rainfall caught and held by vegetation |
| River regime | The pattern of a river's discharge over a year |
| Hard engineering | Man-made structures built to control rivers |
| Soft engineering | Management that works with natural processes to reduce flood risk |
| Eutrophication | Excess nutrients (fertiliser) causing algae growth that removes oxygen from the water |
| Flood plain zoning | Planning rules that restrict building on land at risk of flooding |

## Worked examples

**1. Study a hydrograph showing rainfall peaking at 0900 and discharge peaking at 1300. State the lag time. [1]**
4 hours (1).

**2. Explain how urbanisation can increase the risk of flooding. [4]**
Concrete and tarmac are impermeable so there is little infiltration (1) and more surface runoff (1). Drains and gutters carry water quickly to the river (1), so the lag time is shorter and the peak discharge is higher (1).

**3. "Soft engineering is better than hard engineering for managing river floods." How far do you agree? [6]**
Strong answer structure: soft engineering such as afforestation, flood plain zoning and warnings is cheaper, sustainable and works with nature (1–2), e.g. trees intercept rainfall and slow runoff (1). But it may not be enough in extreme floods and cannot protect existing homes (1). Hard engineering such as embankments or dams gives strong protection to towns (1) but is expensive, can increase flooding downstream and harms habitats (1). Conclusion with a judgement, e.g. a combination is best, with soft used in rural areas and hard for towns at high risk (1). Add a located example for the top mark.

## Common mistakes

- Defining lag time as "the time of the flood". It is the time from **peak rainfall to peak discharge**.
- Reading a graph without units, or confusing rainfall bars with discharge line.
- Saying urban areas flood "because there is more water". The point is impermeable surfaces and drains.
- Saying hard engineering has no disadvantages. Always give an advantage and a disadvantage for each strategy, and name who loses (e.g. downstream).
- Listing case-study facts without linking them to causes and responses.
- Calling a dam "soft engineering". It is hard engineering.
- Writing about coastal defences (groynes, sea walls) in a river answer.
