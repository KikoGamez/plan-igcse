# Settlements and urbanisation

> **In the exam:** Paper 1 (Geographical Themes, 75 marks), Theme 1 Population and settlement. Questions on settlement patterns and site [2–4 marks], settlement hierarchy and function [3–4], land-use zones in an urban area [3–4], causes of urbanisation [4], problems of urban growth and how they are managed [4–6], and an extended 7-mark question using a **named city or urban area**. Maps (OS-style, Paper 2) often show settlement patterns, land use and the CBD.

## What you need to know

- Settlement patterns (nucleated, linear, dispersed) and the factors affecting site and growth.
- Settlement hierarchy: hamlet, village, town, city; spheres of influence and the number and spacing of services.
- Functions of settlements (market, port, administrative, industrial, tourist, residential).
- Land-use zones in urban areas (CBD, inner city, suburbs, rural–urban fringe) in HICs and LICs.
- Urbanisation: causes (rural–urban migration, natural increase) and rates in HICs versus LICs.
- Problems of urban growth (informal settlements, traffic congestion, pollution, services) and ways of managing them, with a named case study.

## Notes

### Site and settlement patterns

**Site** is the exact place where a settlement is built; **situation** is its position in relation to surrounding areas.

| Factor | Why it helps a settlement |
|---|---|
| **Water supply** | Needed for drinking, farming, industry (wet-point sites near rivers or springs) |
| **Flat, well-drained land** | Easy to build and farm; **dry-point sites** avoid flooding |
| **Fertile soil** | Good farmland |
| **Defence** | High ground or river bends (e.g. old towns on hills) |
| **Building materials, fuel** | Wood, stone |
| **Accessibility** | Bridging points, route centres, natural harbours, flat valleys for roads |
| **Resources / economy** | Minerals, energy, trade, jobs |

**Patterns:**
- **Nucleated:** buildings clustered close around a centre (e.g. a crossroads, church, or well). Often where defence or water is key.
- **Linear:** buildings in a line along a road, river, canal or coast.
- **Dispersed:** buildings widely scattered (isolated farms), usually in upland or sparse farming areas.

**Describing a pattern on a map:** name the pattern, say where it is (grid squares, features), and link it to a factor (e.g. "settlements follow the river valley in a linear pattern").

### Settlement hierarchy and function

A **settlement hierarchy** ranks settlements by size and importance. As you go up from hamlet to village to town to city:
- the **number** of settlements gets **fewer**, and they are **further apart**;
- **population** and the **number and range of services** increase;
- the **sphere of influence** (the area from which people use its services) gets **bigger**.

| | Examples of services |
|---|---|
| **Low-order (convenience) goods** | Bread, newspaper: bought often, near home (hamlet, village shop) |
| **High-order (comparison) goods** | Cars, jewellery, furniture: bought rarely, people travel further (town, city) |

**Threshold population:** the minimum number of customers needed to support a service. **Range:** the maximum distance people will travel to use it. Higher-order services have a higher threshold and range.

**Functions:** the main job of a settlement. Examples: **market town** (agricultural trade), **port** (trade; e.g. Rotterdam), **administrative/capital** (government), **industrial** (e.g. Detroit), **tourist/resort** (e.g. Benidorm), **commuter/dormitory** (residential). Most large cities have many functions.

### Land-use zones in cities

Urban land is divided into zones because of **land value** (highest at the centre, where accessibility is greatest) and competition for space.

| Zone | Features |
|---|---|
| **CBD (Central Business District)** | Highest land values and accessibility; **tall buildings**, shops, offices, banks, entertainment; few people live there; congested; pedestrian areas |
| **Inner city** | Old terraced housing, old factories (often now derelict or redeveloped), high-density, low-income; may have redevelopment or regeneration |
| **Suburbs** | Detached/semi-detached houses with gardens; lower density; middle-class; lower land values; schools, parks |
| **Rural–urban fringe** | Edge of city: new housing estates, retail parks, golf courses, airports; farmland; lowest land values; conflicts with the green belt |

- **In LICs:** the pattern differs: the **CBD** is still the core, but the **poorest people** often live on the **edge** in informal settlements (squatter settlements, shanty towns, favelas, e.g. in Rio) or on marginal land (steep slopes, floodplains), with the wealthy living near the centre or in gated suburbs. In HICs, the poorest are often in the inner city and richer people in suburbs.

### Urbanisation

**Urbanisation** is the **increase in the proportion of people living in towns and cities**. **Urban growth** is an increase in the number of people living in towns and cities.

- Over half of the world's people now live in urban areas; fastest growth is in **LICs and MICs** (Africa and Asia).
- **HICs** urbanised in the 19th–20th centuries and now many see **counter-urbanisation** (people moving out to rural areas).

**Causes of urbanisation in LICs:**
- **Rural–urban migration:** push (poverty, low farm incomes, drought, lack of services) and pull (jobs, schools, healthcare, hopes of a better life).
- **Natural increase:** young migrants have children; birth rates are higher than death rates in cities.
- **Industrial growth** and foreign investment in cities.

### Problems of urban growth and management

| Problem | Why it happens | Possible solutions |
|---|---|---|
| **Informal (squatter) housing** | Cities grow faster than housing; poor cannot afford formal homes; built on unsafe land, no water or sewers | **Self-help schemes** (the government provides materials, services; residents build), site and service schemes, slum upgrading (water, electricity, paved roads), legal ownership of land |
| **Traffic congestion and air pollution** | Rising car use; narrow roads; old vehicles | Public transport (metro, BRT, trams), congestion charges, park-and-ride, cycle lanes, limits on old vehicles |
| **Unemployment and the informal economy** | Not enough formal jobs for migrants | Support small businesses, microloans, training |
| **Lack of services and health problems** | Rapid growth; lack of clean water, sanitation, schools | Investment in water, sewers, healthcare, schools; NGO projects |
| **Crime and inequality** | Poverty and overcrowding | Community policing, jobs |
| **Urban sprawl onto farmland** (HICs) | Demand for housing | Green belts, brownfield redevelopment, high-density housing |

**Case study (learn one):**  A city you have studied, e.g. **Mumbai** (Dharavi informal settlement; self-help and redevelopment plans, metro), **Rio de Janeiro** (favelas; Favela-Bairro upgrading), **Lagos**, **Mexico City**, or an HIC city such as **London** (regeneration of Docklands, Olympic Park, congestion charge). For your example, know: **location, causes of growth, two problems, two solutions, and whether they worked**.

## Key terms and definitions

- **Site:** the actual place where a settlement is built.
- **Settlement hierarchy:** the ranking of settlements by size and the services they provide.
- **Sphere of influence:** the area around a settlement from which people use its services.
- **Threshold population:** the minimum number of people needed to support a service.
- **Function:** the main purpose or job of a settlement.
- **Central Business District (CBD):** the commercial centre of a city, with shops, offices and the highest land values.
- **Urbanisation:** an increase in the proportion of a country's population living in towns and cities.
- **Rural–urban migration:** the movement of people from the countryside to towns and cities.
- **Informal settlement (squatter settlement):** housing built without legal permission on land the residents do not own, often lacking water and sanitation.
- **Self-help scheme:** a scheme in which people are given materials and support to build or improve their own homes.
- **Counter-urbanisation:** the movement of people from cities to rural areas.

## Worked examples

**1. Explain why the CBD has the highest land values. [3]**

It is the **most accessible** point, where roads and public transport meet (1), so businesses and shops pay a high price to be near the most customers (1); space is limited, so buildings are tall and compete for room (1).

**2. Explain two reasons why cities in LICs are growing rapidly. [4]**

**Rural–urban migration:** poor farmers move to the city because of low incomes and drought (push) and the chance of jobs and schools (pull) (2). **Natural increase:** many migrants are young, so the birth rate is high and the death rate is lower thanks to better healthcare, so the population grows (2).

**3. Describe the pattern of settlements shown in the map extract. [3]**

Name the pattern (linear along the road/river) (1); give a location with a grid reference (1); mention another feature (dispersed farms in the east) (1). Always use map evidence.

**4. For a named city, explain how its problems have been managed. [7]**

Name the city (e.g. Mumbai); describe two problems (informal housing in Dharavi, congestion); explain solutions with facts (redevelopment plans, new metro lines; self-help schemes); and judge success (improvements but many still live in poor conditions). A **judgement** and named places reach the top band.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing **site** (exact place) with **situation** (relationship to the surroundings).
- Saying the CBD has "low land values" or houses many people. It has high land values and few residents.
- Writing "squatter settlements are in the CBD". They are mostly on the **edge** or on marginal land in LICs.
- Mixing up **urbanisation** (a proportion) and **urban growth** (the number).
- Describing problems with no **explanation** of why they occur or no named place.
- Giving only solutions with no evaluation of success.
- Failing to use map evidence (grid references) in map questions.
