# Coasts: processes and landforms

> **In the exam:** Paper 1, Section A (coasts question, about 25 marks, one of the three topics you choose from). Typical tasks: label or annotate a photo or diagram of a landform (1–3 marks), name a process (1 mark), "describe how longshore drift moves sediment" (3–4 marks), and a 4–6 mark "explain the formation of a cave, arch and stack / a spit". Diagrams with labels earn marks only if the labels explain.

## What you need to know

- Marine processes: wave action, erosion, transportation (including longshore drift) and deposition.
- Weathering (mechanical, chemical, biological) and mass movement (sliding and slumping).
- Influence of geology, vegetation, people and sea-level change on coastal environments.
- Erosional landforms: headlands and bays, cliffs, wave-cut platforms, caves, arches, stacks and stumps.
- Depositional landforms: beaches, spits and bars.

## Notes

### Waves

Waves are made by **wind** blowing over the sea. Their size depends on wind strength, how long the wind blows, and the **fetch** (the distance of open water the wind has blown across). A long fetch gives bigger, more powerful waves. This is why the Atlantic-facing coast of the UK is more powerful than a sheltered coast.

| | Constructive wave | Destructive wave |
|---|---|---|
| Height | low | high |
| Frequency | low (6–8 per minute) | high (10–14 per minute) |
| Swash | strong (forward movement up the beach) | weak |
| Backwash | weak | strong |
| Effect | **deposits** material, builds up beach | **erodes** material, removes beach |

**Swash** = water moving up the beach. **Backwash** = water draining back down.

### Coastal erosion

| Process | Description |
|---|---|
| **Hydraulic action** | waves force air into cracks, compressing it; pressure breaks rock apart |
| **Abrasion** | waves throw sand and pebbles against the cliff, wearing it away |
| **Attrition** | pebbles knock together and become smaller and rounder |
| **Solution** | seawater dissolves rocks such as chalk and limestone |

### Weathering and mass movement

**Weathering** breaks rock down where it is (no movement by water):
- **Mechanical (freeze-thaw):** water gets into cracks, freezes and expands, widening the crack; repeated cycles break the rock off.
- **Chemical:** weak acid in rainwater dissolves rock (e.g. carbonation on limestone and chalk).
- **Biological:** roots of plants grow into cracks and widen them; animals burrow.

**Mass movement** is the downhill movement of material under gravity: **sliding** (a block of rock or soil slides along a flat slip surface) and **slumping** (saturated soft rock, e.g. clay, moves in a curved rotation). Heavy rain makes it more likely.

### Transportation and longshore drift

**Longshore drift** moves sediment along the coast:

1. Waves approach at an **angle** because of the prevailing wind.
2. The **swash** carries material up the beach at the same angle.
3. The **backwash** carries material straight down the slope, at 90° to the coast, because of gravity.
4. Over time material moves in a **zigzag** along the beach.

Other transport: solution, suspension, saltation, traction (as in rivers).

### Deposition

Waves deposit material when they lose energy: in sheltered areas such as bays, when the wind drops, when there is lots of material, or when a coast changes direction (spit). Constructive waves build beaches.

### Influence of geology

- **Hard rock** (e.g. granite; chalk is fairly resistant) resists erosion and forms headlands and high cliffs.
- **Soft rock** (clay, sand) erodes quickly, forming bays and low, gentle cliffs, and is prone to slumping.
- **Concordant coastline:** layers of rock run parallel to the coast. **Discordant coastline:** alternate hard and soft rock run at right angles to the coast, giving headlands and bays.
- Faults and joints (lines of weakness) are eroded first to form caves.

Other influences: **vegetation** (roots bind soil, dunes and salt marshes protect the coast), **people** (sea walls, building, dredging, removal of dunes), **sea-level change** (rising sea level floods valleys; falling sea level leaves raised beaches).

### Erosional landforms

**Headlands and bays:** a discordant coast of alternating hard and soft rock. The soft rock erodes faster and forms **bays**; the hard rock sticks out as **headlands**. Waves are bent (refracted) towards the headland, so energy focuses on it. Beaches form in the sheltered bay.

**Cliffs and wave-cut platforms:**

1. Waves attack the base of the cliff by hydraulic action and abrasion, forming a **wave-cut notch**.
2. The rock above is unsupported and collapses.
3. This repeats and the cliff **retreats**.
4. The flat rock left behind at the base is a **wave-cut platform**.

**Caves, arches, stacks and stumps (6-mark favourite):**

1. Waves attack a **line of weakness** (a crack) in a headland by hydraulic action and abrasion, forming a **cave**.
2. The cave deepens until it breaks through the headland, forming an **arch**.
3. Weathering and erosion widen the arch and weaken the top; the roof **collapses**, leaving a **stack** (a column of rock separated from the land).
4. The stack is eroded at its base and collapses to a **stump**.

### Depositional landforms

**Beaches:** deposited sand and shingle between high and low tide. **Sandy beaches** have gentle slopes and are formed by constructive waves in sheltered bays; **shingle beaches** are steeper with larger pebbles from stronger waves.

**Spit:** a long, narrow ridge of sand or shingle attached to land at one end and ending in open water, formed where the coastline changes direction, e.g. at a river mouth or bay.

1. Longshore drift moves material along the coast.
2. At a change of direction in the coast the material continues in the original direction and is deposited in the sea.
3. The ridge builds out as a spit.
4. Changes in wind direction or wave direction curve the end into a **hook** (recurved end).
5. Behind the spit it is sheltered, so **mudflats and salt marsh** form.

**Bar:** a spit that grows across a bay and joins two headlands, trapping a lagoon behind it. Examples to know: Spurn Head and Dawlish Warren (UK spits), Loe Bar (a bar in Cornwall).

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Fetch | The distance of open water over which the wind has blown |
| Swash | The movement of water up the beach after a wave breaks |
| Backwash | The movement of water back down the beach |
| Longshore drift | The movement of sediment along the coast in a zigzag by swash and backwash |
| Hydraulic action | Waves force air into cracks in rock, breaking it apart |
| Abrasion | Waves hurl sand and shingle at the cliff, wearing it away |
| Wave-cut platform | A flat rock surface at the foot of a cliff left behind as the cliff retreats |
| Stack | A column of rock left standing in the sea after an arch collapses |
| Spit | A long ridge of sand or shingle joined to the land at one end |
| Discordant coastline | A coastline with bands of different rock types at right angles to the coast |
| Mass movement | The downhill movement of material under gravity |

## Worked examples

**1. Describe how longshore drift moves sediment along a beach. [3]**
Waves approach the coast at an angle because of the prevailing wind (1). Swash carries material up the beach at this angle (1) and backwash carries it back down at right angles because of gravity, so material moves in a zigzag along the coast (1).

**2. Explain the formation of a stack. [4]**
Waves attack a crack in a headland by hydraulic action and abrasion forming a cave (1). The cave is eroded through the headland to form an arch (1). The arch is weakened by erosion and weathering until the roof collapses (1), leaving a stack separated from the land (1).

**3. Explain why constructive waves build up beaches. [3]**
They have a strong swash and weak backwash (1), so more material is carried up the beach than is dragged back (1) and is deposited, building up the beach (1).

## Common mistakes

- Saying "backwash carries material along the beach". Swash is at an angle; backwash is straight down.
- Mixing up constructive (build up, strong swash) and destructive (erode, strong backwash) waves.
- Saying erosion "makes" a spit. Spits are formed by **deposition**.
- Missing the process names. Write hydraulic action and abrasion, not "the sea wears it away".
- Putting stack and stump in the wrong order. Order: cave, arch, stack, stump.
- Forgetting the **line of weakness** at the start of cave formation.
- Describing the landform but not saying how it forms when the question says "explain".
