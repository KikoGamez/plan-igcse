# Weather and climate

> **In the exam:** Theme 2 (The natural environment) on **Paper 1 Geographical Themes** (1 h 45 min, 75 marks). You answer three 25-mark questions, one from each theme, so weather and climate can be part of your Theme 2 question. Expect "name the instrument" [1], "describe how weather is measured / where the Stevenson screen is sited and why" [3–4], "describe the climate shown on the graph" [3–4], "explain why the equatorial / hot desert climate has…" [4] and a longer "explain" or "compare" part. Graph reading (climate graphs, temperature and rainfall) also appears on **Paper 2 Geographical Skills**.

## What you need to know

- The **weather elements**: temperature, precipitation, wind speed and direction, humidity, cloud cover, atmospheric pressure, sunshine hours.
- How each is **measured**: instruments, units, and the siting of a **Stevenson screen** (and why).
- The difference between **weather** and **climate**.
- How to **describe, draw and interpret** climate graphs and weather data (rainfall bars, temperature line, ranges).
- The characteristics and causes of an **equatorial** climate and a **hot desert** climate.
- Why the two climates differ (angle of the sun, pressure, cloud, rainfall type, ocean currents).

## Notes

### Weather or climate?

| | Weather | Climate |
|---|---|---|
| Time scale | The conditions at a place over a short time (hours, days) | The **average** weather over a long period, at least **30 years** |
| Example | "It rained 12 mm on Tuesday" | "Rainfall is over 2000 mm a year" |

### Weather elements and instruments

| Element | Instrument | Unit | How / where it is used |
|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature | **Maximum–minimum thermometer** | °C | Inside the Stevenson screen. Records the highest and lowest temperature since last reset |
| Rainfall | **Rain gauge** | mm | Funnel and measuring cylinder, in an open space, the top at ground level or a set height. Read at the same time each day |
| Humidity | **Wet and dry bulb thermometers** (hygrometer) | % | In the screen. The bigger the difference between the two, the drier the air |
| Air pressure | **Barometer** (aneroid) | hPa (millibars) | Indoors; low or falling pressure usually means rain/unsettled weather |
| Wind speed | **Anemometer** | km/h, m/s or knots | In an open site, usually 10 m up |
| Wind direction | **Wind vane** | compass point | Named by where the wind **comes from** (a south-west wind blows from the SW) |
| Sunshine | **Sunshine recorder** | hours | Glass ball burns a trace on a card when the sun shines. Open site, no shadows |
| Cloud cover | By eye | **oktas** (eighths of the sky, 0 to 8) | Look up and estimate how many eighths are covered |

### The Stevenson screen

A white wooden box on legs that holds the thermometers.
- **White paint:** reflects sunlight so the box does not heat up.
- **Louvred (slatted) sides:** air flows through freely, but direct sunlight and rain are kept out.
- **Raised about 1.2 m above the ground:** avoids heat given off by the ground.
- **Over short grass in an open site, away from buildings and trees:** buildings and trees would give shade, shelter or reflected heat.
- **Door faces away from the sun** (north in the northern hemisphere) so the sun does not shine on the instruments when it is opened.

### Presenting and reading climate data

A **climate graph** shows monthly **rainfall as bars** and **temperature as a line**.
- **Annual total rainfall:** add the 12 monthly bars.
- **Annual temperature range:** hottest month − coolest month.
- **Daily (diurnal) range:** highest − lowest in a day.
- Describe with **figures and units**, and say when: "Temperature stays between 26 and 28 °C all year, a range of only 2 °C."
- Pick out **seasons** (wet/dry, hot/cool), **peaks** and any pattern.
- **Weather maps** and station symbols can be shown on a map: cloud cover is shaded in oktas, wind arrows point toward the station from the direction it blows.

### How rain forms (needed for the equatorial climate)

**Convectional rainfall:** the sun heats the ground → air above is heated and **rises** → it cools as it rises → **water vapour condenses** into cloud → heavy rain, often with thunder, in the afternoon.

Other types: **relief** (air forced up mountains) and **frontal** (warm air rises over cold at a front).

### Equatorial climate (0°–10° N and S of the equator, e.g. the Amazon basin, Congo basin, Singapore)

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| Temperature | Hot all year, about **26–28 °C**; annual range very small (1–3 °C); night and day similar |
| Rainfall | Very high, **over 2000 mm** a year; rain in every month, no true dry season |
| Rain pattern | Short, heavy **convectional** storms, usually in the afternoon; humid, cloudy |
| Humidity | Very high |

**Causes**
- The **sun is high in the sky (nearly overhead) all year** → strong, concentrated heating, so temperature stays high with no cool season.
- Strong heating and evaporation (from sea and forest) → lots of moist air that rises → **convectional rain** every day.
- It is the **low-pressure zone** where winds meet and air rises (the ITCZ), so air rises and rain is common.
- Day length is nearly 12 hours all year, so no big seasonal change.

### Hot desert climate (15°–30° N and S, e.g. the Sahara, Atacama, Arabian desert)

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| Temperature | Very hot by day (**30–45 °C** or more in summer); **very cold at night** (can drop near 0 °C); daily range can exceed **30 °C**; annual range larger than the equator (about 15–20 °C) |
| Rainfall | Very low, **under 250 mm** a year (often much less); unreliable; a storm may come once in years, then as a **flash flood** |
| Cloud/sun | Almost **cloudless**, very sunny; very low humidity |

**Causes**
- **High pressure:** at 15–30° the air that rose at the equator **sinks** (descends), warms and holds its moisture → few clouds, so very little rain.
- **Clear skies:** the sun heats the ground strongly by day. At night heat escapes quickly into the sky, so nights are cold (large daily range).
- **Continentality:** far from the sea, so little moisture.
- **Cold ocean currents** along some west coasts (the Atacama, Namib) cool the air so it holds less moisture and rain is rare there.
- The sun is high for much of the year, so summers are very hot.

### Comparing the two

| | Equatorial | Hot desert |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature range (year) | Tiny (1–3 °C) | Larger (about 15–20 °C) |
| Daily range | Small (cloudy) | Very large (clear skies) |
| Rainfall | 2000+ mm, all year | Under 250 mm, unreliable |
| Air movement | Air **rising** (low pressure) | Air **sinking** (high pressure) |
| Cloud | Heavy | Almost none |

## Key terms and definitions

- **Weather:** the day-to-day state of the atmosphere in a place (temperature, rain, wind, cloud, etc.).
- **Climate:** the average weather conditions of a place over a long period (at least 30 years).
- **Stevenson screen:** a white, louvred box that protects thermometers from sun, rain and wind while letting air flow through.
- **Oktas:** the unit for cloud cover, in eighths of the sky.
- **Humidity:** the amount of water vapour in the air.
- **Convectional rainfall:** rain caused when the ground is heated, air rises, cools and condenses.
- **Temperature range:** the difference between the highest and lowest temperature.
- **Precipitation:** all water falling from the atmosphere (rain, snow, hail, sleet).

## Worked examples

**1. Explain why the Stevenson screen is painted white and has louvres. [3]**

White paint reflects the sun's rays so the box does not heat up and give false readings (1). The louvres let air flow through so the temperature inside is the same as outside (1). They also keep out direct sunlight and rain, which would affect the thermometers (1).

**2. A climate graph shows temperatures of 26–28 °C all year and 2400 mm rainfall with every month wet. Describe and explain this climate. [5]**

*Describe:* The climate is hot all year with a very small range of 2 °C (1). Rainfall is very high, 2400 mm, and falls in every month (1). *Explain:* The place is near the equator, so the sun is high in the sky all year, giving constant heating and high temperatures (1). The heat evaporates water and warm air rises (1), cooling and condensing to give heavy convectional rain, mostly in the afternoon (1).

**3. Explain why hot deserts have large differences between day and night temperatures. [3]**

The sky is almost cloudless, so the ground heats strongly by day (1). With no cloud to trap heat, it escapes quickly at night (1). The air is dry, with little water vapour to hold the heat (1).

## Common mistakes

- Writing that the weather is the "same as" climate. Climate needs the idea of an **average over 30+ years**.
- Saying the wind vane shows where the wind is **going**. It shows where the wind comes **from**.
- Describing a climate graph without **figures and units**, or reading the temperature scale from the wrong axis.
- Saying deserts have "no rain" or equatorial rain "all day". Say "very low, unreliable" and "usually afternoon".
- Giving only "it is hot because it is near the equator". Use **high sun angle / overhead sun**.
- For hot deserts, forgetting that **sinking air (high pressure)** is the key cause.
- Forgetting the unit: **mm** for rain, **oktas** for cloud, **hPa** for pressure.
