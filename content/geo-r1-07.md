## Notes

### The four sectors

| Sector | What happens | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | Extracting or growing raw materials from the land or sea | farming, fishing, mining, forestry, quarrying |
| **Secondary** | Making (manufacturing) and processing raw materials into goods | car assembly, steel, food processing, construction |
| **Tertiary** | Providing **services** to people and businesses | teachers, nurses, shops, banks, tourism, transport |
| **Quaternary** | **Knowledge-based** work: research, IT, development, consultancy | research scientist, software developer, biotech |

Exam hint: a research scientist is quaternary (this has been asked as a multiple-choice question). Photos of a factory floor or a car production line are secondary; a mine or farm is primary.

### Employment structure and development (Clark-Fisher model)

Countries tend to move through stages as they develop:

1. **Low income**: most people work in the **primary** sector (subsistence farming). Little money, little technology.
2. **Industrialising / emerging**: primary falls, **secondary** grows fast as factories open (cheap labour, investment, TNCs).
3. **High income**: secondary falls, **tertiary** and **quaternary** become the largest, because people have more money to spend on services and the country has skills and technology.

| Country type | Typical pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Low income | primary employment above 50% | Ethiopia, Niger |
| Emerging | big secondary share, primary falling quickly | China, Vietnam |
| High income | tertiary above 70%, primary below 5% | UK, USA |

Check the exact percentages for your own case-study countries in your notes.

### Triangular graphs

Each corner is 100% of one sector (primary, secondary, tertiary/quaternary). Each country is one dot. To read a dot, follow the grid lines parallel to the side opposite each corner. Dots near the primary corner are low-income countries; dots near the tertiary corner are high-income. A line of dots over time shows the Clark-Fisher shift. Always read **all three** values and check they total 100.

### Location of economic activity

| Sector | Key location factors |
|---|---|
| Primary | where the **resource** is (coal seam, fertile soil, fishing grounds); climate and relief; workforce |
| Secondary | **raw materials and power**, labour (cost and skill), **transport links** (ports, motorways), market, land cost, government incentives (grants, tax breaks, enterprise zones) |
| Tertiary | **accessibility and customers**: city centres, retail parks, airports; high-quality communications |
| Quaternary | skilled graduates, **universities**, science and business parks, fast broadband, good quality of life |

Factors **change over time**: coal and iron once drew factories to coalfields, but now electricity, motorways and containers mean factories can be anywhere with cheap labour or good transport. High-tech firms now locate near universities (Cambridge, Bangalore) rather than near raw materials.

### Why employment in each sector changes

- **Raw materials**: if a resource runs out (e.g. UK coal and tin) or becomes uneconomic, primary jobs and related secondary jobs fall.
- **Globalisation**: TNCs move **manufacturing** to countries with cheaper labour (secondary falls in the UK, grows in China and Vietnam). Services such as call centres also move offshore.
- **Mechanisation**: machines and robots replace workers, so fewer people are needed on farms and in factories (output rises, jobs fall).
- **Demographic change**: an ageing population needs more health care and care homes (tertiary grows); rural-urban migration moves workers from farming to factories and offices.
- **Government policies**: privatisation, closing state-owned industries, grants to attract investment, education spending, tax breaks for tech firms, or building special economic zones (China, from 1980).

### Impacts of sector shift: developed country (e.g. the UK)

- Primary and secondary employment fell steeply from the 1970s as coal mines and heavy industry closed; the UK now has roughly 80% of workers in services.
- **Positive**: cleaner environment (less pollution), new jobs in services, better paid and safer work, growth of quaternary (science parks), derelict land redeveloped (e.g. docklands).
- **Negative**: **unemployment** in mining and steel towns (e.g. South Wales valleys), the **negative multiplier** (shops close, population leaves), social problems and a north-south wealth gap, dependence on imports.

### Impacts of sector shift: emerging or developing country (e.g. China, India, Vietnam)

- Employment moves from farming (primary) to factories (secondary) and then services.
- **Positive**: higher wages, more tax money, new infrastructure, better living standards, a growing middle class, **positive multiplier effect** as workers spend money.
- **Negative**: pollution and environmental damage, long hours and poor conditions, **rural-urban migration** causing overcrowding and squatter settlements, rural areas losing young workers, inequality between regions.

Use numbers from your case study (for example % employed in each sector at two dates). Numerical data scores marks.

### Informal employment

Informal jobs are **not registered with the government**: no tax paid, no contract, no insurance. Examples: street vendors, rickshaw drivers, recycling waste pickers, domestic cleaners, small workshops. They are most common in cities in developing and emerging countries (the **informal sector** can be 50% or more of jobs).

**Causes**
- **Rural-urban migration**: more people arrive than there are formal jobs, and migrants often have low education or skills.
- **Slow economic development**: not enough factory and office jobs; people must create their own work.
- Formal jobs require qualifications, documents or an address; **lack of education** is a cause (a past exam question).
- Registering a business is expensive and complicated.

**Advantages**: provides income and a living when there is no formal job; flexible hours; low start-up costs; provides cheap goods and services; recycling and repairs help the environment; income supports families.

**Disadvantages**: low and irregular pay; no job security, sick pay or pension; long hours and dangerous conditions (e.g. waste picking); child labour; **no tax paid**, so the government cannot fund services; workers cannot get bank loans; no legal protection.

Megacity example (use the one you studied, for example **Dharavi in Mumbai**): thousands of small recycling, pottery, leather and textile businesses in a very dense area; informal workers send goods across the city and abroad. Remember to add facts and figures from your notes.

### Malthus and Boserup

| | Malthus (1798) | Boserup (1965) |
|---|---|---|
| Idea | Population grows **exponentially** (1, 2, 4, 8 ...), food only **arithmetically** (1, 2, 3, 4 ...) | Population growth **stimulates** invention, so food production rises |
| Outcome | Population outstrips food, then is cut back by "**checks**": famine, disease, war | New farming methods and technology keep pace with population: "necessity is the mother of invention" |
| Outlook | Pessimistic | Optimistic |
| Evidence | Famine in Ethiopia, Sahel food crises | Green Revolution, irrigation, GM crops, fertilisers |

On a line graph, Malthus shows the population line crossing above the food-supply line, then a crash; Boserup shows food supply rising along with population.

## Key terms and definitions

- **Primary sector**: employment in extracting or harvesting natural resources, e.g. farming, fishing, mining.
- **Secondary sector**: employment in manufacturing goods from raw materials.
- **Tertiary sector**: employment in providing services.
- **Quaternary sector**: employment in knowledge-based activities such as research, IT and development.
- **Clark-Fisher model**: the idea that employment shifts from primary to secondary to tertiary/quaternary as a country develops.
- **Mechanisation**: using machines to replace human labour.
- **Globalisation**: the growing connection between countries through trade, investment and communication.
- **Informal employment**: work that is not registered, taxed or regulated by the government.
- **Economic sector shift**: a change in the proportions of people working in each sector.
- **Multiplier effect**: when an increase (positive) or decrease (negative) in jobs in one place leads to more or fewer jobs and spending in others.

## Worked examples

**1. (a) Identify the sector of a call-centre worker. [1] (b) State one reason for growth in tertiary employment in many countries. [1]**

(a) Tertiary. (b) As people get richer they spend more on services such as health, education, leisure and finance.

**2. For a named developed country, explain one positive and one negative impact of an economic sector shift. [4]**

*Model (UK).* Positive: the shift from coal mining to services has reduced air pollution (1) because fewer coal-fired factories and power stations operate, so the environment and people's health improve (1). Negative: mining towns such as those in South Wales lost thousands of jobs (1), causing a negative multiplier as shops closed and young people left (1).

Marks: each impact = identification + development ("because ...", "so ..."). Name the country and a place.

**3. Suggest reasons why a graph shows employment in the primary sector falling in an emerging country. [3]**

Mechanisation of farming has meant fewer workers are needed (1). Many people move from villages to cities (rural-urban migration) to find better paid factory jobs (1). The government has encouraged investment in manufacturing zones, so jobs there have grown while farm jobs fall (1).

**4. Explain one disadvantage of informal employment. [2]**

Workers have no job security or sick pay (1), so if they are ill or lose their stall they have no income and may fall into poverty (1).

## Common mistakes

- Putting construction, power or food processing in the wrong sector: **making** things is secondary, **providing** a service is tertiary.
- Writing "the UK has less manufacturing" with no reason, number or place. Give a reason and a named example.
- Saying informal workers "do not have jobs": they do work, but it is unregistered and untaxed.
- Mixing up Malthus (pessimistic, food is the limit) and Boserup (optimistic, innovation).
- Answering "explain" with one idea and no development: use **because / so / which leads to**.
- Reading a triangular graph from the wrong axis. Check each reading adds up to 100%.
