## Notes

### Demand, production and the energy gap

**Energy demand** is rising worldwide, fastest in emerging countries (China, India), for three main reasons:
- **population growth**: more people need electricity, heating and transport;
- **increased wealth**: richer people buy cars, air conditioning, phones and appliances, and industry expands;
- **technological advances**: new machines and devices use energy (though some technology also improves efficiency).

**Production** is uneven. Oil and gas are concentrated in a few places (the Middle East, Russia, the USA); coal in China, India and the USA. Many countries must **import** energy. An **energy gap** exists when demand is higher than the amount of energy a country can produce or afford. **Energy security** means having reliable, affordable access to energy; countries seek it through a **balanced energy mix** (a range of sources, both home-produced and imported, so they do not rely on one fuel or one supplier).

Resources, figures and graphs: global energy consumption has risen for decades, but the **share** of renewables is growing; fossil fuels still supply most energy (around 80%). Use a resource in the exam: quote figures and years.

### Sources of energy

| Source | Type | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Coal** | non-renewable | cheap, plentiful, reliable | most CO₂ per unit, acid rain, mining damage |
| **Oil** | non-renewable | high energy, easy to transport, fuels vehicles | CO₂, spills, price shocks, will run out |
| **Natural gas** | non-renewable | burns cleaner than coal, flexible | still emits CO₂, leaks (methane), needs pipelines |
| **Uranium (nuclear)** | non-renewable | no CO₂ when generating, very high output | radioactive waste, accident risk, expensive to build and close |
| **Shale gas/oil** (fracking) | non-renewable | new supply, more energy security | earth tremors, water use and pollution risk, local opposition |
| **Solar** | renewable | no emissions or fuel cost, good in sunny places, panels on roofs | depends on sunshine (not at night), high set-up cost, panel manufacture |
| **Wind** | renewable | no emissions, free wind, can be offshore | unreliable, visual and noise impact, bird deaths |
| **HEP** | renewable | steady, can store water, flood control | dams flood land and homes, harm river ecosystems, very costly |
| **Geothermal** | renewable | reliable 24/7, small footprint | only in tectonically active places (Iceland), costly to drill |
| **Biomass** | renewable (can be) | uses waste, carbon-neutral if replanted | needs land, can cause deforestation, releases CO₂ when burned |

**Exam rule:** for "explain one advantage" give the advantage and a reason or effect: "Solar panels produce electricity without releasing CO₂ (1), so they do not add to the enhanced greenhouse effect and climate change (1)".

### Impacts on people and the environment

- **Fossil fuels**: burning releases **carbon dioxide**, the main cause of the **enhanced greenhouse effect**; also air pollution, oil spills and land destroyed by mining. But they provide jobs and cheap, reliable energy.
- **Renewables**: low emissions and a sustainable supply, but may be costly, intermittent and change landscapes. Dams displace people (e.g. the Three Gorges Dam in China displaced over a million people).
- **Nuclear**: low carbon, but waste stays dangerous for thousands of years; accidents (Chernobyl 1986, Fukushima 2011) have long-term effects.

### Managing energy sustainably

Sustainable energy use means meeting present needs without damaging the future. Three approaches:

| Approach | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Education** | raising awareness so people change behaviour | school campaigns, energy labels, smart-meter feedback |
| **Efficiency** | getting the same output from less energy | LED bulbs, A-rated appliances, hybrid and electric vehicles, loft and wall insulation, combined heat and power in industry |
| **Conservation** | simply using less | switching off lights, turning down heating, car-sharing, public transport, walking and cycling |

Apply them in **three settings**: industry, transport and the home.

### Carbon and ecological footprints

- **Carbon footprint**: the total greenhouse gases (usually as tonnes of CO₂) produced by a person, product, activity or country. Calculated as **activity × emission factor**, e.g. car journeys (km) × kg CO₂ per km, plus electricity (kWh) × kg per kWh, plus food and flights.
- **Ecological footprint**: the area of land and sea (in global hectares) needed to supply a person's resources and absorb their waste. If it is bigger than the land available, resources are used unsustainably.
- You may be given a table of activities and emission values and asked to add up or compare. Show the sum, give units, then say how you could reduce it (e.g. switch car trips to cycling).

## Key terms and definitions

- **Energy demand**: the amount of energy people, industry and transport need.
- **Energy gap**: when a country's energy demand is greater than the energy it can produce or afford.
- **Energy security**: having a reliable and affordable supply of energy.
- **Energy mix**: the combination of different energy sources a country uses.
- **Non-renewable energy**: energy sources that will run out or take millions of years to form, e.g. coal, oil, gas, uranium.
- **Renewable energy**: energy sources that will not run out, e.g. solar, wind, HEP, geothermal, biomass.
- **Fossil fuel**: coal, oil and gas formed from ancient plants and animals.
- **Sustainable energy**: meeting today's energy needs without reducing the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
- **Energy efficiency**: using less energy to do the same job.
- **Energy conservation**: reducing the amount of energy used.
- **Carbon footprint**: the total greenhouse gases produced by an activity, person or product.
- **Ecological footprint**: the land and sea area needed to supply someone's resources and absorb their waste.

## Worked examples

**1. Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of using solar panels to generate electricity. [4]**

Advantage: they produce electricity without releasing greenhouse gases (1), so they reduce the contribution to climate change (1). Disadvantage: they only generate when it is sunny (1), so there needs to be another source or expensive batteries at night and in winter (1).

**2. Suggest one reason for an increase in investment in renewable energy shown on a graph. [3]**

Governments want to cut CO₂ emissions to meet climate targets (1). The cost of solar and wind technology has fallen, so they are more affordable (1) and become more attractive than building new coal plants (1).

**3. Analyse the possible reasons for changes in global energy consumption (8 marks, with a graph of consumption by fuel, 1990 to 2020).**

Model outline: Describe the pattern using data (total rising from ... to ..., coal rising in Asia, renewables small but growing). Reasons: population growth (more users); wealth and industrialisation in emerging countries (China, India) raising demand; technology (more devices, but also efficiency); fossil fuels remain cheap and reliable; government policy and climate agreements pushing renewables; cost falls in solar/wind; energy security concerns after price shocks. Link at least two factors to figures from the graph and finish with a short judgement about which is most important. Quote evidence at least twice.

## Common mistakes

- Saying "renewable energy is free" or "never runs out and has no impacts": all have costs and disadvantages.
- Saying nuclear power "creates pollution": it does not emit CO₂ when generating; the problem is radioactive waste and accident risk.
- Writing only "it is cheap" or "it is expensive" with no explanation or comparison.
- Mixing up **efficiency** (same job, less energy) and **conservation** (use less).
- Mixing up energy **demand** and energy **production**.
- Forgetting to use the resource in 8-mark questions, or only copying figures without explaining why.
