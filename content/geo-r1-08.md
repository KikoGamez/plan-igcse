# Ecosystems: tropical rainforests and hot deserts

> **In the exam:** Theme 2 on **Paper 1 Geographical Themes** (75 marks, 1 h 45 min; three 25-mark questions, one from each theme). Expect "describe the vegetation / name a layer" [1–3], "explain how a plant or tree is adapted to the climate" [3–4], "describe the causes and effects of deforestation" [4–6], "explain how the rainforest can be managed sustainably" [4–6] and often a last part worth 6–8 marks asking you to "explain" or "evaluate", with a named example (e.g. the Amazon). ## What you need to know

- The **natural vegetation** of the tropical rainforest and hot desert, linked to climate.
- The **characteristics** of tropical rainforest vegetation: layers, species variety, and how it is **adapted** to a hot, wet climate.
- How vegetation in hot deserts is **adapted** to heat and lack of water.
- The **causes** and **effects** of deforestation, including environmental and human impacts.
- Strategies for **sustainable management** of tropical rainforests, and why they matter.
- Use a **case study** or example with place names and facts.

## Notes

### Why climate decides vegetation

Plants need warmth and water. The equatorial climate (hot, wet all year) gives the **tropical rainforest**: dense, tall, evergreen, species-rich. The hot desert climate (very dry, large temperature range) gives **sparse** vegetation of tough, drought-resistant plants. (Climate details are in the Weather and climate topic.)

### Tropical rainforest vegetation

- **Evergreen:** trees keep leaves all year, so there is no leaf-fall season; growth is year-round.
- **Very high biodiversity:** over half of the world's land species may live in rainforests; hundreds of tree species in a small area.
- **Layered structure:**

| Layer | Height | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Emergent** | 40–50 m + | Tallest trees with umbrella crowns above the canopy; get the most sunlight and wind |
| **Canopy** | 25–40 m | Dense mass of leaves; blocks most light; most of the animals and flowers live here |
| **Understorey** | 5–25 m | Shaded; young trees, shrubs, climbers (lianas) |
| **Shrub / ground layer** | 0–5 m | Very dark; little grows; leaf litter rots quickly |

**Adaptations to hot, wet conditions**
- **Drip tips** on leaves: pointed leaf ends let heavy rain run off quickly, so leaves do not rot or get covered in fungus.
- **Buttress roots:** large flared roots support very tall trees on thin, shallow soil.
- **Shallow, wide roots:** take up nutrients from the thin top layer of soil.
- **Tall straight trunks** with branches only at the top: compete for light.
- **Thick, waxy leaves** and dark green broad leaves to catch light (some trees); **epiphytes** (plants like orchids growing on branches) and **lianas** climb toward light.
- **Fast nutrient cycle:** hot, humid conditions mean leaf litter decays within weeks and nutrients are absorbed straight back by roots. Soils are **infertile** because nutrients are held in the plants, not the soil.

### Hot desert vegetation

Plants called **xerophytes** are adapted to survive with very little water and great heat.

| Adaptation | How it helps | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Water storage in stems/leaves** | Stores water through long droughts | Cactus (thick fleshy stem) |
| **Spines instead of leaves** | Less surface for water loss; protects from animals | Cactus |
| **Small, thick, waxy leaves** | Reduces **transpiration** (water loss) | Acacia, many desert shrubs |
| **Long tap roots** | Reach deep groundwater | Mesquite, acacia (roots reach tens of metres) |
| **Wide, shallow roots** | Collect water from light rain quickly before it evaporates | Cactus |
| **Seeds that lie dormant** | Germinate, flower and seed in a few days after rain | Desert annuals |

Plants are **widely spaced** (each needs a big area of soil for water) and cover is **sparse**. Animals are adapted too: camels store fat in the hump and drink huge amounts; many animals are **nocturnal** and burrow in the day.

### Deforestation in tropical rainforests

**Deforestation** is the permanent removal of trees. Brazil's Amazon (the world's largest rainforest) is the best-known example. (Other examples: Borneo and Sumatra in Indonesia; the Congo basin.)

**Causes**

| Cause | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Commercial logging** | Valuable hardwoods (mahogany, teak) cut for timber and export |
| **Cattle ranching and farming** | Forest cleared for pasture and crops such as **soya** and **palm oil** |
| **Subsistence farming** | Poor farmers use slash-and-burn to clear small plots; the soil is used up within a few years |
| **Mineral extraction (mining)** | Open-pit mines for iron ore, gold; roads cut through forest |
| **Road building and settlement** | Roads (e.g. the Trans-Amazonian Highway) open the forest to settlers and loggers |
| **Hydro-electric dams** | Flood large forest areas |

**Effects**

| Environmental | Social / economic |
|---|---|
| **Loss of biodiversity** and habitats; species may go extinct | **Indigenous peoples** lose land and traditional ways of life |
| **Soil erosion:** with no roots or canopy, heavy rain washes soil away; rivers silt up | **Jobs and income** from logging, farming and mining; money for development |
| **Flooding** downstream: less interception, more surface run-off | Conflicts over land |
| **Climate change:** less carbon dioxide absorbed and burning releases CO₂; less transpiration may reduce local rainfall | Loss of medicinal plants and forest products |
| **Nutrient loss:** soils become infertile after a few years of farming | Long-term loss of the resource for future generations |

### Sustainable management of the rainforest

**Sustainable** use means meeting today's needs without destroying the resource for the future.

- **Selective logging:** cut only mature trees, leave the rest, so the forest regrows.
- **Replanting (reforestation)** to replace logged trees.
- **Conservation and protected areas:** national parks and reserves (e.g. protected areas and indigenous reserves in the Amazon).
- **Ecotourism:** low-impact tourism that gives local people income (e.g. guided walks, lodges) while the forest is kept intact.
- **Education and agroforestry:** teach farmers to grow crops with trees; **international agreements** and certification (such as FSC wood) to reduce illegal trade; **debt-for-nature swaps** and funding from richer countries.
- **Quotas and laws**, satellites and patrols to check illegal logging.

**Why it matters:** the rainforest stores carbon, shelters biodiversity, and helps control climate and water cycles.

## Key terms and definitions

- **Biodiversity:** the variety of plant and animal species in an area.
- **Deforestation:** the removal of trees from a forest, permanently.
- **Sustainable management:** using a resource so that it continues to be available for future generations.
- **Xerophyte:** a plant adapted to survive in very dry conditions.
- **Canopy:** the dense layer of leaves and branches formed by the crowns of most trees.
- **Buttress roots:** large, wide roots that support tall trees.
- **Transpiration:** loss of water vapour from plant leaves.
- **Selective logging:** cutting down only certain mature trees and leaving the rest.
- **Ecotourism:** tourism that protects the environment and benefits local people.

## Worked examples

**1. Explain how plants in hot deserts are adapted to the climate. [4]**

Many have long tap roots (1) to reach deep underground water (1). Cacti store water in thick fleshy stems (1) and have spines instead of leaves to cut transpiration (1). (Any four, with reasons.)

**2. Describe the effects of deforestation on the environment. [4]**

Biodiversity is lost as habitats are destroyed (1). Without tree roots the soil is exposed and heavy rain erodes it (1). Rivers become silted and flooding increases because less rain is intercepted (1). Burning trees releases carbon dioxide, adding to global warming (1).

**3. Explain how the tropical rainforest can be managed sustainably. [6]**

Selective logging (1) means only mature trees are cut, so the forest can regrow (1). Replanting trees replaces those that were felled (1). Creating national parks and reserves protects the forest from clearing (1). Ecotourism gives local people an income without destroying trees, so they have a reason to protect it (1). Laws and satellite monitoring can reduce illegal logging (1). (Place-based detail, e.g. Brazil's protected areas, would secure the top marks.)

## Common mistakes

- Saying rainforest trees "lose their leaves in winter". They are **evergreen**.
- Mixing up **adaptations**: drip tips are for the rainforest (heavy rain); tap roots and spines for the desert.
- Writing "deforestation causes global warming" with no link. Say: fewer trees → less CO₂ absorbed and burning releases CO₂.
- Listing causes with no **detail** ("farming" scores less than "cleared for cattle ranching and soya").
- Describing **management** that is not sustainable, such as "stop all logging" with no reason or alternative.
- Forgetting the **positive** economic effects when the question asks for both sides.
- Giving a case study with no facts. Name the place, the cause and one number or detail.
