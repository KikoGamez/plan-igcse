# Geographical skills and fieldwork

> **In the exam:** Skills run through **both papers**: every resource question (graph, map, photo, table) tests them, and calculations are worth 2 marks ("calculate the range", "percentage increase", "show your working"). **Section B** of each paper is the fieldwork question: **Paper 1** (physical, 20 of the 70 marks) and **Paper 2** (human, 20 of the 105 marks); you answer **one** of three (river/coast/hazard, or economic activity and energy/rural/urban). It mixes your own enquiry ("State one type of sampling you used", "Explain one way you managed a risk") with **unfamiliar data** (plot a point, calculate % increase, give a conclusion, evaluate data presentation). Calculators are allowed.

## What you need to know

- **Atlas and map skills**: distributions and patterns, sketch maps, site, situation and shape of settlements; grid references, scale, distance, contours, gradient and cross sections.
- **Graphical skills**: choose and draw bar charts, pie charts, line graphs, histograms (equal class intervals), scatter graphs; **read** triangular graphs, radial graphs, wind roses, proportional symbols, population pyramids, choropleth maps and flow-line maps.
- **Numerical and statistical skills**: percentage change, ratio, proportion, magnitude/frequency and log scales (Richter), mean, median, mode, modal class, range, quartiles/IQR, cumulative frequency, lines of best fit, interpolation/extrapolation, identifying weaknesses in selective data.
- **Fieldwork (enquiry) skills**: question or hypothesis, sampling (random, systematic, stratified) and sample size, risk assessment, equipment, recording sheets, accuracy and reliability, presentation, analysis, conclusion, evaluation.
- The spec methods for the unfamiliar fieldwork contexts in each topic (below).

## Notes

### Map skills

- **Four-figure grid reference**: eastings (along the bottom) first, then northings: "along the corridor, then up the stairs". Six-figure references give a precise point (use the tenths of a square).
- **Scale**: 1:50,000 means 1 cm on the map = 50,000 cm = 500 m on the ground. For a distance: measure in cm × scale. Using a ruler: 4.2 cm on a 1:25,000 map = 4.2 × 25,000 = 105,000 cm = **1.05 km**.
- **Contours** join points of equal height; close together = steep, wide apart = gentle; spot heights give exact points.
- **Gradient** = vertical change ÷ horizontal distance. Rise 50 m over 1,000 m = 50/1000 = 0.05 = **1 in 20**.
- **Cross section / transect**: line of height (or data) along a route; check the vertical scale.
- **Describing a distribution**: say where (compass directions, regions), the pattern (clustered, dispersed, linear), exceptions, and use numbers or place names. Site = the exact location; situation = relation to surroundings; shape = nucleated, linear, dispersed.
- **Photographs and satellite images**: identify land uses, relief, scale clues; label and annotate; "oblique" = taken at an angle, "vertical/aerial" = from above.

### Graph reading and drawing

| Graph | Use | Tip |
|---|---|---|
| Bar chart | compare categories | equal-width bars, labelled axes, gaps between bars |
| Histogram | continuous data in class intervals | bars touch; equal class widths |
| Line graph | change over time | plot points then join; label axes with units |
| Scatter graph + best fit line | relationship between two variables | line goes through the middle of points, not necessarily through the origin; describe as **positive, negative or no correlation** |
| Pie chart | proportions | angle = (value ÷ total) × 360 |
| Triangular graph | three-part data (e.g. employment sectors) | read along gridlines; three values add to 100 |
| Population pyramid | age and sex structure | wide base = high birth rate; narrow top = low life expectancy |
| Choropleth | shaded regions by value | darker = higher; describe patterns |
| Flow-line map | movement (e.g. migration) | width of the arrow = size of flow |
| Proportional symbol | symbols sized by value | larger symbol = larger value |
| Wind rose | wind direction frequency | longest bar = most frequent direction |

**Describing a graph (3-part rule):** overall **trend** (up/down/stable), **data** (two values with units and dates), **anomaly** (any unusual point). Do not explain unless asked.

### Calculations (show your working)

| Skill | Formula | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Range** | highest − lowest | 16.4 − 12.1 = 4.3 |
| **Mean** | sum ÷ number of values | (4 + 6 + 8) ÷ 3 = 6 |
| **Median** | middle value of an ordered list (average the two middle ones if even) | 3, 5, 9, 12 → (5 + 9) ÷ 2 = 7 |
| **Mode / modal class** | most frequent value / class | |
| **Interquartile range** | upper quartile − lower quartile | Q3 − Q1 (spread of the middle half) |
| **Percentage change** | (change ÷ **original**) × 100 | 0.4 → 0.7 m/s: (0.3 ÷ 0.4) × 100 = **75% increase** |
| **Ratio** | simplify | 30 : 45 = 2 : 3 |
| **Percentage of total** | (part ÷ total) × 100 | 18 ÷ 60 × 100 = 30% |

The **Richter scale is logarithmic**: each whole number is a tenfold increase in the measured wave amplitude (and about 32 times more energy). A **1 : 200 flood** means the flood level has a 1 in 200 (0.5%) chance in any one year.

### Fieldwork: the enquiry process

1. **Title and hypothesis/question** (e.g. "Velocity increases downstream").
2. **Site selection**: where and why (safe, accessible, represents the variable); number of sites.
3. **Sampling**: **random** (unbiased, may miss areas), **systematic** (every nth point; even coverage, may miss patterns) or **stratified** (samples each group in proportion; need prior knowledge). **Sample size**: larger = more reliable but takes more time.
4. **Risk assessment**: identify hazards and control them (e.g. fast-flowing water: work in pairs, wear life jackets/wellies, check weather and tides; busy road: stay on pavements, hi-vis, adult supervision; sun: hat and sun cream).
5. **Equipment** and method (state the equipment and say how it was used).
6. **Presentation** of data (graphs, maps, annotated photos).
7. **Analysis, conclusion and evaluation**.

### Methods for each Section B topic (from the specification)

| Topic | Quantitative | Qualitative | Secondary |
|---|---|---|---|
| **River** | channel width, depth, velocity (float or flow meter), gradient (clinometer), sediment size/shape | annotated field sketches, photographs showing changes downstream | GIS/topographic map, river flow data |
| **Coast** | sediment size and shape, beach profile (ranging poles, clinometer), erosion of a cliff or intertidal zone | annotated field sketches, photographs showing variations along the coast | local geology map, historic maps |
| **Hazards (extreme weather)** | weather diary: wind, rain, temperature and pressure; hazard perception questionnaire; local risk and hazard maps | annotated field sketches, photos before/during/after | online live feed from a weather station; news accounts |
| **Economic activity and energy** | environmental quality survey (EQS), structured questionnaire (closed questions) | annotated photographs, interviews with stakeholders | local report on energy use, historic maps |
| **Rural** | EQS, structured questionnaire, landscape/building survey | annotated photographs, interviews | local report on rural change, historic maps |
| **Urban** | EQS, structured questionnaire, urban land use survey | annotated photographs, interviews | local report on urban change, historic maps |

### Typical Section B questions and how to score

| Question | How to answer |
|---|---|
| "State one type of sampling you used" (1) | name it: random, systematic or stratified |
| "Explain one way you managed a risk" (2) | risk + control: "Because the river was fast flowing, we worked in pairs and did not go deeper than knee height" |
| "Describe how you used one piece of equipment" (2) | equipment + steps: "We placed a tape measure across the channel from bank to bank and recorded the width to the nearest cm" |
| "Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of using annotated field sketches" (4) | Advantage: shows key features and focuses on what matters, no equipment needed. Disadvantage: subjective and depends on drawing skill, does not give numbers |
| "Explain one way you made sure results were accurate" (3) | repeat readings and take a mean; use the same equipment and method; more sites; careful reading at eye level |
| "Plot the data" (2) | accurate points on the correct axes, with a line or bar as appropriate; independent variable (distance) along the bottom |
| "Suggest one conclusion" (2) | "Mean river depth increases with distance downstream" plus a figure |
| Identify the term **anomaly** (1) | data that does not fit the pattern |
| "Evaluate how effective your site selection and sampling were" (8) | **say what you did** (title, sites, sampling method), strengths, weaknesses (small sample, bias, time of day/weather, access), the effect on **results**, an overall judgement, **and a suggested improvement** |
| "Evaluate the data presentation methods" (8) | for each method (graph, map, photo): strengths (clear trend, easy to compare), weaknesses (no scale, cluttered, too few points, cannot show change), suitability for the aim; end with a judgement |

**Reliability** = would you get similar results if repeated. **Accuracy** = how close results are to the true value. **Validity** = whether the data actually answers the question.

## Key terms and definitions

- **Hypothesis**: a testable statement that can be proved or disproved by fieldwork.
- **Primary data**: data collected first-hand by you.
- **Secondary data**: data collected by someone else, e.g. census, maps, websites.
- **Quantitative data**: numerical data. **Qualitative data**: descriptive data (words, images).
- **Random / systematic / stratified sampling**: choosing sites by chance / at regular intervals / in proportion to groups.
- **Anomaly**: data that does not fit the pattern.
- **Reliability**: how repeatable results are. **Accuracy**: how close a measurement is to the true value.
- **Correlation**: a relationship between two variables.
- **Risk assessment**: identifying hazards and how to reduce harm.
- **Scale**: the ratio between distance on a map and distance on the ground.
- **Contour**: a line on a map joining places of equal height.
- **Gradient**: steepness of a slope (vertical change ÷ horizontal distance).

## Worked examples

**1. Calculate the percentage increase in river velocity from day 1 (0.50 m/s) to day 6 (0.65 m/s). Show your working. [2]**

Change = 0.65 − 0.50 = 0.15 (1). (0.15 ÷ 0.50) × 100 = **30%** (1).

**2. Suggest one conclusion the students could reach from a graph showing pebble length falling from 8 cm to 3 cm along 200 m of coast. [2]**

Pebble size decreases along the beach in the direction of longshore drift (1), from 8 cm to 3 cm over 200 m (1), because the pebbles are worn down by attrition. *(Include data to earn the second mark.)*

**3. Explain one way you managed a risk in your enquiry. [2]**

On the beach, there was a risk of being cut off by the incoming tide (1), so we checked tide times in advance and began fieldwork on a falling tide (1).

**4. Reading a map: the contour interval is 10 m. Between two points 500 m apart the height rises from 60 m to 110 m. Calculate the gradient. [2]**

Rise = 50 m (1); gradient = 50 ÷ 500 = 0.1 = 1 in 10 (1).

## Common mistakes

- Percentage change: dividing by the **new** value instead of the **original**; forgetting to write % or units.
- Giving six-figure grid references with northings first: always **eastings first**.
- Describing a graph without any **numbers** or dates, or explaining when only asked to describe.
- Drawing a best-fit line through the origin, or joining the dots on a scatter graph.
- Fieldwork: writing generic comments ("it was reliable") without referring to **your** enquiry, sites, equipment or results. Name them.
- Forgetting to say how a weakness affected the results, or to suggest an improvement.
- Missing units (m/s, cm) in calculations.

## Practice questions

2. A river's width increases from 4 m to 7 m. Calculate the percentage increase. [2]
3. On a 1:50,000 map two villages are 6 cm apart. Calculate the real distance in km. [2]
4. Describe how you would give a four-figure grid reference. [2]
5. State one type of sampling and explain one advantage of it. [2]
6. Explain one way you managed a risk during fieldwork on a named topic. [2]
7. Suggest two ways you could make sure your fieldwork data was accurate. [2]
8. A student's graph shows no data for sites 5 and 6 and has no scale. Evaluate this method of data presentation. [4]
