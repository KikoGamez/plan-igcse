# Algebra: linear and simultaneous equations

> **In the exam:** In **both papers**, every series. Paper 2 nearly always has a pair of linear simultaneous equations ("show all your working", 3–4 marks), a linear equation with brackets or fractions (2–3 marks) and a "make x the subject" question (2–4 marks, often with the subject appearing twice or under a root). Paper 4 uses equations inside problem-solving (form and solve an equation from a word problem or a shape) and sometimes one linear and one quadratic equation solved together.

## What you need to know

- Construct expressions, equations and formulae from information given in words or diagrams.
- Solve linear equations in one unknown, including those with brackets and fractions.
- Solve simultaneous linear equations in two unknowns (by elimination or substitution).
- (Extended) Solve simultaneous equations involving one linear and one non-linear equation.
- Change the subject of formulae, including (Extended) where the subject appears twice or appears as a power or root.
- Substitute numbers into formulae.

## Notes

### Linear equations

Do the same to both sides; aim to get x terms on one side and numbers on the other.

- **Brackets:** expand first. 5(x − 2) = 3x + 7 → 5x − 10 = 3x + 7 → 2x = 17 → x = 8.5.
- **Fractions:** multiply **every term** by the LCM of the denominators.
  (x + 1)/3 − (x − 2)/4 = 2 → × 12: 4(x + 1) − 3(x − 2) = 24 → 4x + 4 − 3x + 6 = 24 → **x = 14**.
- **x in the denominator:** multiply both sides by the denominator. 5/(x + 1) = 2 → 5 = 2x + 2 → x = 1.5.
- **Forming equations:** define the letter, write the equation from the information, solve, then answer the actual question (e.g. "find the largest angle", not just x).
  Angles of a triangle x, 2x + 10, 3x − 40: 6x − 30 = 180 → x = 35 → angles 35°, 80°, 65°.
- Check by substituting back.

### Simultaneous linear equations

**Elimination** (best when neither equation is "y = …"):
1. Label the equations (1) and (2).
2. Multiply one or both so that one letter has the same coefficient.
3. Same signs → subtract; different signs → add.
4. Solve for one letter, substitute into an original equation for the other.
5. Check in the **other** original equation.

Example: 3x + 2y = 16 (1), 5x − 4y = 1 (2).
(1) × 2: 6x + 4y = 32. Add to (2): 11x = 33 → x = 3. Then 9 + 2y = 16 → y = 3.5.
Check in (2): 15 − 14 = 1 ✓.

**Substitution** (best when one equation is "y = …" or easy to rearrange):
2x + 3y = 12 and 3x − y = 7 → y = 3x − 7 → 2x + 3(3x − 7) = 12 → 11x = 33 → x = 3, y = 2.

**Word problems:** choose letters, write two equations, solve, answer in context with units.
3 pens and 2 rulers cost £4.30; 5 pens and 1 ruler cost £4.60 → 3p + 2r = 4.30, 5p + r = 4.60.

The solution of a pair of simultaneous equations is the **point of intersection** of their graphs.

### One linear and one non-linear equation (Extended)

1. Rearrange the linear equation to y = … (or x = …).
2. Substitute into the non-linear equation.
3. Rearrange to a quadratic = 0 and solve (factorise or formula).
4. Find the matching value of the other letter for **each** solution — give the answers as pairs.

Example: y = x + 1 and x² + y² = 13.
x² + (x + 1)² = 13 → 2x² + 2x − 12 = 0 → x² + x − 6 = 0 → (x + 3)(x − 2) = 0.
x = 2, y = 3 or x = −3, y = −2.

### Changing the subject

Undo operations in reverse order, doing the same to both sides.

| Formula | Make … the subject | Result |
|---|---|---|
| v = u + at | a | a = (v − u)/t |
| V = ⅓πr²h | r | r = √(3V/(πh)) |
| T = 2π√(L/g) | L | L = gT²/(4π²) |
| y = 3x² − 5 | x | x = ±√((y + 5)/3) |

**Subject appearing twice** — collect, factorise, divide:
1. Clear fractions (multiply out).
2. Expand brackets.
3. Get all terms with the subject on one side, everything else on the other.
4. **Factorise** the subject out.
5. Divide by the bracket.

Example: make b the subject of a = (b + 2)/(b − 3).
ab − 3a = b + 2 → ab − b = 3a + 2 → b(a − 1) = 3a + 2 → **b = (3a + 2)/(a − 1)**.

Roots and squares: square both sides to remove a square root; square-root both sides to remove a square (± if the question allows negative values).

## Worked examples

**1.** Solve (x + 1)/3 − (x − 2)/4 = 2. [3]

- 4(x + 1) − 3(x − 2) = 24 — **M1**
- 4x + 4 − 3x + 6 = 24 — **M1**
- **x = 14** — **A1**

**2.** Solve the simultaneous equations. You must show all your working. [3]
3x + 2y = 16
5x − 4y = 1

- correctly multiplying to make the coefficients of x or y the same, and adding or subtracting appropriately — **M1**
- **x = 3** — **A1**
- **y = 3.5** — **A1**

**3.** Make b the subject of the formula a = (b + 2)/(b − 3). [4]

- a(b − 3) = b + 2 — **M1**
- ab − b = 3a + 2 (subject terms collected) — **M1**
- b(a − 1) = 3a + 2 — **M1**
- **b = (3a + 2)/(a − 1)** — **A1**

**4.** Solve the simultaneous equations y = x + 1 and x² + y² = 13. [5]

- x² + (x + 1)² = 13 — **M1**
- x² + x − 6 = 0 (or 2x² + 2x − 12 = 0) — **A1**
- (x + 3)(x − 2) = 0 — **M1**
- **x = 2, y = 3** and **x = −3, y = −2** — **A2** (A1 for both x values only)

## Common mistakes

- Multiplying only some terms by the LCM when clearing fractions (the number on the right is forgotten).
- Sign error with a minus in front of a fraction: −(x − 2) becomes −x + 2, not −x − 2.
- Simultaneous equations: subtracting when the signs are different, or the arithmetic of negatives (−4y − (−4y)).
- Only finding x; or not showing working on Paper 2 (an answer by trial earns no marks when working is demanded).
- In linear/non-linear pairs, giving x values without the corresponding y values, or pairing them wrongly.
- Rearranging: making errors when the subject appears twice because the factorising step is skipped.
- Writing √(a + b) = √a + √b while rearranging.

## Practice questions

1. Solve 7 − 2x = 3(x + 4). [2]
2. Solve the simultaneous equations 2x + 3y = 12 and 3x − y = 7. [3]
3. 3 pens and 2 rulers cost £4.30. 5 pens and 1 ruler cost £4.60. Find the cost of one pen and of one ruler. [4]
4. Make L the subject of T = 2π√(L/g). [3]
5. Make x the subject of 3x + a = bx − 5. [3]
6. Solve 5/(x + 1) = 2. [2]
7. Solve the simultaneous equations y = 2x − 1 and y = x² − 4. [5]
8. The angles of a triangle are x°, (2x + 10)° and (3x − 40)°. Find the size of the largest angle. [3]

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. 7 − 2x = 3x + 12 → −5 = 5x → **x = −1**
2. y = 3x − 7 → 2x + 9x − 21 = 12 → **x = 3, y = 2**
3. 3p + 2r = 4.30, 5p + r = 4.60 → 10p + 2r = 9.20 → 7p = 4.90 → **pen £0.70, ruler £1.10**
4. T/(2π) = √(L/g) → T²/(4π²) = L/g → **L = gT²/(4π²)**
5. 3x − bx = −5 − a → x(3 − b) = −5 − a → **x = (a + 5)/(b − 3)** (or (−5 − a)/(3 − b))
6. 5 = 2x + 2 → **x = 1.5**
7. x² − 4 = 2x − 1 → x² − 2x − 3 = 0 → (x − 3)(x + 1) = 0 → **x = 3, y = 5** and **x = −1, y = −3**
8. 6x − 30 = 180 → x = 35 → angles 35°, 80°, 65° → **largest = 80°**
