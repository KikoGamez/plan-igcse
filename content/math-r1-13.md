# Geometry: similarity, congruence and constructions

> **In the exam:** Similarity appears in most series. Paper 2: lengths in similar triangles, or area/volume scale factors with nice numbers (3–4 marks). Paper 4: similar solids (volume, surface area, mass, capacity) worth 3–5 marks, often inside a longer mensuration question. Constructions (a triangle from three sides with ruler and compasses) and scale drawings appear less often, usually 2–3 marks.

## What you need to know

- Use and interpret the terms similar and congruent; recognise congruent shapes.
- Calculate lengths of similar shapes.
- Extended: use the relationships between lengths and areas of similar shapes, and between lengths, surface areas and volumes of similar solids.
- Extended: solve problems and give simple explanations involving similarity.
- Measure and draw lines and angles; construct a triangle given the lengths of all three sides, using a ruler and a pair of compasses only.
- Draw and interpret scale drawings, and use scales on maps and plans.
- Draw, use and interpret nets.

## Notes

### Congruent and similar

- **Congruent** shapes are identical in shape and size: corresponding sides are equal and corresponding angles are equal (a reflected copy is still congruent).
- **Similar** shapes have the same shape but different size: corresponding angles are equal and corresponding sides are in the same ratio.
- Two triangles are similar if their angles are equal (two pairs of equal angles is enough, because the third angle must then be equal too).

### Finding lengths in similar shapes

1. Match corresponding vertices (equal angles). Redraw the two triangles separately, the same way round, if they overlap.
2. Linear scale factor k = (length on the big shape) ÷ (corresponding length on the small shape).
3. Big length = small length × k; small length = big length ÷ k.

Typical overlapping diagram: DE is parallel to BC, with D on AB and E on AC. Then triangle ADE is similar to triangle ABC (corresponding angles are equal). Be careful: use AB, not DB, as the side of the big triangle.

### Area and volume scale factors

If the linear scale factor is k:

| Quantity | Scale factor |
|---|---|
| Length (height, radius, perimeter) | k |
| Area (surface area, area of label, paint needed) | k² |
| Volume (capacity, mass of the same material) | k³ |

Going backwards:
- Given an area ratio, linear factor k = √(area ratio).
- Given a volume ratio, linear factor k = ∛(volume ratio).
- From volume to area: k = ∛(volume ratio), then area factor = k².

Example: volumes 54 cm³ and 250 cm³ → volume ratio 250/54 = 125/27 → k = 5/3 → area factor = 25/9.

### Maps and scales

- Scale 1 : 25 000 means 1 cm on the map represents 25 000 cm = 250 m in real life.
- Areas on maps: multiply by (25 000)², then convert units carefully (1 m² = 10 000 cm², 1 km² = 1 000 000 m²).

### Constructing a triangle from three sides (ruler and compasses only)

Example: triangle ABC with AB = 7 cm, AC = 6 cm, BC = 5 cm.
1. Draw AB = 7 cm with a ruler.
2. Set the compasses to 6 cm, put the point on A and draw an arc.
3. Set the compasses to 5 cm, put the point on B and draw an arc crossing the first.
4. The intersection is C. Join AC and BC.
5. **Leave all construction arcs visible** — they earn the marks.

Accuracy expected: lengths within ±2 mm, angles within ±2°.

### Scale drawings and nets

- Convert real distances to drawing distances using the scale, then measure accurately.
- A net is a 2D shape that folds into a solid. A cube has 11 different nets. Check that faces that meet along an edge have equal edge lengths.

## Worked examples

**1.** In triangle ABC, D is on AB and E is on AC, with DE parallel to BC. AD = 4 cm, DB = 6 cm and DE = 5 cm.
(a) Find BC. [2]
(b) The area of triangle ADE is 12 cm². Find the area of the trapezium DBCE. [3]

(a) AB = 4 + 6 = 10, so k = 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5 M1; BC = 5 × 2.5 = **12.5 cm** A1
(b) Area factor = 2.5² = 6.25 M1; area ABC = 12 × 6.25 = 75 M1; DBCE = 75 − 12 = **63 cm²** A1

**2.** Two mathematically similar cylinders have heights 6 cm and 9 cm. The volume of the smaller cylinder is 160 cm³. Find the volume of the larger cylinder. [2]

k = 9 ÷ 6 = 1.5; volume = 160 × 1.5³ M1 = **540 cm³** A1

**3.** Two similar bottles have surface areas 75 cm² and 300 cm². The smaller bottle holds 250 ml. How much does the larger bottle hold? [3]

Area factor = 300 ÷ 75 = 4 → k = √4 = 2 M1; volume factor = 2³ = 8 M1; 250 × 8 = **2000 ml** A1

## Common mistakes

- Using DB instead of AB as the side of the large triangle in an overlapping diagram.
- Multiplying an area or volume by k instead of k² or k³.
- Going from area to volume by multiplying the area factor by k without first finding k (e.g. treating an area ratio of 4 as a volume ratio of 8 without justification — find k = 2 first).
- Not matching corresponding sides: always match by equal angles, not by position on the page.
- Rubbing out construction arcs, or drawing the triangle freehand.
- Map area problems: dividing by 25 000 instead of 25 000², or converting cm² to m² by dividing by 100.

## Practice questions

1. Triangle P has sides 6 cm, 8 cm and 10 cm. Triangle Q is similar to P and its longest side is 25 cm. Find the other two sides of Q. [2]
2. A map has scale 1 : 25 000.
 (a) Two towns are 8 cm apart on the map. Find the real distance in km. [2]
 (b) A lake has an area of 3 cm² on the map. Find its real area in km². [2]
3. Two similar solids have volumes 54 cm³ and 250 cm³. The surface area of the smaller solid is 72 cm². Find the surface area of the larger solid. [3]
4. Two similar cones have heights 10 cm and 15 cm. Write down the ratio of their volumes in its simplest form. [2]
5. A model car is made to a scale of 1 : 40. The real car is 4.2 m long.
 (a) Find the length of the model in cm. [1]
 (b) The model needs 15 cm² of paint for its roof. Find the area of the real roof in m². [2]
6. Using a ruler and compasses only, construct triangle XYZ with XY = 8 cm, XZ = 6.5 cm and YZ = 5 cm. Then measure angle YXZ. [3]
7. In triangle ABC, D is on AB and E is on AC with DE parallel to BC. AD = 3 cm, AB = 7.5 cm and BC = 9 cm. Find DE. [2]
8. Two similar jugs have heights 12 cm and 18 cm. The larger jug has mass 810 g. Both are made of the same material. Find the mass of the smaller jug. [3]

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. k = 25 ÷ 10 = 2.5: **15 cm and 20 cm**
2. (a) 8 × 25 000 = 200 000 cm = **2 km** (b) 3 × 25 000² = 1 875 000 000 cm² = 187 500 m² = **0.1875 km²**
3. Volume ratio 250/54 = 125/27 → k = 5/3 → area factor 25/9; 72 × 25/9 = **200 cm²**
4. k = 1.5 → 1.5³ = 27/8 → **8 : 27**
5. (a) 420 ÷ 40 = **10.5 cm** (b) 15 × 40² = 24 000 cm² = **2.4 m²**
6. Arcs of radius 6.5 cm from X and 5 cm from Y must be visible. Angle YXZ ≈ **39°** (accurate value 38.6°, so a measurement of about 37°–40° is fine)
7. k = 7.5 ÷ 3 = 2.5; DE = 9 ÷ 2.5 = **3.6 cm**
8. k = 18 ÷ 12 = 1.5; volume (mass) factor 3.375; 810 ÷ 3.375 = **240 g**
