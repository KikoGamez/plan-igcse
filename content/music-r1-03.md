# Elements: harmony, tonality and cadences

> **In the exam:** Paper 1 (70 marks). Common questions: "Name the chord (I, IV, V, V7…) at the marked bar" [1], "Which cadence ends the phrase?" [1] (perfect, imperfect or interrupted, sometimes with a printed score), "Does the music modulate? To which key?" [1 to 2], "Is the harmony consonant or dissonant, diatonic or chromatic?" [1], "Name the device in the bass (tonic pedal, dominant pedal)" [1], and descriptive marks for "tonality and harmony" in a 4 to 8 mark description.

## What you need to know

- Tonality: major and minor; modulation to related keys (dominant, subdominant, relative major, relative minor); tonic major and tonic minor.
- Harmony: primary and secondary chords; dominant seventh and diminished seventh.
- Cadences: perfect, imperfect and interrupted.
- Consonant and dissonant; diatonic and chromatic harmony.
- Suspensions; tonic pedal and dominant pedal.
- Related texture and device words: drone, parallel motion, cluster chords (see topic 5).

## Notes

### 1. Chords and chord numbers

A **triad** is three notes built in thirds from a root. In a key, each scale degree gives a chord, numbered with Roman numerals. In C major:

| Chord | Notes | Type | Name |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | C E G | major | Tonic (**primary**) |
| ii | D F A | minor | Supertonic (**secondary**) |
| iii | E G B | minor | Mediant (secondary) |
| **IV** | F A C | major | Subdominant (**primary**) |
| **V** | G B D | major | Dominant (**primary**) |
| vi | A C E | minor | Submediant (secondary) |
| vii° | B D F | diminished | Leading-note chord |

The **primary chords** are I, IV and V; most folk and pop songs use little else (I–IV–V–I; I–V–vi–IV). **Secondary chords** are ii, iii and vi (and vii°). In a minor key the I chord is minor, and V is made **major** by the raised 7th (A minor: V is E G♯ B).

**Dominant seventh (V7):** V with a minor 7th added on top (G B D **F** in C major). It creates a strong pull to I and is common in Classical music.

**Diminished seventh:** four notes built from stacked minor 3rds (e.g. B D F A♭). It sounds tense and unstable, perfect for drama (Romantic music, silent-film style, horror in film music) and often leads into a V chord.

### 2. Consonance, dissonance, diatonic, chromatic

- **Consonant:** stable, pleasant, "at rest" (major and minor triads).
- **Dissonant:** tense, clashing, needs to resolve (a V7, a diminished 7th, a cluster).
- **Diatonic harmony:** uses only the notes of the key.
- **Chromatic harmony:** uses notes outside the key (accidentals); richer and more colourful, typical of the Romantic period.

A **suspension** is a note held over from the previous chord that clashes with the new chord and then falls by step to resolve (prepare, suspend, resolve). Common in Baroque slow movements, and a good dissonance to quote.

A **pedal** is a long-held note in the bass while the chords above change. A **tonic pedal** is the key note (I); a **dominant pedal** is the 5th note and builds tension before a return (very common before a recapitulation). A **drone** is a continuous held note or fifth (bagpipes, Indian tanpura).

### 3. Cadences

A **cadence** is two chords that end a phrase, like punctuation.

| Cadence | Chords | Effect | Say it like this |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Perfect** | V → I (or V7 → I) | Finished, full stop | "sounds final and complete" |
| **Imperfect** | ends on **V** (I → V, ii → V, IV → V) | Unfinished, comma/question | "stops on the dominant, expects more" |
| **Interrupted** | V → vi | Surprise; "expected I, got vi" | "sounds like a deceptive swerve" |

(You may also hear a plagal cadence, IV → I, "Amen". The Cambridge list names perfect, imperfect and interrupted.)

In a printed score, find the last two chords of a phrase, work out their roots and number them in the key. Example in C major: bass G then C = V–I = perfect. Bass G then A (with A C E) = V–vi = interrupted. Bass C or D or F then G = imperfect.

### 4. Tonality and modulation

**Tonality** is the key a piece is centred on. **Major** keys sound bright; **minor** keys darker or sadder.

**Modulation** is a move from the home key to a new key. In the exam you need the **related keys**:

| From C major | Name | Clue in the music |
|---|---|---|
| G major | **Dominant** | F♯ appears |
| F major | **Subdominant** | B♭ appears |
| A minor | **Relative minor** | G♯ appears (raised 7th) |

From a minor key (e.g. A minor), related keys are C major (relative major), E minor (dominant) and D minor (subdominant). **Tonic major/minor** means the same tonic with a change of mode (C minor to C major, e.g. a "happy ending" in a minor piece, or vice versa).

How to notice a modulation by ear: the music suddenly feels like it has a new "home" note; often a perfect cadence in the new key confirms it. In Baroque and Classical music the second theme or section is often in the dominant (or relative major for a minor-key piece).

### 5. Writing about harmony

Use the vocabulary precisely: "The harmony is mostly **diatonic** with **primary chords** I, IV and V [1]; there is a **perfect cadence** at the end of the first phrase [1]; a **dominant pedal** builds tension before the return [1]." Avoid just "the chords change".

## Key terms and definitions

- **Triad:** a chord of three notes: root, 3rd and 5th.
- **Primary chords:** chords I, IV and V.
- **Secondary chords:** chords ii, iii and vi (and vii°).
- **Cadence:** a pair of chords at the end of a phrase.
- **Perfect cadence:** chord V followed by chord I.
- **Imperfect cadence:** a cadence ending on chord V.
- **Interrupted cadence:** chord V followed by chord vi.
- **Modulation:** a change of key within a piece.
- **Tonic pedal / dominant pedal:** a held or repeated note on the 1st / 5th degree in the bass while the harmony above changes.
- **Suspension:** a note held over into the next chord so it clashes, then resolves downwards by step.
- **Diatonic / chromatic:** using notes of the key only / using notes outside the key.

## Worked examples

**1. A phrase ends with the bass G followed by A (A C E above it) in C major. Name the cadence. [2]**
Chords are V then vi [1]: an **interrupted cadence** [1].

**2. In a piece in G major, F♯ is the 7th degree. Later you hear many C♯ notes and a perfect cadence in D major. What has happened? [2]**
There is a **modulation** [1] to the **dominant** key, D major [1].

**3. "Describe the harmony of the extract." (Baroque slow movement.) [4]**
"The harmony is **diatonic** [1] and mainly in a **minor key** [1]. Several **suspensions** create dissonance that resolves [1], and the phrases end with **perfect cadences** [1]."

## Common mistakes

- Calling any cadence "perfect" because it "sounds good". A perfect cadence is V–I only.
- Confusing imperfect (ends on V) with interrupted (V–vi).
- Calling V–I in a minor key "minor V". The V chord in the minor is normally major (raised 7th).
- Writing "key change" with no direction. Name it: dominant, subdominant, relative minor/major.
- Using "dissonant" to mean "bad" or "diatonic" to mean "simple". Use the definitions.
- Mixing up pedal (held bass note, chords change above) with drone (continuous, usually throughout).
