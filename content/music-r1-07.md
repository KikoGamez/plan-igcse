# Western classical music: Baroque, Classical and Romantic

> **In the exam:** Paper 3 (Appraising, 1 h 45 min, 40% of the course) asks you to recognise features of music from different periods in the unfamiliar-music questions, and to place your set works in their period. Expect short answers (1–2 marks: "name the period", "give two features that suggest this period") and a longer extended response (up to 12 marks) where period context helps you compare pieces.

## What you need to know

- The approximate dates of the **Baroque** (c.1600–1750), **Classical** (c.1750–1820) and **Romantic** (c.1820–1900) periods.
- The typical musical features of each: texture, harmony, instruments, forms, dynamics, melody.
- Key forms and genres: concerto grosso, fugue, suite, sonata, symphony, string quartet, lied, character piece, programme music.
- Famous composers of each period and how to tell the periods apart by ear.
- How your instrumental and vocal set works fit into these periods (for example Bach, Beethoven and Purcell).

## Notes

### The big picture

| | **Baroque** | **Classical** | **Romantic** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dates | c.1600–1750 | c.1750–1820 | c.1820–1900 |
| Keyword | **Ornate, contrapuntal** | **Balanced, clear** | **Expressive, dramatic** |
| Texture | Polyphonic / contrapuntal (fugue, imitation); **basso continuo** | Mostly **homophonic**: tune + accompaniment | Rich homophony with some counterpoint; thick, lush |
| Melody | Long, flowing, decorated lines; sequences | **Balanced phrases** (4 + 4 bars, question and answer) | Long, lyrical, emotional; wide range |
| Harmony | Functional major/minor; sequences; suspensions | Simple and clear: I, IV, V; **perfect cadences**; Alberti bass | **Chromatic**; dissonance; sudden or remote modulations |
| Dynamics | **Terraced** (sudden louder/quieter, no gradual change) | **Crescendo and diminuendo** become common | Extreme range (ppp–fff); big contrasts; **rubato** |
| Orchestra | Small: strings + continuo (harpsichord/organ), some wind and trumpets | Larger, standard: strings, pairs of wind, horns, trumpets, timpani; clarinet added | Very large: more brass, percussion, harp; piano is a major solo instrument |
| Composers | Bach, Handel, Vivaldi, Purcell | Haydn, Mozart, early Beethoven | Chopin, Schumann, Schubert, Liszt, Brahms, Tchaikovsky, Wagner |

### Baroque (c.1600–1750)

- **Basso continuo (figured bass):** a bass line played by a cello/bass viol with a chord instrument (harpsichord, organ or lute). It is the "engine room" of nearly all Baroque music.
- **Polyphony and counterpoint:** several independent melodies at once. **Fugue**: a subject enters in turn in each voice (the exposition), then returns in different keys.
- **Ornamentation:** trills, mordents, turns, appoggiaturas. Performers often added their own decoration.
- **Terraced dynamics:** because the harpsichord and organ cannot get gradually louder or quieter, the music changes volume in "steps".
- **Forms and genres:** **concerto grosso** (small group of soloists, the *concertino*, against the full orchestra, the *ripieno*), **ritornello form** (a main theme returns between solo episodes), **binary-form dance suites** (allemande, courante, sarabande, gigue), **cantata**, **oratorio**, **opera**, **ground bass** (a short bass pattern repeated over and over, as in Purcell).
- **Harpsichord**: plucked strings, bright, metallic, same volume whatever the touch.
- Tonality: major and minor keys are now firmly established, with sequences (a pattern repeated higher or lower) driving the music forward.

### Classical (c.1750–1820)

- **Clarity and balance.** Melodies are made of regular phrases, often in pairs: a question (antecedent) and an answer (consequent).
- **Homophonic texture:** a clear melody over accompaniment. A typical accompaniment is the **Alberti bass** (a broken chord played in the left hand: low–high–middle–high).
- **Harmony** is mostly primary chords (I, IV, V, V7). Phrases end with clear **perfect or imperfect cadences**. Modulation is usually to closely related keys (dominant or relative).
- **Dynamics:** gradual **crescendo and diminuendo** appear. The piano (fortepiano) replaces the harpsichord because it can do this.
- **Forms:** **sonata form** (exposition, development, recapitulation), **minuet and trio**, **rondo**, **theme and variations**. These are used in the **symphony**, **string quartet**, **sonata** and **concerto** (soloist and orchestra, with a cadenza).
- Composers: Haydn, Mozart; Beethoven starts in this style and pushes towards the Romantic.

### Romantic (c.1820–1900)

- **Emotion and individuality.** Composers wanted to express feelings, stories, nature and national identity.
- **Harmony:** more **chromatic** notes, **dissonance** that is left unresolved for longer, and surprising modulations to distant keys.
- **Melody:** long, singing, expressive tunes; wider pitch range; **rubato** (flexible tempo: "robbed time").
- **Bigger orchestra:** extra brass (trombones, tuba), more percussion, harp, piccolo, cor anglais. Greater use of **timbre** and colour.
- **Programme music** tells a story or paints a picture (Berlioz, *Symphonie fantastique*). **Absolute music** has no story.
- **Character pieces** for piano: nocturne, prelude, étude, waltz (Chopin, Schumann). **Lieder**: art songs for voice and piano (Schubert, Schumann).
- **Nationalism:** folk melodies and rhythms of a composer's country (Dvořák, Grieg, Smetana).
- **Virtuosity:** dazzling technique (Liszt, Paganini).

### Recognising the period by ear: a quick method

1. **Texture:** interweaving independent lines plus a harpsichord → Baroque. Neat tune and accompaniment → Classical. Rich, thick and emotional → Romantic.
2. **Dynamics:** sudden steps → Baroque. Smooth crescendo in neat phrases → Classical. Huge swells and rubato → Romantic.
3. **Instruments:** harpsichord/continuo → Baroque. Piano and standard orchestra → Classical or Romantic. Huge orchestra → Romantic.
4. **Harmony:** clear and predictable → Baroque/Classical. Chromatic, unstable → Romantic.

### Link to your set works

Check your set-work list: instrumental and vocal set works from this era should be placed in their period with evidence. For example, a concerto with a harpsichord continuo and a ritornello structure is **Baroque**; a sonata with a crisp sonata-form first movement and a dramatic slow introduction can show how Beethoven stretches the **Classical** style towards the Romantic. ## Key terms and definitions

- **Basso continuo:** a bass line with chords filled in by a keyboard or lute, used throughout the Baroque period.
- **Concerto grosso:** a Baroque work in which a small group of soloists (concertino) plays against a larger group (ripieno).
- **Ritornello:** a recurring theme for the full orchestra that alternates with solo episodes.
- **Fugue:** a piece in which a short theme (the subject) is introduced in one voice and then imitated by others in turn.
- **Terraced dynamics:** sudden changes between loud and soft with no gradual change.
- **Alberti bass:** a left-hand accompaniment of broken chords in a repeating pattern.
- **Sonata form:** exposition, development, recapitulation.
- **Rubato:** slight speeding up and slowing down of the tempo for expression.
- **Programme music:** instrumental music that tells a story or depicts a scene.
- **Chromatic:** using notes outside the key (semitone steps).
- **Lied (plural lieder):** a German art song for solo voice and piano.

**1. You hear an excerpt for harpsichord, two violins and cello. Name the period and give two reasons. [3]**

*Model answer:* Baroque [1]. The harpsichord and cello are playing a basso continuo bass line [1]. The violins imitate each other in counterpoint / the dynamics are terraced [1].

*How the marks work:* one mark for the period, then one per **specific feature** that you could hear. "It sounds old" earns nothing.

**2. Describe how the music in the Romantic period is different from the Classical period. [4]**

*Model answer:* Romantic music uses much more chromatic harmony and unexpected modulations, while Classical harmony is mostly simple (I, IV, V) with clear cadences [2]. Romantic orchestras are bigger, with more brass and percussion [1]. Romantic composers use a wider range of dynamics and rubato for expression; Classical phrases are balanced and regular [1].

*Technique:* "differ" questions need both sides. Use a comparison word: "whereas", "while".

**3. Give two features of Classical melodies. [2]**

*Model answer:* Balanced phrases of equal length (e.g. 4 bars + 4 bars) [1]; question-and-answer (antecedent/consequent) phrases / mostly stepwise and diatonic with an accompaniment like an Alberti bass [1].

## Common mistakes

- Using "Classical" to mean "classical music in general". In the exam, **Classical** means c.1750–1820.
- Saying "the piano" for Baroque music. Baroque keyboards are the **harpsichord** and the **organ**.
- Calling any contrast in volume "crescendo". Baroque loud/quiet changes are **terraced**, not gradual.
- Writing "it is emotional" for Romantic without naming a feature (chromatic harmony, rubato, big dynamic range).
- Mixing up Baroque and Classical texture: Baroque is mainly polyphonic, Classical mainly homophonic.
- Describing one period without evidence from the music. Always link **feature → period**.
