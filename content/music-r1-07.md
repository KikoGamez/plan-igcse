# Western music: Baroque and Classical

> **In the exam:** Paper 1 (Listening, c.1 h 15 min, 70 marks, 40% of the grade) plays eight short extracts, with at least one from each of the seven areas of study. Areas 1 (Baroque) and 2 (Classical) give you extracts such as a concerto movement or a chamber piece. Expect multiple choice (1 mark: "Which instrument plays the solo?", "Identify the cadence"), short answers (1–2 marks: "Give two features that show this is Baroque") and a few questions with printed music (key, intervals, missing notes, following a score). Extracts are *related* to the focus works in structure, genre or style, so you must know the style, not just the pieces.

## What you need to know

- **Area 1: Baroque music.** Focus on the solo concerto and the concerto grosso. Focus works: Vivaldi, *Spring* (*The Four Seasons*), movement 1; Handel, Concerto Grosso in D major Op. 6 No. 5, movements 1, 2, 4 and 6.
- **Area 2: Classical music.** Focus on chamber music for three to six players and on sonata form. Focus work: Mozart, Quintet for piano and winds in E flat major, K. 452, movement 1.
- Typical features of each style: texture, instruments, harmony, structure, dynamics, ornaments.
- Terms in the syllabus: *ripieno, concertino, basso continuo, ritornello, episode, sonata form (exposition, development, recapitulation, first and second subject, transition, codetta, coda), cadenza, minuet and trio, theme and variations, Alberti bass, terraced dynamics, trill, mordent, turn, appoggiatura, acciaccatura, imitation, sequence.*
- Typical performance practice in Baroque music (continuo, ornamentation, small forces).
- Wider listening (not prescribed, not exhaustive): Corelli *Christmas Concerto*, Bach Orchestral Suite No. 3, Jacquet de la Guerre Suite in A minor, Telemann Trio Sonata in F; Haydn Piano Trio No. 39 "Gypsy", Mozart String Quartet K. 458 "The Hunt", Mozart Variations K. 573, Haydn Symphony No. 95, Beethoven Piano Concerto No. 1.

## Notes

### The two periods at a glance

| | **Baroque** (c.1600–1750) | **Classical** (c.1750–1820) |
|---|---|---|
| Texture | Often **polyphonic** and **imitative** (fugue-like entries); also homophonic | Mostly **homophonic**: clear melody with accompaniment; some imitation in development sections |
| Bass | **Basso continuo**: a bass line (cello/bass/bassoon) plus a chord instrument (harpsichord or organ) playing throughout | No continuo. Accompaniment patterns such as the **Alberti bass** (broken chords in the left hand) |
| Melody | Long, flowing, ornamented lines; **sequences** very common; one idea spun out | Balanced, **symmetrical phrases** (e.g. 4 + 4 bars, question and answer), tuneful and clear |
| Harmony | Major/minor tonality fully established; chords move fast and logically; **sequences** of chords | Simple, clear chords; strong, clear **cadences** (perfect, imperfect) mark the ends of phrases |
| Dynamics | **Terraced**: sudden changes, loud tutti then soft solo; no gradual crescendo (harpsichord cannot do it) | **Crescendo and diminuendo** are normal; also sforzando for surprise (piano can play loud and soft) |
| Instruments | Strings, harpsichord, organ, recorder, trumpet, oboe; small orchestras | Growing orchestra: strings, pairs of woodwind, horns, trumpets, timpani; **fortepiano/piano** replaces harpsichord |
| Ornaments | Frequent: trills, mordents, turns; players often add their own | Used but more restrained; written out, e.g. appoggiaturas, turns |
| Main forms | **Ritornello**, concerto grosso, solo concerto, suite of dances, fugue, binary | **Sonata form**, minuet and trio, rondo, theme and variations, symphony, string quartet |
| Mood | One main mood per movement | Contrast of moods **within** a movement (first and second subjects) |

### Area 1: the Baroque concerto

A **concerto** contrasts a small group or soloist with a larger group.

- **Solo concerto:** one soloist against the orchestra (Vivaldi's *Spring* has a solo violin).
- **Concerto grosso:** a small group, the **concertino**, against the full string group, the **ripieno** (also called *tutti*). In Handel's Op. 6 the concertino is two violins and a cello. The ripieno is the string orchestra. A **basso continuo** (harpsichord plus cello/bass) plays with everyone.

**Ritornello form** (fast movements): the full orchestra plays a main theme, the **ritornello** ("little return"). Soloist or concertino play contrasting **episodes**, often showing off and modulating to related keys. The ritornello returns in new keys, in fragments, and finally in the home key. Think of it as: R – episode – R – episode – R.

**Vivaldi, *Spring*, 1st movement (Allegro, E major).** From the set *The Four Seasons* (c.1725). Each concerto is **programme music** based on a sonnet, and the music imitates the text.
- Ritornello: bright, cheerful, **tutti**, in E major, with a lively rhythm to suggest the joy of spring.
- Birdsong episode: solo violin and the two violins of the orchestra imitate birds with **trills** and repeated high notes.
- Streams: gentle, murmuring strings in a soft dynamic.
- Storm: thunder and lightning, shown by fast **tremolo** and rushing scale passages, with a move to the minor.
- The ritornello returns to close the movement in the home key.
- Use these ideas for any "programme music" question: *word-painting*, imitation of nature, solo violin as the star.

**Handel, Concerto Grosso Op. 6 No. 5 (D major, published 1740).** A set of six short movements, each with its own character. The four you study:
- **Mvt 1, Larghetto e staccato:** a slow, stately opening; the "staccato" refers to detached, punctuated chords and the movement has a dignified, ceremonial character.
- **Mvt 2, Allegro:** quick, **imitative** (fugue-like) writing, where the theme is passed between parts. Concertino and ripieno alternate.
- **Mvt 4, Largo:** slow and expressive, lyrical melodic lines over a steady continuo.
- **Mvt 6, Menuet:** a **dance** in triple time, clear phrases, homophonic, light and elegant.
Use them to illustrate: concertino/ripieno contrast, continuo, imitation, a slow-fast-dance order of movements, and the idea that one mood stays through each movement.

**Performance practice:** continuo keyboard realises the chords from a **figured bass**; performers add ornaments, especially in repeats; small ensembles; players used gut strings and baroque bows, so the sound is lighter and less vibrant than a modern orchestra.

### Area 2: Classical chamber music and sonata form

**Chamber music** is for a small group with **one player per part**, no conductor. Examples: string quartet (2 violins, viola, cello), piano trio (piano, violin, cello). Mozart's quintet uses **piano, oboe, clarinet, horn and bassoon**.

**Sonata form** (used for first movements):

| Section | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Exposition** | **First subject** in the tonic (home key). A **transition** modulates. **Second subject** in a contrasting key (dominant for a major piece, relative major for a minor one). Ends with a **codetta** |
| **Development** | Themes from the exposition are broken up, developed, **sequenced**, and pushed through many keys. Tension builds, often ending on the dominant |
| **Recapitulation** | First subject returns in the tonic. The second subject now **also in the tonic**. The piece feels resolved |
| **Coda** | Optional ending that confirms the tonic |

**Mozart, Quintet K. 452, movement 1 (E flat major, 1784).** Starts with a **slow introduction (Largo)** before the main **Allegro moderato** in sonata form. The piano and the four wind instruments take turns in the lead, passing ideas from player to player: the writing is conversation-like and mostly **homophonic**, with melody and accompaniment, and the winds often blend together as a group. The first subject is in the tonic E flat. The second subject appears in B flat (the dominant) in the exposition, then in E flat in the recapitulation. Wind instruments to know: **horn in E flat** and **clarinet in B flat** are *transposing* instruments (a syllabus point: you may need to transpose a short passage to concert pitch).

**Other Classical forms:** **minuet and trio** (minuet–trio–minuet: ternary, 3/4 time); **rondo** (A B A C A); **theme and variations**; **ternary**; **cadenza** in a concerto (unaccompanied show-off section for the soloist, usually near the end of the movement). **Alberti bass** (e.g. C–G–E–G repeated) is a Classical giveaway.

### How to recognise the period in an unfamiliar extract

1. Is there a **harpsichord or organ** playing a bass line with chords? → Baroque (continuo).
2. Is the dynamic **suddenly** loud or soft, not gradual? → Baroque (terraced).
3. Are parts **chasing** each other with the same tune (imitation)? → Baroque, or a development section.
4. Does the melody move in neat, even 4-bar phrases over simple chords and an Alberti bass? → Classical.
5. Is there a **piano** or a clarinet in the ensemble? (clarinet was rare before c.1750) → Classical or later.
6. Are there gradual crescendos and a contrast of two themes? → Classical sonata form.
7. If you cannot decide, give features and say "typical of the Baroque/Classical period". You get marks for the features.

## Key terms and definitions

- **Basso continuo:** a bass line with chords played over it by a keyboard (harpsichord or organ), plus a bass instrument, throughout a Baroque piece.
- **Concerto grosso:** a Baroque piece contrasting a small group of soloists (concertino) with a larger group (ripieno).
- **Concertino:** the small group of soloists in a concerto grosso.
- **Ripieno:** the larger group (full orchestra) in a concerto grosso.
- **Ritornello:** a returning theme played by the full orchestra in a Baroque concerto movement.
- **Episode:** a contrasting section, usually for the soloist(s), between ritornello statements.
- **Terraced dynamics:** sudden changes between loud and soft, with no gradual change.
- **Alberti bass:** a left-hand accompaniment of broken chords in a repeated pattern.
- **Sonata form:** a structure with exposition, development and recapitulation.
- **Exposition / development / recapitulation:** presentation of themes / exploration of themes in new keys / return of the themes in the home key.
- **Cadenza:** an unaccompanied showy passage for the soloist, often improvised.
- **Sequence:** a melodic or harmonic pattern repeated at a higher or lower pitch.
- **Imitation:** a melody played in one part is repeated soon after in another part.
- **Programme music:** instrumental music that describes a story, scene or idea.

## Worked examples

**1. This extract is from a Baroque concerto grosso. Give two features of the music that suggest it is Baroque. [2]**
*Model answer:* "The harpsichord plays a continuo part under the strings (1). The dynamics change suddenly between loud tutti and soft concertino, which is terraced dynamics (1)."
*Why it earns marks:* each feature is specific, uses a correct term and refers to the music. "It sounds old" earns nothing.

**2. Describe how the music of the Allegro from *Spring* by Vivaldi depicts spring. [4]**
*Model answer:* "The ritornello is played by the whole orchestra in a major key with a bouncy rhythm, so it sounds joyful (1). The solo violin plays high trills and repeated notes to imitate birdsong (1). Murmuring soft strings suggest streams (1). Fast tremolo and rushing scales in the minor show a thunderstorm (1)."
*Why it earns marks:* four separate musical events, each linked to a scene.

**3. In sonata form, what happens to the second subject in the recapitulation? [2]**
*Model answer:* "It returns (1) in the tonic key, not the dominant as in the exposition (1)."
*Why it earns marks:* "it comes back" gets only one mark; the key change is the second.

**4. Mozart's horn is in E flat. It plays the written note C. What is the sounding (concert) pitch? [1]**
*Model answer:* E flat horn parts sound a major sixth lower than written, so written C sounds E flat below it (1).
*Tip:* for B flat clarinet the sound is a major second **lower** than written; for A clarinet a minor third lower.

## Common mistakes

- Saying "Baroque has no dynamics". It does, but they are terraced and mostly built into the instrumentation.
- Confusing **concertino** (small solo group) with **ripieno** (large group).
- Writing "harpsichord" for any piano-like sound. Check: harpsichord has a plucked, metallic, constant volume; piano can get louder or quieter.
- Describing sonata form without the **keys** (second subject: dominant in the exposition, tonic in the recapitulation).
- Giving only one-word answers when asked to "describe". Say what and where.
- Saying "Classical" means "all classical music". In this topic, Classical means c.1750–1820.
