# Popular music and film music

> **In the exam:** Paper 3 (Appraising, 1 h 45 min, 40%) includes questions on popular, stage and screen music, both in your set works (e.g. a rock song, a musical, a film theme) and in unfamiliar extracts. Typical questions: identify instruments, describe how a theme is used, name a technique (leitmotif, riff, backing vocals), compare two pieces. Marks range from 1 to 12.

## What you need to know

- Features of popular styles: **rock, pop, jazz, blues, soul/funk, reggae, rap/hip-hop, electronic dance, musical theatre**.
- Typical **instrumentation** (the "band"), structures (verse–chorus, 12-bar blues, AABA, strophic) and devices (riff, hook, break, fill, walking bass, riff-based vamps).
- How **film music** supports the picture: **leitmotif**, underscore, diegetic and non-diegetic music, **mickey-mousing**, sound effects, themes, and use of orchestral and electronic sounds.
- How **musical theatre** songs tell stories (character song, ensemble, reprise, key changes).
- Music technology: **multi-tracking, overdubbing, effects (reverb, delay, distortion, phasing), synthesisers, sampling, quantisation, panning**.

## Notes

### Popular styles at a glance

| Style | Typical features |
|---|---|
| **Blues** | **12-bar blues** (I–IV–I–V–IV–I); **blue notes** (flattened 3rd, 5th, 7th); call and response; guitar, harmonica, piano; swung rhythm |
| **Jazz** | **Swing** rhythms; **syncopation**; **improvisation**; walking bass; extended chords (7ths, 9ths); brass, saxophone, piano, double bass, drum kit |
| **Rock** | Drum kit, bass guitar, electric guitars (**distortion**, riffs, solos), vocals; strong **backbeat** (beats 2 and 4); verse–chorus |
| **Pop** | Catchy **hooks**, verse–chorus–bridge, simple harmony, synths and drum machine, backing vocals, clear production |
| **Soul / funk** | Strong rhythm section; syncopated bass (slap bass); horn section; emotional vocals; riff-based grooves |
| **Reggae** | **Off-beat** guitar chords ("skank"); bass line is melodic and prominent; slow-moderate tempo; **one drop** drum pattern |
| **Hip-hop / rap** | Rapped vocals, **sampling**, drum machine beat, turntable scratching, repeated loops |
| **EDM / dance** | Four-on-the-floor kick drum (120–130 bpm), synthesisers, **build and drop**, filters, repeating loops |
| **Musical theatre** | Songs to move the story on; sung or spoken dialogue; orchestra in a pit; ballads, ensembles, reprises |

### Building blocks of popular music

- **Riff:** a short repeated rhythmic or melodic pattern (a guitar riff). **Ostinato** is the general term.
- **Hook:** the catchy part you remember, often in the chorus.
- **Verse–chorus structure:** verses tell the story; the **chorus** repeats the main message and hook; a **bridge** (middle eight) gives contrast; an **intro** and **outro** frame it.
- **Break/fill:** a short drum fill or break between sections.
- **Instrument roles:** drums and bass = **rhythm section**; guitar/keys = chords and riffs; the lead vocal = melody; **backing vocals** = harmony and effect.
- **Harmony** is often a four-chord loop (e.g. I–V–vi–IV) and **power chords** in rock.
- **Production:** **multi-tracking** (recording layers separately), **overdubbing**, **panning** (placing sounds left/right), **reverb** (echo of a room), **delay**, **distortion**, **phasing/flanging** (a "swooshing" effect), **auto-tune**, **compression**.

### Film and screen music

Film music (the **score**) supports the story, creates mood and gives information the picture cannot.

- **Leitmotif:** a short theme linked to a character, object or idea, which returns and changes with the plot (changing key, tempo, instrument, mode). Typical of Wagner and later film composers such as John Williams.
- **Underscore:** music played quietly under dialogue and action.
- **Diegetic music:** part of the world of the film (a radio, a band on screen). **Non-diegetic:** the score the characters cannot hear.
- **Mickey-mousing:** music that copies the action exactly (a falling melody as someone trips).
- **Techniques for mood:** **tension** = tremolo strings, rising pitch, dissonance, ostinato, slow build; **heroism** = brass fanfare, major key, strong rhythm; **sadness** = slow, minor, solo instrument (cello, oboe); **danger** = low brass, timpani, chromatic notes.
- **Orchestral vs electronic scoring:** many scores use a symphony orchestra (late-Romantic style); others use synthesisers or hybrid scores. A **sequencer** and **samples** can be used to create sounds.
- **Timing devices:** **hit points** (music matched to key moments), **click track** (metronome for the conductor), **stingers** (a short, loud chord for shock).

### Musical theatre

- Songs advance the plot or reveal character. Common types: **opening number**, **"I want" song**, ballad, **ensemble** number, **finale**.
- Techniques: **key change** to raise emotion (often up a tone/semitone), **reprise**, **recitative-like** dialogue, spoken-sung sections.
- Orchestra: strings, brass, woodwind, keyboards and drum kit; **belting** (strong chest-voice singing) and legit vocal styles.
- Your set works may include a song from a musical and a film theme: check your list and learn the specific features of each.

### Describing a popular-music extract (a method)

1. **Style and instrumentation:** "A rock band with distorted electric guitar, bass, drum kit and lead vocals."
2. **Structure:** intro, verse, chorus, bridge, solo.
3. **Rhythm and tempo:** backbeat, syncopation, swung, fast or slow.
4. **Melody and harmony:** riff, hook, repeated chords, key change.
5. **Production:** reverb, multi-tracking, panning, distortion.

## Key terms and definitions

- **Riff:** a short repeated melodic or rhythmic phrase.
- **Hook:** a short catchy idea that stays in the listener's mind.
- **Backbeat:** emphasis on beats 2 and 4 of the bar.
- **12-bar blues:** a chord pattern of twelve bars based on chords I, IV and V.
- **Leitmotif:** a recurring theme associated with a person, place or idea.
- **Diegetic music:** music that the characters in the film can hear.
- **Underscore:** background music under dialogue or action.
- **Mickey-mousing:** music that closely imitates on-screen action.
- **Multi-tracking:** recording different parts separately and layering them.
- **Reverb:** an effect that simulates echo in a space.
- **Sampling:** reusing a piece of an existing recording in a new track.
- **Syncopation:** accenting off-beats or weak beats.

## Worked examples

**1. Describe how a composer can use a leitmotif in a film. [3]**

*Model answer:* A leitmotif is a short theme linked to a character or idea [1]. Each time that character appears the theme returns, perhaps on a different instrument [1]. It can be changed (e.g. into the minor key, slower, quieter) to show how the character or situation has changed [1].

**2. You hear an excerpt of a rock song. Give three features of the music that suggest it is rock. [3]**

*Model answer:* Distorted electric guitar playing a riff [1]; drum kit with a strong backbeat on beats 2 and 4 [1]; electric bass guitar and a lead vocal / a verse–chorus structure [1].

**3. Explain how music is used to create tension in a film chase scene. [4]**

*Model answer:* The tempo is fast and the music is driven by a repeated ostinato in the strings or percussion [1]. Dissonant chords and chromatic notes make the harmony unsettled [1]. Pitch rises or there is a crescendo to build excitement [1]. Brass or timpani hits mark key moments (hit points) [1].

*How marks are earned:* name the technique, then say what it does.

## Common mistakes

- Saying "the music is exciting" with no musical feature. Name the technique (ostinato, crescendo, brass).
- Confusing **diegetic** and **non-diegetic**. The characters can hear diegetic music only.
- Using "melody" and "riff" as synonyms. A riff is a short repeating pattern.
- Calling a bridge a "chorus". A bridge **contrasts**; a chorus **repeats**.
- Writing about film music without mentioning how it relates to the **picture**.
- Naming an effect (e.g. "reverb") without saying what it sounds like. Add "sounds as if in a large room".
