# The muscular system

> **In the exam:** Paper 1. Typical questions: name a muscle from a diagram or a sporting action (1 mark), identify the agonist and antagonist in a movement (2 marks), explain how muscles work in antagonistic pairs (2–3 marks), describe types of muscle contraction (isotonic/isometric) and match muscle fibre types to activities (2–4 marks). Longer questions often ask you to link a muscle or fibre type to a named sport.

## What you need to know

- The location and a sporting use of the major voluntary muscles: **deltoid, trapezius, latissimus dorsi, pectoralis major, biceps, triceps, wrist flexors and extensors, abdominals (rectus abdominis), hip flexors, gluteals (gluteus maximus), quadriceps, hamstrings, gastrocnemius, tibialis anterior**.
- **Antagonistic muscle pairs**: agonist (prime mover), antagonist, and the role of a **fixator**.
- Muscle contractions: **isotonic** (concentric and eccentric) and **isometric**.
- The three **muscle fibre types**: slow oxidative (type I), fast oxidative glycolytic (type IIa) and fast glycolytic (type IIx), and which sports suit each.

## Notes

### Voluntary muscles you must know

Muscles you control are **voluntary (skeletal) muscles**. They are attached to bones by tendons and pull on bones to create movement. They can only **pull** (contract), never push.

| Muscle | Where | Main action | Sporting example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Deltoid** | Top of the shoulder | Abducts, flexes and extends the arm at the shoulder | Raising the arm to serve in tennis |
| **Trapezius** | Upper back, neck to mid-back | Raises and pulls the shoulder blades together | Shrugging; pulling in rowing |
| **Latissimus dorsi** | Large muscle of the back, either side | Adducts and rotates arm; pulls arm down and back | Pulling down in butterfly swimming; pull-up |
| **Pectoralis major** | Chest | Brings arm across the chest (adduction, flexion) | Bench press; throwing |
| **Biceps** | Front of upper arm | Flexes the elbow | Bicep curl; climbing |
| **Triceps** | Back of upper arm | Extends the elbow | Shot put; push-up |
| **Wrist flexors / extensors** | Inside / outside of forearm | Bend / straighten the wrist | Flick of a basketball shot; golf swing |
| **Abdominals (rectus abdominis)** | Front of the stomach | Flexes the trunk; stabilises the spine | Sit-ups; core stability |
| **Hip flexors** | Front of the hip | Lift the knee and leg forward (flexion at hip) | Sprinting knee lift |
| **Gluteus maximus** | Buttocks | Extends the hip; moves the leg back | Sprint start, jumping |
| **Quadriceps** | Front of the thigh (four muscles) | Extend the knee | Kicking; jumping |
| **Hamstrings** | Back of the thigh (three muscles) | Flex the knee | Bringing the heel up in running |
| **Gastrocnemius** | Calf, back of the lower leg | Plantar flexes the ankle | Pushing off on tiptoe; sprint start |
| **Tibialis anterior** | Front of the shin | Dorsi flexes the ankle | Pulling the toes up on landing |

### How muscles move bones: antagonistic pairs

Muscles work in **pairs** because they can only pull. When one contracts, the other relaxes and gets longer.

- **Agonist (prime mover)**: the muscle that **contracts** to produce the movement.
- **Antagonist**: the muscle that **relaxes** (lengthens) to allow the movement.
- **Fixator**: a muscle that **stabilises** one part of the body (usually a joint or the trunk) so the agonist has a firm base to pull against.

| Joint and movement | Agonist | Antagonist |
|---|---|---|
| Elbow flexion (curling up) | Biceps | Triceps |
| Elbow extension (straightening) | Triceps | Biceps |
| Knee extension (kicking) | Quadriceps | Hamstrings |
| Knee flexion (heel up) | Hamstrings | Quadriceps |
| Ankle plantar flexion (tiptoe) | Gastrocnemius | Tibialis anterior |
| Ankle dorsi flexion (toes up) | Tibialis anterior | Gastrocnemius |
| Hip extension | Gluteus maximus | Hip flexors |
| Hip flexion | Hip flexors | Gluteus maximus |
| Trunk flexion (sit-up) | Abdominals | (Lower back muscles) |

**Important:** the roles **swap** when the movement reverses. In the downward phase of a bicep curl the **triceps** extends the elbow but the biceps lowers the weight under control (see eccentric below).

**Fixator example:** in a biceps curl, the muscles around the shoulder and the trunk (e.g. the deltoid and trapezius) act as fixators; they hold the shoulder still so the biceps can curl the forearm.

### Types of muscle contraction

| Type | What happens to the muscle | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Isotonic: concentric** | Muscle **shortens** while contracting, producing movement | Upward phase of a bicep curl (biceps); upward phase of a squat (quadriceps) |
| **Isotonic: eccentric** | Muscle **lengthens** while contracting, controlling movement | Lowering the weight in a bicep curl (biceps); landing from a jump (quadriceps absorb the force) |
| **Isometric** | Muscle contracts but **stays the same length**; no movement | Holding a plank; holding a gymnastics "L-sit"; a rugby scrum push when it does not move |

**Memory trick:** **con**centric = **c**losing together (shortening); eccentric = **e**xtending (lengthening).

### Muscle fibre types

Skeletal muscle is made of fibres. Everyone has a mix, set mostly by genetics, but training can change the characteristics a little.

| | **Slow oxidative (type I)** | **Fast oxidative glycolytic (type IIa)** | **Fast glycolytic (type IIx)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contraction speed | Slow | Fast | Fastest |
| Force produced | Low | Medium–high | Highest |
| Fatigue | **Very resistant** to fatigue | Moderately resistant to fatigue | Fatigue **quickly** |
| Colour | Red (many capillaries and myoglobin) | Pink/red | White (fewer capillaries) |
| Energy system | Aerobic | Aerobic and anaerobic | Anaerobic |
| Size | Small | Medium | Large |
| Suits | Marathon running, long-distance cycling, posture muscles | 400 m, 800 m, 1500 m, team sports with repeated sprints (football, hockey) | 100 m sprint, weightlifting, shot put |

**Why the red colour?** **Myoglobin** stores oxygen in muscle and **capillaries** bring more blood; both help aerobic work.

**Linking to the activity:** a marathon runner needs fibres that keep working for hours without tiring: **slow twitch** (type I). A sprinter needs a very fast, powerful but short burst: **fast glycolytic** (type IIx).

### Muscle and exercise

- Regular training can cause **hypertrophy** (the muscle fibres get thicker and stronger).
- Lack of use leads to **atrophy** (muscle wasting).
- **Muscle tone**: even at rest, muscles hold a slight tension, helping posture.

## Key terms and definitions

- **Voluntary muscle**: skeletal muscle that you consciously control.
- **Agonist (prime mover)**: the muscle that contracts to cause movement at a joint.
- **Antagonist**: the muscle that relaxes to allow the agonist to move the joint.
- **Antagonistic pair**: two muscles that work in opposite ways at a joint.
- **Fixator**: a muscle that stabilises a joint or body part while the agonist works.
- **Isotonic contraction**: contraction in which the muscle changes length and movement occurs.
- **Concentric contraction**: the muscle shortens while it contracts.
- **Eccentric contraction**: the muscle lengthens while it contracts.
- **Isometric contraction**: the muscle contracts without changing length; no movement.
- **Slow oxidative fibre**: fibre that contracts slowly, resists fatigue and uses oxygen (aerobic).
- **Fast glycolytic fibre**: fibre that contracts quickly and forcefully, but fatigues quickly.
- **Hypertrophy**: an increase in the size of a muscle.
- **Myoglobin**: protein that stores oxygen in muscle.

## Worked examples

**1. During the upward phase of a bicep curl, identify the agonist and antagonist, and name the type of contraction in the agonist. [3 marks]**

*Model answer:* Agonist: biceps (1). Antagonist: triceps (1). The biceps shortens, so it is a concentric (isotonic) contraction (1).

**2. Explain why a marathon runner has a high proportion of slow oxidative fibres. [3 marks]**

*Model answer:* Slow oxidative fibres are resistant to fatigue (1), they use oxygen / work aerobically (1), so the runner can keep contracting for a long time at a low intensity (1).

*Tip:* match the fibre to the demands of the sport: duration, intensity, fatigue.

**3. A sprinter starts from the blocks. Name the muscle that plantar flexes the ankle, and its antagonist. [2 marks]**

*Model answer:* Gastrocnemius (1); tibialis anterior (1).

**4. Describe isometric contraction and give a sporting example. [2 marks]**

*Model answer:* The muscle contracts without changing length / no movement at the joint (1); e.g. holding a plank / holding a handstand / a scrum under pressure (1).

## Common mistakes

- Saying the **antagonist** "does the opposite work"; be exact: it **relaxes and lengthens**.
- Forgetting that agonist and antagonist **swap** when the movement reverses.
- Writing "quads" or "hammies" instead of **quadriceps** and **hamstrings** (use proper names).
- Calling a sprinter's fibre type "fast twitch" only; if the question asks for the type, name it: **fast glycolytic (type IIx)**.
- Mixing up concentric and eccentric. Eccentric is lengthening under tension, e.g. lowering a weight.
- Saying isometric muscles "do nothing". They are working, but there is no change in length and no movement.
- Forgetting the sporting example in a 2-mark "describe and give an example" question.
