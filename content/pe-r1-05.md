# Energy supply and the effects of exercise

> **In the exam:** Paper 1 (Theory, 1 hour 45 minutes, 100 marks; short and structured questions). Typical items: "Write the word equation for aerobic respiration" [2], "Name the waste product of anaerobic respiration" [1], "Explain why a 100 m sprint is anaerobic" [2–3], "State two short-term effects of exercise on the body" [2], "Explain what is meant by EPOC / oxygen debt" [2–3], "Describe two long-term effects of training on the body" [2–4]. Marks go for the exact equation wording and for linking intensity and duration to the type of respiration.

## What you need to know

- Energy comes from food: muscle cells release energy from glucose by respiration; some glucose is stored as glycogen in the muscles and liver.
- Aerobic respiration: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water (+ energy). Anaerobic respiration: glucose → lactic acid (+ energy).
- Link duration and intensity to aerobic or anaerobic respiration, with examples.
- Recovery: Excess Post-exercise Oxygen Consumption (EPOC, oxygen debt) and factors affecting recovery time.
- Short-term effects of exercise: heart rate, breathing rate, red skin/sweating, fatigue, nausea/light-headedness.
- Long-term effects of exercise: heart size (hypertrophy), resting pulse rate (bradycardia), stroke volume, ability to tolerate lactic acid.

## Notes

### Where energy comes from

Muscle cells need energy to contract. The energy comes from **glucose** (from carbohydrate in food) which is released in a process called **respiration**. Glucose that is not used straight away is converted to **glycogen** and stored in the **muscles and liver**, ready for exercise. Unused energy is stored as fat.

### Aerobic respiration (with oxygen)

**Glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water** (+ lots of energy)

- Used for **long-duration, low-to-moderate intensity** activity, when the heart and lungs can deliver enough oxygen to the muscles.
- Produces a large amount of energy and no harmful waste: CO₂ is breathed out and water is lost as sweat and breath.
- Examples: marathon running, long-distance swimming, cycling, 90 minutes of football (most of the game), walking.

### Anaerobic respiration (without oxygen)

**Glucose → lactic acid** (+ a small amount of energy)

- Used for **short-duration, high-intensity** activity, when the muscles need energy faster than oxygen can be supplied.
- Produces **little energy** and **lactic acid**. Lactic acid builds up and causes muscle fatigue and pain, so it can only be kept up for a short time.
- Examples: 100 m sprint, a shot put throw, a high jump, a weightlifting repetition, a sprint finish in a 1500 m race.

| | Aerobic | Anaerobic |
|---|---|---|
| Oxygen used? | Yes | No |
| Intensity | Low to moderate | High |
| Duration | Long (over about 90 seconds to hours) | Short (up to about a minute or two) |
| Energy released | Lots | Little |
| Waste product | CO₂ and water | **Lactic acid** |
| Example | Marathon | 100 m sprint |

Most sports use **both**: a football player jogs (aerobic) and sprints (anaerobic). At the start of every exercise, the body briefly uses anaerobic energy before breathing and heart rate catch up.

### Recovery and EPOC

After hard exercise you keep breathing heavily and your heart rate stays high. This is **EPOC: Excess Post-exercise Oxygen Consumption** (also called **oxygen debt**).

- Anaerobic exercise creates a **debt** of oxygen and builds up **lactic acid**.
- Extra oxygen is needed after exercise to **remove the lactic acid** (and to restore the body to its resting state), so **breathing rate stays high** after you stop.
- The bigger the debt, the longer the recovery.

**Factors affecting recovery time:** fitness level (fitter people recover faster); intensity and duration of the exercise (more intense = longer recovery); age; diet and hydration; a proper cool down (light activity keeps blood flowing and helps remove lactic acid); rest and sleep.

### Short-term effects of exercise (during and straight after)

| Effect | Why |
|---|---|
| **Heart rate increases** | To send more oxygen-rich blood to the working muscles |
| **Breathing rate increases** | To take in more oxygen and remove more carbon dioxide |
| **Red skin / sweating** | Blood vessels near the skin widen and sweat evaporates to cool the body (heat control) |
| **Fatigue (tiredness)** | Lactic acid build-up and use of energy stores |
| **Nausea / feeling light-headed** | Too much intensity, not enough oxygen to the brain or stomach, heat, dehydration |

### Long-term effects of regular exercise (training adaptations)

| Effect | What happens | Benefit |
|---|---|---|
| **Hypertrophy of the heart** | The heart muscle gets bigger and stronger | Pumps more blood with each beat |
| **Bradycardia** (lower resting pulse) | Resting heart rate falls (e.g. under 60 bpm, often 40–50 in endurance athletes) | The heart works less hard at rest and has a bigger reserve in exercise |
| **Increased stroke volume** | More blood leaves the heart per beat | More oxygen delivered to the muscles; higher cardiac output |
| **Greater tolerance of lactic acid** | The body can work harder for longer before lactic acid causes fatigue | Delays fatigue, better performance |

(Other benefits you can use as extras: stronger bones and muscles, more capillaries, lower body fat, lower risk of heart disease.)

## Key terms and definitions

- **Respiration**: the process by which muscle cells release energy from glucose.
- **Aerobic respiration**: releasing energy from glucose using oxygen (glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water).
- **Anaerobic respiration**: releasing energy from glucose without oxygen (glucose → lactic acid).
- **Glycogen**: the form in which glucose is stored in the muscles and liver.
- **Lactic acid**: the waste product of anaerobic respiration that causes muscle fatigue.
- **EPOC (oxygen debt)**: the extra oxygen the body takes in after exercise to remove lactic acid and recover.
- **Hypertrophy**: an increase in the size of a muscle (including the heart).
- **Bradycardia**: a resting heart rate below 60 beats per minute, as a result of training.

## Worked examples

**Q1. Write the equation for aerobic respiration and explain when a footballer uses it. [3]**

- Glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water (2: one for the reactants, one for the products).
- Used when jogging or walking, i.e. long-duration, low-intensity effort (1).

**Q2. Explain why a 100 m sprinter uses anaerobic respiration. [3]**

- The sprint is very high intensity (1) and over in about 10 seconds (1).
- The muscles need energy faster than the heart and lungs can supply oxygen, so energy is released without oxygen (1), producing lactic acid.

**Q3. After a 400 m race a runner is still breathing heavily. Explain why. [3]**

- She worked anaerobically and built up lactic acid (1).
- Extra oxygen is needed to break down/remove the lactic acid (EPOC / oxygen debt) (1), so breathing rate stays high after exercise (1).

**Q4. Describe two long-term effects of a 12-week endurance training programme on the heart. [4]**

- Hypertrophy: the heart muscle becomes larger and stronger (1), so stroke volume rises (1).
- Resting heart rate falls (bradycardia) (1) because each beat pumps more blood, so the heart needs fewer beats to deliver the same cardiac output (1).

## Common mistakes

- Writing "carbon dioxide + oxygen" in the wrong place in the aerobic equation, or leaving out **water**.
- Saying anaerobic means "no breathing". It means **without oxygen** being used for energy release.
- Writing "lactic acid causes DOMS/cramp" as a fact without saying it causes **fatigue**.
- Saying the **long-term** effect is "heart rate goes up". Long term, the **resting** heart rate falls.
- Mixing **short-term** (during one session) and **long-term** (after weeks or months of training).
- Giving "oxygen debt" without saying what the oxygen is used for (removing lactic acid).
- Not linking to both **intensity AND duration** when explaining aerobic vs anaerobic.
