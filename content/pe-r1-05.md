# Energy systems and the effects of exercise

> **In the exam:** Paper 1. Expect: name the energy system used in a named activity (1 mark), describe the aerobic and anaerobic systems (fuel, oxygen, duration, by-products; 2–4 marks), explain **EPOC** / oxygen debt (2–3 marks), and state **short-term and long-term effects** of exercise on the body systems (2–6 marks, often in extended questions on a training programme). Data questions may show heart rate, lactic acid or oxygen uptake during and after exercise.

## What you need to know

- **ATP** as the energy currency of the body.
- The three energy systems: **ATP-PC (alactic)**, **lactic acid (anaerobic glycolytic)** and **aerobic**; fuels, duration, oxygen and by-products.
- The **energy continuum**: which system dominates in which activity.
- **Aerobic and anaerobic** exercise and the **recovery process**: **EPOC** (excess post-exercise oxygen consumption), lactate removal, DOMS.
- **Short-term effects** of exercise on the cardiovascular, respiratory and muscular systems.
- **Long-term effects (adaptations)** of regular training on the skeletal, muscular, cardiovascular and respiratory systems.

## Notes

### ATP: the energy currency

Muscles contract using energy released when **ATP** (adenosine triphosphate) is broken down to **ADP + phosphate**. The body stores only a tiny amount of ATP (enough for 1–2 seconds), so it has to be **re-made constantly** by the three energy systems.

### The three energy systems

| | **ATP-PC (alactic)** | **Lactic acid system** | **Aerobic system** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Oxygen?** | **Anaerobic** (without oxygen) | **Anaerobic** | **Aerobic** (with oxygen) |
| **Fuel** | **Phosphocreatine (PC)** stored in muscle | **Glucose** (carbohydrate) | **Glucose**, then **fats** (and a little protein) |
| **By-products** | None (no lactic acid) | **Lactic acid** (lactate) | **Carbon dioxide and water** |
| **Duration** | About **0–10 seconds** | About **10 seconds to 1–3 minutes** | **Over 3 minutes** and for hours |
| **Intensity** | Maximum, explosive | High | Low to moderate, sustained |
| **ATP yield** | Very small, but **very fast** | Small | **Large** |
| **Sports** | 100 m sprint, shot put, weightlifting, a jump | 400 m, 200 m swim, hockey sprints | Marathon, cycling, football (overall) |

**Why lactic acid matters:** lactic acid builds up in the muscle, causing **fatigue** and the burning feeling, and it forces the athlete to slow down. It can be converted back into glucose or used as fuel when oxygen is available again.

**Aerobic system:** glucose is broken down with oxygen into **CO₂ and water**, releasing much more energy, and the system can last as long as oxygen and fuel last. At low intensity, **fats** are an important fuel.

### The energy continuum

Energy systems do **not** work one at a time. All three run together, but the **dominant system** depends on **intensity and duration** of the activity:

- Very short, maximum effort (shot put, 100 m): **ATP-PC**.
- Short-to-medium, high intensity (400 m, 800 m): **lactic acid**, with some aerobic.
- Long, steady (marathon, long swim): **aerobic**.
- Team sports (football, hockey, netball): a **mixture**; aerobic base with ATP-PC and lactic acid for sprints.

**Exam technique:** name the **system**, give the **reason** from intensity and duration, e.g. "ATP-PC, because the shot put is a very short, maximal movement using stored PC without oxygen".

### Aerobic and anaerobic exercise

- **Aerobic exercise**: exercise at a pace where the heart and lungs can supply enough oxygen; can be sustained for a long time (jogging, a 5 km run).
- **Anaerobic exercise**: exercise at such high intensity that oxygen cannot be supplied fast enough; short bursts (sprinting).

### Recovery: EPOC

After hard exercise, breathing and heart rate stay raised for a while. The extra oxygen taken in during recovery is **EPOC** (excess post-exercise oxygen consumption, sometimes called **oxygen debt**).

It is used to:
- **replace oxygen stores** in blood and muscle (myoglobin);
- **restore ATP and phosphocreatine** stores;
- **remove lactic acid** (converting it to glucose or using it as fuel);
- supply the **raised metabolism** and body temperature after exercise.

The harder the exercise, the **bigger the EPOC** and the longer the recovery. **Active recovery** (cool-down jogging) helps clear lactic acid faster than stopping.

**DOMS** (delayed onset muscle soreness): pain and stiffness 24–72 hours after unusual exercise, caused by tiny tears in muscle fibres (especially after eccentric work), not by lactic acid. A cool-down, stretching, rest, hydration and gradual build-up can reduce it.

### Short-term effects of exercise

These happen **during** and **just after** a session.

| System | Short-term effect | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Cardiovascular** | **Heart rate, stroke volume and cardiac output increase** | Deliver more oxygen and fuel to muscles |
| | **Vascular shunting**; blood pressure rises; skin flushes (to lose heat) | Redirect blood to muscles; cool the body |
| **Respiratory** | **Breathing rate and tidal volume increase** (so minute ventilation rises) | More oxygen in, more CO₂ out |
| **Muscular** | **Muscle temperature rises**; muscles become more flexible and contract faster; **lactic acid builds up**; muscle fatigue; micro-tears can cause DOMS | Energy released as heat; anaerobic work |
| **Energy** | Fuel stores (glycogen, PC) are used | ATP production |
| **Other** | Sweating; raised body temperature; hydration needed | Temperature control |

### Long-term effects (adaptations) of regular training

These happen over **weeks and months** of regular exercise.

| System | Long-term effect | Benefit |
|---|---|---|
| **Skeletal** | **Increased bone density** and strength; stronger ligaments and tendons; thicker cartilage | Lower risk of fractures and osteoporosis; fewer injuries |
| **Muscular** | **Hypertrophy** (bigger, stronger muscles); **increased muscle tone**; more **capillaries**, **myoglobin** and **mitochondria** | More force; less fatigue; more efficient aerobic energy production |
| **Cardiovascular** | **Cardiac hypertrophy** (bigger, stronger heart); **increased stroke volume**; **lower resting heart rate** (bradycardia); **increased maximum cardiac output**; more capillaries (**capillarisation**); lower blood pressure; **faster recovery** | Heart works more efficiently; more oxygen delivered |
| **Respiratory** | Stronger respiratory muscles; increased **vital capacity**; more **alveoli-capillary** surface in use; increased **minute ventilation at maximum** | More oxygen in and CO₂ out |
| **Energy systems** | Higher stores of ATP, PC, glycogen; **delayed onset of fatigue**; higher tolerance of lactic acid; **faster recovery** | Better performance and quicker recovery between efforts |

**How to link:** lower resting HR is the result of a **higher stroke volume**: the heart pumps more blood per beat, so it needs fewer beats to deliver the same cardiac output at rest (Q = HR × SV).

## Key terms and definitions

- **ATP**: adenosine triphosphate; the molecule that provides energy for muscle contraction.
- **Aerobic**: with oxygen.
- **Anaerobic**: without oxygen.
- **ATP-PC system**: energy system that uses phosphocreatine to re-make ATP quickly for about 10 seconds, without oxygen and without lactic acid.
- **Lactic acid system**: energy system that breaks down glucose without oxygen, producing ATP and lactic acid.
- **Aerobic system**: energy system that breaks down glucose (and fats) with oxygen to produce ATP, carbon dioxide and water.
- **Energy continuum**: the idea that the three energy systems work together, with the dominant one depending on intensity and duration.
- **EPOC**: excess post-exercise oxygen consumption; the extra oxygen taken in during recovery to restore the body to its resting state.
- **Lactic acid**: by-product of anaerobic glycolysis that causes muscle fatigue.
- **DOMS**: delayed onset muscle soreness; muscle pain and stiffness felt 24–72 hours after exercise.
- **Hypertrophy**: increase in the size of a muscle (or the heart).
- **Bradycardia**: a resting heart rate below 60 bpm, common in trained athletes.

## Worked examples

**1. Name the main energy system used by a shot putter and explain why. [2 marks]**

*Model answer:* ATP-PC system (1) because the throw is a very short, maximal effort (under 10 seconds), using stored phosphocreatine without oxygen (1).

**2. Explain what is meant by EPOC and why it is bigger after sprinting than after jogging. [3 marks]**

*Model answer:* EPOC is the extra oxygen taken in after exercise to return the body to its resting state (1). Sprinting is anaerobic, so a larger oxygen debt builds up and more lactic acid has to be removed and ATP/PC stores restored (1). Jogging is aerobic, so a smaller debt is built up (1).

**3. State three long-term effects of regular aerobic training on the cardiovascular system. [3 marks]**

*Model answer:* Resting heart rate decreases / bradycardia (1); stroke volume increases (1); heart size / strength increases (cardiac hypertrophy) (1). (Also accept: more capillaries, lower blood pressure, faster recovery.)

**4. Describe two short-term effects of exercise on the respiratory system. [2 marks]**

*Model answer:* Breathing rate increases (1); tidal volume increases (1).

## Common mistakes

- Saying lactic acid causes DOMS. DOMS is mainly **micro-tears in muscle**; lactic acid is cleared within hours.
- Writing "anaerobic = no energy" or that the aerobic system "only starts after 3 minutes". All systems work at once; one **dominates**.
- Saying that "the body switches off" one system. It is a **continuum**.
- Mixing up **short-term** (during/just after the session) and **long-term** (adaptations after weeks) effects.
- Mixing up **EPOC** and breathlessness. EPOC is the **extra oxygen used after exercise** for recovery.
- Writing "lactic acid" for the ATP-PC system (it makes no lactic acid) or forgetting that the aerobic system produces **CO₂ and water**.
- Not linking lower resting heart rate to **increased stroke volume**.
