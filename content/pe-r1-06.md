# Components of fitness and fitness testing

> **In the exam:** Paper 1 (and data skills also on Paper 2). Typical questions: name or define a component of fitness (1–2 marks), choose the component most important for a named sport and justify it (2–3 marks), name a fitness test for a component (1 mark), describe how to carry out a test (2–3 marks), and **interpret a table or graph of test results** against normative data (2–4 marks), including comment on the validity or reliability of a test (2 marks). Often feeds into a 4–6 mark question on a training programme.

## What you need to know

- The components of **health-related fitness**: cardiovascular fitness, muscular strength, muscular endurance, flexibility, body composition.
- The components of **skill-related fitness**: agility, balance, coordination, power, reaction time, speed.
- Which components matter in which sports, with justification.
- **Fitness tests** for each component, how they are done, and what they measure.
- **Why we test fitness**: limitations of testing, and what makes a test **valid, reliable, objective**.
- How to **interpret fitness test data** (comparing with normative data, comparing before/after, spotting errors).

## Notes

### Health-related fitness

| Component | Definition (exam wording) | Sport example |
|---|---|---|
| **Cardiovascular fitness (aerobic endurance)** | The ability of the heart, lungs and blood vessels to supply oxygen to the working muscles over a long period | Marathon, 5 km, cycling |
| **Muscular strength** | The maximum force a muscle (or group of muscles) can apply **once** against a resistance | Weightlifting 1-rep max, a rugby scrum push |
| **Muscular endurance** | The ability of a muscle to **work repeatedly** (contract many times) without tiring | Rowing, press-ups, a long swim |
| **Flexibility** | The range of movement possible at a joint | Gymnastic splits, a hurdler's leg lead |
| **Body composition** | The percentage of the body that is **fat, muscle, bone and water** | Weight-category sports (boxing, rowing), distance running (low fat) |

### Skill-related fitness

| Component | Definition | Sport example |
|---|---|---|
| **Agility** | The ability to **change direction quickly** while keeping control | Dodging in rugby, a slalom skier |
| **Balance** | Keeping the centre of mass over the base of support (static or dynamic) | Gymnastics beam, a skateboarder |
| **Coordination** | Using two or more body parts together **smoothly and efficiently** | A tennis serve, hand–eye in cricket |
| **Power** | **Strength × speed**: the ability to do strength movements quickly (force × velocity) | A high jump take-off, shot put, volleyball spike |
| **Reaction time** | The time taken to **respond to a stimulus** | Sprint start, goalkeeper saving a penalty |
| **Speed** | The rate of movement: how fast a body part or the whole body can move; speed = distance ÷ time | 100 m, a fast bowler's arm |

**Which component matters most (exam technique):** state the component and give a reason tied to the activity. "A goalkeeper needs **reaction time** to respond quickly to a shot" (1 + 1).

### Fitness tests: what they measure and how

| Component | Test | How it is done | Result measured |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cardiovascular fitness** | **Cooper 12-minute run** | Run as far as possible in 12 min | Distance covered (m) |
| | **Multi-stage fitness test (bleep test)** | Shuttle runs of 20 m to bleeps that get faster | Level and shuttle reached |
| | **Harvard step test** | Step up and down for up to 5 min, then measure recovery pulse | Heart rate recovery (fitness index) |
| **Muscular strength** | **Grip dynamometer** | Squeeze the dynamometer as hard as possible | Force in kg |
| **Muscular endurance** | **Sit-ups / press-ups in 1 minute** | Complete as many correct repetitions as possible in a minute | Number of repetitions |
| **Flexibility** | **Sit and reach test** | Sit with legs straight, reach forward along a ruler | Distance reached (cm) |
| **Body composition** | **BMI, skinfold calipers, bioelectrical impedance** | Height/weight; pinch of skin at sites; small current through the body | BMI value; % body fat |
| **Agility** | **Illinois agility run** | Run a set course of cones as fast as possible | Time (s) |
| **Balance** | **Stork stand** | Stand on one leg, heel raised, hands on hips | Time (s) until balance lost |
| **Coordination** | **Wall toss test** | Throw and catch a ball against a wall using alternate hands | Number of catches in 30 s |
| **Power** | **Vertical jump (Sargent) / standing broad jump** | Jump as high / far as possible | Height / distance (cm) |
| **Reaction time** | **Ruler drop test** | A ruler is dropped and caught; note where | Distance (cm) converted to time |
| **Speed** | **30 m sprint** | Sprint 30 m from a standing or crouched start | Time (s) |

(Use the tests named in your class or the specification. Any of these valid tests earns the mark if you name it correctly.)

### Why test fitness?

- To **identify strengths and weaknesses** and decide what to train.
- To **set goals** and see progress (retest after a training block).
- To **compare** with others/normative data, or to **check suitability** for a sport or position.
- To **motivate** performers.

**Limitations of fitness tests:** many are **not sport-specific** (a bleep test is not like a football match); some need equipment or a large space; **maximal tests** are demanding and risky for the unfit; results are affected by motivation, time of day, weather or surface; and a test measures only one component.

### Validity, reliability and objectivity

- **Valid**: the test really measures what it claims to measure (a sit and reach measures hamstring/lower back flexibility, **not** whole-body flexibility).
- **Reliable**: the test gives **consistent results** when repeated under the same conditions (same equipment, procedure, tester).
- **Objective**: the result is measured in numbers, with no tester opinion (a timed sprint rather than "looks fast").

To keep a test fair: same **surface, equipment, warm-up, time of day, tester** and **instructions** each time.

### Interpreting fitness test data

**Normative data** are tables of results from large groups (by age and gender) showing ratings such as "poor, below average, average, good, excellent".

How to answer data questions:
1. **Read the table carefully**: check the units and the row/column (gender, age).
2. **Compare the performer's score to the ratings** and say the category.
3. **Compare two scores** (before and after training, or two performers) and give the size of the change.
4. **Draw a conclusion** for the sport or the training plan.

**Illustration** (made-up example data): a Year 11 student records these results:

| Test | Before training | After 8 weeks |
|---|---|---|
| Cooper 12-min run | 2100 m | 2350 m |
| Sit and reach | 8 cm | 14 cm |
| 30 m sprint | 4.9 s | 4.7 s |

- Cooper: **+250 m**, a rise of about **12%** (250 ÷ 2100 × 100), so cardiovascular fitness has **improved**.
- Sit and reach: **+6 cm** so flexibility has improved.
- 30 m sprint: time has **decreased** by 0.2 s, so speed has improved (a **lower** time is better).

**Watch for:** a test where a **lower score is better** (sprint time, agility time, reaction time), and for results that have been affected by problems (a windy day, no warm-up).

### Percentage change

**% change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100.** Check whether the question asks for an increase or decrease and write the unit.

## Key terms and definitions

- **Health-related fitness**: the components of fitness that contribute to good health and everyday life.
- **Skill-related fitness**: the components of fitness that help performance in sport.
- **Cardiovascular fitness**: the ability of the heart, lungs and blood vessels to supply oxygen to the working muscles over a long period.
- **Muscular strength**: the maximum force a muscle can exert in a single contraction.
- **Muscular endurance**: the ability of a muscle to contract repeatedly without fatigue.
- **Flexibility**: the range of movement possible at a joint.
- **Body composition**: the proportion of fat, muscle, bone and water in the body.
- **Agility**: the ability to change direction quickly while keeping control.
- **Balance**: the ability to keep the body stable (centre of mass over the base of support).
- **Coordination**: the ability to use two or more body parts together smoothly and accurately.
- **Power**: the ability to produce strength quickly (strength × speed).
- **Reaction time**: the time taken to respond to a stimulus.
- **Speed**: the maximum rate at which a person can move.
- **Validity**: how well a test measures what it is meant to measure.
- **Reliability**: how consistent the results are when the test is repeated.
- **Normative data**: data collected from a large group, used to compare an individual's results.

## Worked examples

**1. Name the component of fitness most important to a sprinter at the start of a race and justify your choice. [2 marks]**

*Model answer:* Reaction time (1) so she can respond quickly to the starting gun and get off the blocks first (1).

**2. Describe how you would carry out the multi-stage fitness test. [3 marks]**

*Model answer:* Mark out a 20 m course (1). Run between the lines in time with bleeps, which get faster each level (1). Stop when you cannot reach the line in time twice; record the level/shuttle reached (1).

**3. A performer scores 2100 m in the Cooper test in September and 2310 m in December. Calculate the percentage improvement. [2 marks]**

*Model answer:* (2310 − 2100) ÷ 2100 × 100 (1) = 10% (1).

**4. Give one reason why a fitness test result may not be reliable and one way to make it more reliable. [2 marks]**

*Model answer:* Different conditions each time (e.g. weather or surface changed, or tester varied) (1). Standardise conditions: same equipment, surface, warm-up, time of day and tester (1).

## Common mistakes

- Mixing **muscular strength** (one maximum effort) with **muscular endurance** (many repetitions).
- Writing "fitness" or "stamina" without naming the component. Use the exact words, e.g. **cardiovascular fitness**.
- Defining **power** only as "strong". Power is **strength × speed**.
- Confusing **agility** (changing direction) with **speed** (straight-line) or **coordination**.
- Calling the sit and reach test a test of "all-round flexibility". It tests lower back and hamstring flexibility.
- In data questions, forgetting that **lower is better** for timed tests.
- Not giving a sporting reason when asked to "justify".
- Mixing up **valid** (measures the right thing) and **reliable** (repeatable).
