# Principles and methods of training

> **In the exam:** Appears on the theory papers as a mix of short answers (1–2 marks: name a principle, define overload, calculate a heart-rate zone) and longer 4–6 mark questions that ask you to design or justify a training programme for a named performer or sport. Examiners reward applying the principle to the performer given, not just naming it. Check with your teacher which paper this sits on in your course.

## What you need to know

- The principles of training: **specificity, progressive overload, reversibility, tedium** (SPORT), plus **individual differences / rest and recovery**.
- Applying overload with **FITT**: frequency, intensity, time, type.
- **Maximum heart rate** (220 − age), **training zones** (aerobic and anaerobic) and **target zones**.
- Training methods: **continuous, fartlek, interval, circuit, plyometrics, weight (resistance) training, flexibility (static, ballistic, PNF) training**.
- Which method suits which component of fitness and which activity.
- The idea of seasons of training (**pre-season, peak/competitive season, off-season**) and designing a simple programme.

## Notes

### Why train?

Training is a planned programme of exercise that improves fitness or performance. A good programme is **specific**, makes the body work harder than normal, can be kept up, and allows recovery.

### Principles of training (SPORT)

| Principle | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **S**pecificity | Training must match the needs of the sport, the muscles used and the fitness component needed | A sprinter does short, fast sprints, not long slow runs |
| **P**rogressive overload | Gradually make training harder so the body keeps adapting | Increase the weight lifted by a small amount each week |
| **R**eversibility | Fitness gained is lost when you stop training or train less (“use it or lose it”) | An injured player loses stamina after a few weeks of rest |
| **T**edium | Boredom caused by repeating the same training, which lowers motivation | Vary routes, drills or methods to stay interested |

Extra principles to know:

- **Overload**: working harder than you normally do so that the body adapts. It is the reason progressive overload works. Without overload, fitness does not improve.
- **Rest and recovery**: adaptation happens during recovery, not during the session. Too little rest causes **overtraining** (fatigue, injury, falling performance).
- **Individual differences**: each person has a different starting level, body type, goals and ability to recover, so programmes should be personal.

### FITT: how to apply overload

| Letter | Meaning | Ways to increase |
|---|---|---|
| **F**requency | How often you train | Train 4 days a week instead of 3 |
| **I**ntensity | How hard you train | Run faster, lift heavier, raise heart rate |
| **T**ime | How long each session lasts | Run for 40 minutes instead of 30 |
| **T**ype | The kind of training used | Add intervals or hill running |

Only change one or two variables at a time. Increasing everything at once leads to injury.

### Maximum heart rate and training zones

- **Maximum heart rate (max HR) = 220 − age.** For a 16-year-old: 220 − 16 = **204 bpm**.
- **Aerobic training zone:** about **60–80% of max HR**. Used to improve cardiovascular endurance and fat use. For max HR 204: 122–163 bpm.
- **Anaerobic training zone:** about **80–90% of max HR**. Used to improve speed, power and tolerance of lactic acid. For max HR 204: 163–184 bpm.
- Below 60% of max HR gives little training effect (a “recovery” level).

To find a percentage: **max HR × percentage ÷ 100**. So 70% of 204 = 204 × 0.7 = 142.8 ≈ 143 bpm.

Exam tip: show the working. “220 − 16 = 204; 204 × 0.6 = 122; 204 × 0.8 = 163” scores even if you slip on a final digit.

### Training methods

**Continuous training**
- Steady pace, no rest, usually 20–60+ minutes, in the aerobic zone (e.g. jogging, cycling, swimming).
- Improves **cardiovascular endurance** and muscular endurance.
- Advantages: easy to do, needs little equipment, good for beginners. Disadvantages: can be boring (tedium), does not improve speed or power, not specific to sports with sprints.

**Fartlek training** (Swedish for “speed play”)
- Continuous running where the pace and terrain change (walk, jog, sprint, hills) in an unplanned way.
- Improves aerobic and anaerobic fitness. Good for games players (football, hockey).
- Advantages: varied, can be done anywhere. Disadvantages: hard to measure progress, performer can choose to go easy.

**Interval training**
- Periods of **work** alternate with periods of **rest or recovery** (e.g. 8 × 200 m sprints with 60 s rest).
- You can change work time, rest time, intensity and number of repetitions (overload).
- Improves speed, anaerobic capacity and (with longer work periods) aerobic fitness. Good for sprinters and team sports.
- Advantages: easy to control and measure, specific to the sport. Disadvantages: very demanding, need a measured area and a stopwatch.

**Circuit training**
- A series of **stations** (e.g. press-ups, squats, sit-ups, burpees, step-ups) done in order, each for a set time or number of reps, with rest between.
- Improves muscular endurance, strength and some aerobic fitness. Stations can be matched to the sport.
- Advantages: many people can train at once, varied, easy to change difficulty. Disadvantages: needs space and equipment; stations may not suit every sport; people may use poor technique when tired.

**Plyometric training**
- Explosive bounding and jumping exercises (box jumps, depth jumps, hopping, clap press-ups) in which a muscle is stretched then quickly contracts.
- Improves **power** (speed + strength). Good for volleyball, basketball, sprinters and jumpers.
- Disadvantages: high injury risk if done badly, tough on joints; needs good fitness base and a safe landing surface.

**Weight (resistance) training**
- Lifting weights or using resistance machines. Key words: **repetitions (reps)** = number of lifts in one go; **sets** = a group of reps; **resistance** = the weight.
- Training for different outcomes:

| Aim | Weight | Reps | Sets |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strength | heavy (80%+ of max) | low (about 1–6) | 3–5 |
| Muscular endurance | light | high (15+) | 3 or more |
| Power | moderate, lifted quickly | low–moderate | 3–5 |

- Advantages: can target specific muscles, easy to progress. Disadvantages: needs equipment and supervision; risk of injury from bad technique or lifting too heavy.

**Flexibility training**
- **Static stretching:** hold a stretch with no movement (e.g. 15–30 seconds). **Active:** performer holds position using own muscles. **Passive:** a partner or object helps.
- **Ballistic stretching:** bouncing stretch. Higher risk of injury.
- **PNF (proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation):** stretch the muscle, contract it against a partner’s resistance for a few seconds, then stretch further. Very effective but needs a partner.
- Improves **flexibility**; useful for gymnasts, dancers, swimmers.

### Matching method to fitness component

| Fitness component | Best methods |
|---|---|
| Cardiovascular endurance | continuous, fartlek, interval (long work) |
| Muscular endurance | circuit, light-weight high-rep training |
| Strength | heavy weights, low reps |
| Speed | interval (sprints) |
| Power | plyometrics, weights lifted quickly |
| Flexibility | static, PNF stretching |

### Seasons of training (periodisation)

- **Pre-season:** build a general fitness base, then work on sport-specific fitness.
- **Peak / competition season:** maintain fitness, fine-tune skills and tactics, plenty of rest before matches.
- **Off-season (post-season):** active rest, keep moderately active, treat injuries, avoid big losses (reversibility).

### Designing a programme (4–6 mark questions)

1. Say the **aim** and the **fitness component** it needs.
2. Choose a **method** and say why it suits the sport.
3. Use **FITT** to describe it (e.g. 3 times a week, 70% max HR, 30 minutes, fartlek).
4. Say how you will apply **progressive overload**.
5. Mention **rest days** and avoiding tedium.

## Key terms and definitions

- **Training:** a planned programme of exercise designed to improve fitness or performance.
- **Specificity:** training that matches the requirements of the activity, muscles and fitness component needed.
- **Progressive overload:** gradually increasing the amount of work so the body continues to adapt.
- **Overload:** training harder than normal to force the body to adapt.
- **Reversibility:** fitness gains are lost when training stops or is reduced.
- **Tedium:** boredom from repeating the same training.
- **FITT:** frequency, intensity, time, type; ways of applying overload.
- **Maximum heart rate:** the highest number of beats per minute the heart can reach (220 − age).
- **Training zone:** the range of heart rate (as a % of max HR) in which training gives a specific benefit.
- **Repetition:** one complete movement of an exercise. **Set:** a group of repetitions.
- **Plyometrics:** explosive jumping training that improves power.

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** A 15-year-old’s maximum heart rate is estimated using 220 − age. Calculate the lower and upper limits of their aerobic training zone (60–80%). [3]

*Model answer:* Max HR = 220 − 15 = **205 bpm** (1). Lower limit = 205 × 0.6 = **123 bpm** (1). Upper limit = 205 × 0.8 = **164 bpm** (1). The zone is 123–164 bpm.

**Example 2.** A hockey player wants to improve speed. Describe how interval training could be used, and explain why it suits hockey. [4]

*Model answer:* Run a set distance as fast as possible, e.g. 6 × 30 m sprints (1), with a rest or jog recovery between each, e.g. 30 seconds (1). Make it harder by adding repetitions or shortening the rest (progressive overload) (1). Hockey involves repeated short sprints with recovery, so interval training is specific to the game (1).

**Example 3.** Explain what is meant by the principle of reversibility. [2]

*Model answer:* Fitness gained from training is lost (1) when you stop training or train less, e.g. after an injury or a long break (1).

## Common mistakes

- Writing “overload” when asked for a **principle** and not saying it must be **progressive**. Always say “gradually”.
- Mixing up **sets** and **reps**. A set is a group of reps.
- Saying fartlek is “interval training”. Fartlek is unplanned changes of speed with no set rest; interval has fixed work and rest.
- Forgetting the working in max HR questions, or using 200 − age.
- Naming a method without linking it to the sport or fitness component. “Circuit training” alone scores nothing at 4 marks.
- Saying “frequency” means how hard. Frequency = how **often**; intensity = how hard.
- Suggesting plyometrics for beginners without noting the injury risk.

## Practice questions

1. State the four principles of training that make up SPORT. [4]
2. Calculate the maximum heart rate of a 17-year-old, and 85% of it. [3]
3. What is the difference between frequency and intensity in FITT? [2]
4. Name the training method that has stations done in order with rest between. [1]
5. Explain why fartlek training is suitable for a netball or football player. [3]
6. A weightlifter wants to increase strength. Suggest suitable weight, reps and sets. [3]
7. Describe how a 400 m runner could apply progressive overload to an interval session. [3]
8. Explain what is meant by overtraining and one way to avoid it. [2]
