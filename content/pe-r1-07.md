# Principles and methods of training

> **In the exam:** Paper 1 (Theory, 1 hour 45 minutes, 100 marks, all questions compulsory). Expect a structured question on a named performer or sport: "Describe how overload could be applied using FITT" [3], "Explain why reversibility matters" [2], "Describe one advantage and one disadvantage of circuit training" [2], "Which training method would improve power? Justify your answer" [3], plus a calculation of 60–80% of maximum heart rate [2–3]. Examples must come from the Cambridge activity list.

## What you need to know

- **SPORT** principles: Specificity, Progression, Overload, Reversibility, Tedium.
- **FITT** to apply overload: Frequency, Intensity, Time, Type.
- The dangers of **overtraining**.
- Methods: **continuous, weight, fartlek, plyometric, circuit, HIIT** – description, advantages, disadvantages, safety, and which fitness component each improves.
- Calculating intensity: **60–80% of maximum heart rate** (continuous), **1 rep max** (weights), **Borg scale** (fartlek).
- **High-altitude training**: why it is used, advantages and disadvantages.
- Warm-up and cool-down are covered in the topic "Injury prevention and performance-enhancing drugs".

## Notes

### SPORT: the principles of training

| Principle | Meaning | Example (a 1500 m runner) |
|---|---|---|
| **Specificity** | Training matches the demands of the activity and the component of fitness you want to improve | Long continuous runs and intervals, not just bench press |
| **Progression** | Gradually increase the workload so the body keeps adapting | Add 5 minutes to the run each week |
| **Overload** | Work harder than normal so the body adapts (use FITT) | Run at 75% instead of 65% of max heart rate |
| **Reversibility** | Fitness gained is lost when training stops or is reduced (use it or lose it) | Fitness drops after 6 weeks off with injury |
| **Tedium** | Boredom from repeating the same training, which lowers motivation | Vary routes, use fartlek or a group session |

### FITT: how to apply overload

| Letter | Meaning | How to overload |
|---|---|---|
| **F**requency | How often you train | 3 sessions a week → 4 |
| **I**ntensity | How hard | Faster pace, heavier weight, higher heart rate |
| **T**ime | How long each session lasts | 20 minutes → 30 minutes |
| **T**ype | The method of training used | Switch from continuous to interval to suit the aim |

Do not increase everything at once: progress gradually (progression) or you risk injury.

### Overtraining

**Overtraining** = training too much, too hard or without enough rest. Dangers: injuries (e.g. stress fractures, muscle strains), long-term fatigue, weaker immune system/more illness, loss of motivation, and **performance getting worse**. Prevent it with rest days, varied sessions and progression.

### Methods of training

| Method | What it is | Mainly improves | Advantages | Disadvantages | Safety |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Continuous** | Steady work for 20+ minutes without rest: run, swim, cycle, row. Intensity 60–80% of max HR | Cardiovascular endurance, muscular endurance of legs | Little equipment, easy to do alone, burns fat, good for beginners | Can be boring (tedium), does not improve speed/power, repetitive-strain risk | Suitable footwear, hydrate |
| **Weight** | Resistance work with free weights, kettlebells or machines; isotonic. Sets and reps | Strength (heavy weight, few reps), muscular endurance (light weight, many reps), power | Targets specific muscles, easy to progress, measurable | Needs equipment/gym, can cause injury with poor technique | Spotter, correct technique, collars on bars |
| **Fartlek** ("speed play") | Continuous running with changes of speed and terrain (sprint, jog, walk, hills) | Aerobic and anaerobic fitness | Varied (less boring), no equipment, can be done anywhere, suits games players | Hard to measure and control intensity, easy to go easy on yourself | Check equipment/terrain |
| **Plyometric** | Explosive bounding and jumping: depth jumps, hurdle jumps, box jumps | **Power** | Good for explosive sports (jumping, sprinting) | High injury risk to joints and muscles, needs a strength base | Soft surface, good landing technique, build up gradually |
| **Circuit** | A series of stations (e.g. 8–10), each for a set time then moving on; each station can target a different fitness component or muscle group | Muscular endurance, strength, general fitness | Varied, many people at once, can be matched to any sport | Needs space and equipment, can be hard to organise | Equipment set up safely, enough space |
| **HIIT** | High-intensity intervals (e.g. 30 s all-out) with rest or low-intensity periods | Cardiovascular endurance, speed, anaerobic capacity | Short sessions, improves fitness quickly | Very demanding, risk of overexertion/injury, not for beginners | Warm up, build up slowly, monitor effort |

**Work and rest periods** (circuit and HIIT): the ratio can be changed to change the training effect. More rest and shorter work = higher quality, more power/speed. Short rest = more endurance. Rest lets the body **remove waste products** (e.g. lactic acid) and recover.

### Calculating intensity

- **Maximum heart rate (MHR) = 220 − age.** For a 16-year-old: 220 − 16 = 204 bpm.
- **Aerobic training zone (continuous training) = 60–80% of MHR.** 60% of 204 = 122 bpm; 80% = 163 bpm. So she should train at about **122–163 bpm**.
- **1 rep max (1RM)** = the heaviest weight you can lift once with good technique. Choose weights as a % of 1RM: heavy (about 80–90%) for strength, lighter (about 50–70%) with more reps for muscular endurance.
- **Borg scale** (rating of perceived exertion, RPE, 6–20): you rate how hard you feel you are working; useful in fartlek where heart rate changes constantly. Roughly RPE × 10 ≈ heart rate.

### High-altitude training

Training (or living) at **altitude**, where there is less oxygen in the air. The body responds by making **more red blood cells**, so more oxygen can be carried.

- **Advantage:** better oxygen delivery → better **endurance** performance (e.g. marathon, cycling) when back at sea level.
- **Disadvantages:** hard to train at normal intensity (less oxygen so tiredness comes sooner), altitude sickness (headache, nausea), expensive, travel and time away from home, and the benefit disappears within a few weeks (**reversibility**).

### Choosing a method (exam skill)

| Aim / sport | Best method | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Marathon runner (endurance) | Continuous | Long, steady aerobic work |
| Volleyball player (jump power) | Plyometric | Explosive jumps train power |
| Rugby player (strength) | Weight training | Overload muscles with heavy resistance |
| Footballer (varied sprints + jogging) | Fartlek or HIIT | Copies the stop-start pattern of the game |
| School team wanting several fitness components at once | Circuit | Stations for each component |

## Key terms and definitions

- **Specificity:** training must match the needs of the activity and the fitness component to be improved.
- **Progression:** gradually increasing the amount of training so the body keeps improving.
- **Overload:** making the body work harder than it is used to so that it adapts.
- **Reversibility:** training adaptations are lost when training stops or reduces.
- **Tedium:** boredom caused by training that is repetitive.
- **FITT:** Frequency, Intensity, Time, Type: ways to apply overload.
- **Overtraining:** training too much with too little rest, leading to injury, illness or lower performance.
- **Maximum heart rate:** the highest number of beats per minute, estimated as 220 − age.
- **1 rep max:** the maximum weight a person can lift once.
- **Plyometrics:** explosive jumping exercises that improve power.

## Worked examples

**1. Anna is a netball player. Describe how she could use the FITT principle to apply overload to her training. [4]**

*Model answer:* Frequency: increase from 2 to 3 sessions a week [1]. Intensity: work harder, e.g. run at a higher percentage of her maximum heart rate or lift heavier weights [1]. Time: lengthen each session, e.g. 30 to 40 minutes [1]. Type: change method, e.g. add interval training to improve speed [1].
*Marks are earned by giving a **specific change** for each letter, not just naming it.*

**2. Calculate the training zone for continuous training for a 15-year-old. Show your working. [3]**

*Model answer:* MHR = 220 − 15 = 205 [1]. 60% = 123 bpm [1]. 80% = 164 bpm [1]. (Zone 123–164 bpm.)

**3. Explain why a footballer who stops training for six weeks with an injury loses fitness. [2]**

*Model answer:* This is **reversibility** [1]: the body adapts to the training load, so when training stops the improvements (e.g. aerobic capacity, muscle strength) gradually disappear [1].

**4. Explain one disadvantage of plyometric training for a long-jumper. [2]**

*Model answer:* The high-impact landings put a lot of stress on the joints and muscles [1], which can cause injuries (e.g. sprains or strains) if technique is poor or a beginner does too much [1].

## Common mistakes

- Writing "specific" without saying **what** is specific (the sport, the muscles, the component of fitness).
- Giving FITT answers like "intensity: train harder". Add a measurable change.
- Confusing **overload** (what we do) and **progression** (doing it gradually over time).
- Saying circuit training only improves "all fitness". Name the components: muscular endurance, strength, etc.
- Mixing up the 60–80% zone with 220 − age: calculate the max first, then take the percentages.
- Saying weight training "makes you bulky" for every goal: light weight and many reps builds muscular endurance.
- Writing about overtraining as just "getting tired": give injury, illness or reduced performance.
