# Preventing injury and performance-enhancing drugs

> **In the exam:** Appears on the theory papers as short answers (1–2 marks: name an injury, give a risk, state the aim of a warm-up) and 3–6 mark questions that ask you to explain how injuries are prevented or treated, or to evaluate the use of a drug for a named sport. For drug questions you must link the drug to the sport and give both an effect and a risk. Check with your teacher which paper this sits on.

## What you need to know

- The **purpose and structure of a warm-up and cool-down**, and their effects on the body.
- **Ways to prevent injury**: warm-up, protective clothing and equipment, correct technique, rules and officials, fitness, risk assessment, suitable environment.
- **Common sports injuries**: soft tissue (sprain, strain, torn cartilage, blisters, bruises), hard tissue (fractures, dislocations) and others (concussion, tennis elbow, shin splints, Achilles tendonitis).
- **Treatment** of minor injuries: PRICE / RICE and when to seek medical help.
- **Performance-enhancing drugs (PEDs)**: anabolic steroids, beta blockers, diuretics, stimulants, narcotic analgesics, peptide hormones (EPO, HGH), plus blood doping. Their **effects, risks and the sports that misuse them**.
- Why athletes take drugs, and why they are banned (fair play, health, role models).

## Notes

### Warm-up

A warm-up prepares the body for exercise. A good one has three parts:

1. **Pulse raiser**: light jogging or skipping for 5–10 minutes. Gradually increases heart rate, breathing rate and muscle temperature.
2. **Stretching**: dynamic stretches for the muscles to be used (leg swings, arm circles) and sometimes static stretches to improve range of movement.
3. **Skill-related practice**: sport-specific drills (passing, shooting, practice serves) at gradually increasing speed.

**Why it helps:**
- Warmer muscles are more elastic and contract faster and more forcefully.
- Blood flow to muscles increases, so more oxygen arrives.
- Joints are lubricated (more synovial fluid), improving range of movement.
- Mentally prepares the performer and rehearses skills.
- Lowers the risk of **muscle strains** and other injuries.

### Cool-down

A cool-down returns the body to its resting state:

1. **Light exercise** (slow jog or walk) for 5–10 minutes keeps blood flowing so **waste products such as lactic acid** are removed, and the heart rate falls gradually.
2. **Stretching** (static) of the muscles used maintains or improves flexibility.

Benefits: reduces the chance of **blood pooling** and dizziness, helps reduce muscle soreness (DOMS), speeds up recovery.

### Preventing injuries

| Method | How it helps |
|---|---|
| Warm-up and cool-down | Prepares muscles and joints; speeds recovery |
| Correct technique | Reduces strain on joints (e.g. lifting with a straight back, correct tackling) |
| Protective equipment | Shin pads, gum shields, helmets, pads and guards absorb impact |
| Suitable clothing and footwear | Right studs for the surface; layers for cold; sun cream and hat in heat |
| Rules and officials | Ban dangerous play; referees stop play for fouls and injuries; weight and age categories |
| Fitness and training | A fit, strong body is less likely to be injured; avoid overtraining |
| Safe environment | Check pitch, court and equipment; remove hazards; do not play on frozen or waterlogged surfaces |
| Hydration and nutrition | Dehydration causes cramp and fatigue, which lead to injury |
| Risk assessment | Identify hazards, decide how likely and how serious, then control them |
| Rest | Avoid playing while injured or exhausted |

### Common injuries

**Soft tissue injuries**
- **Sprain**: a stretched or torn **ligament** (bone to bone), often at the ankle. Signs: pain, swelling, bruising, joint unstable.
- **Strain**: a pulled or torn **muscle or tendon** (muscle to bone), e.g. hamstring. Signs: sudden pain, weakness, swelling.
- **Torn cartilage**: damage to cartilage in the knee, often from twisting. Pain, swelling, joint locking.
- **Tendonitis** (e.g. Achilles, tennis elbow): inflammation of a tendon from overuse. Pain with movement.
- **Blisters, abrasions, bruises**.

**Hard tissue injuries**
- **Fracture**: a broken bone. **Closed** (simple): skin intact. **Open** (compound): bone breaks the skin. **Stress fracture**: small crack from repeated impact (e.g. distance runners).
- **Dislocation**: bone forced out of its joint position (e.g. shoulder).

**Other**
- **Concussion**: brain injury after a blow to the head. Signs: headache, confusion, dizziness, nausea. Player must stop at once and not return the same day. Seek medical help.
- **Shin splints**: pain along the shin bone from repeated impact.
- **Hypothermia, heat exhaustion**: from extreme temperatures.

### Treating soft-tissue injuries: PRICE

- **P**rotection: stop and protect the area from further harm.
- **R**est: stop using it.
- **I**ce: wrapped ice pack for about 10–20 minutes to reduce swelling and pain (not directly on skin).
- **C**ompression: bandage firmly (not too tight) to limit swelling.
- **E**levation: raise the injury above heart level to reduce swelling.

Avoid heat and alcohol for the first 48 hours. Seek medical help for suspected fractures, dislocations, head injuries, or if pain is severe or not improving.

### Performance-enhancing drugs (PEDs)

Drugs taken to improve sporting performance. Most are **banned by WADA** (World Anti-Doping Agency) and athletes are tested in and out of competition.

| Drug | Effect on performance | Health risks | Sports |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Anabolic steroids** | Build muscle mass, strength and power; faster recovery | Liver damage, high blood pressure, heart disease, aggression, acne; in males: smaller testicles, in females: deeper voice, facial hair | Weightlifting, sprinting, throwing events, rugby |
| **Beta blockers** | Slow heart rate and reduce shaking, calming nerves and giving steadiness | Low blood pressure, tiredness, slow heart rate, breathing problems | Archery, shooting, snooker, darts |
| **Diuretics** | Remove water so weight drops quickly; can mask other drugs | Dehydration, cramps, dizziness, low blood pressure | Boxing, judo, horse racing (making weight) |
| **Stimulants** (e.g. amphetamines) | Increase alertness, reduce fatigue, raise heart rate | Addiction, high blood pressure, heart problems, anxiety, can hide pain leading to injury | Cycling, football, athletics |
| **Narcotic analgesics** (e.g. morphine) | Block pain so training and competing continue | Addiction, injury made worse as pain is masked, breathing problems | Contact sports, any with high injury rates |
| **Peptide hormones**: **EPO** | Raises red blood cell count so more oxygen is carried; improves endurance | Thicker blood, risk of blood clots, stroke, heart attack | Endurance cycling, distance running |
| **Peptide hormones**: **HGH** (human growth hormone) | Muscle growth and repair, fat reduction | Abnormal bone growth, diabetes risk, heart problems | Strength and power sports |

**Blood doping**: removing and storing the athlete’s own blood, then re-infusing it before competition so there are more red blood cells. Improves endurance. Risks: blood clots, infection, heart strain. Banned.

### Why use PEDs, and why they are banned

**Reasons athletes use them:** winning brings money, fame and sponsorship; pressure from coaches or nations; belief that others are cheating; short careers.

**Arguments against:** it is **cheating** (unfair advantage), it harms **health**, it sets a bad example to young people, and it damages the reputation of the sport. Consequences: bans, loss of medals and titles, fines, loss of sponsors, reputation.

Some athletes may take legal supplements (e.g. protein, creatine); these are different from banned drugs, but athletes should check ingredients as contaminated products can cause a positive test.

## Key terms and definitions

- **Warm-up:** preparation for exercise made up of a pulse raiser, stretching and skill practice.
- **Cool-down:** light exercise and stretching after activity that returns the body to its resting state.
- **Sprain:** damage to a ligament. **Strain:** damage to a muscle or tendon.
- **Fracture:** a break or crack in a bone. **Dislocation:** a bone displaced from its joint.
- **Concussion:** a brain injury caused by a blow to the head or body.
- **PRICE:** protection, rest, ice, compression, elevation.
- **Performance-enhancing drug:** a substance taken to improve sporting performance, usually banned.
- **WADA:** World Anti-Doping Agency, which sets the list of banned substances.
- **Blood doping:** increasing the number of red blood cells (e.g. by re-infusing stored blood) to improve oxygen carrying.
- **Risk assessment:** identifying hazards and deciding how to reduce them.

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** Describe the three parts of a warm-up. [3]

*Model answer:* A **pulse raiser** such as light jogging to increase heart rate and body temperature (1); **stretching** of the main muscles to be used (1); **skill-related practice** such as passing drills at increasing intensity (1).

**Example 2.** An archer is offered beta blockers. Explain why an archer might take them and give one risk. [3]

*Model answer:* Beta blockers slow the heart rate (1) and reduce muscle tremor / calm nerves (1), so the archer can hold the bow steadier. One risk is low blood pressure or tiredness / breathing problems (1).

**Example 3.** A netball player twists her ankle and it swells. Name the injury and describe how it should be treated. [4]

*Model answer:* A **sprain** (ligament damage) (1). Use PRICE: protect and **rest** the ankle (1); apply **ice** wrapped in cloth (1); use a **compression** bandage and **elevate** the ankle (1). Seek medical help if it is severe.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing **sprain** (ligament) and **strain** (muscle/tendon).
- Saying a warm-up “prevents” injury without saying how (warmer, more elastic muscles; more blood flow; joints lubricated).
- Naming a drug effect without a **sport**. “Beta blockers calm nerves” scores more when linked to archery or shooting.
- Giving only the benefit of a drug. Mark schemes want both an effect and a risk.
- Putting ice directly on the skin or using heat on a fresh injury.
- Saying a cool-down “gets rid of lactic acid” without noting the part played by keeping blood flowing.
- Calling creatine or protein shakes “banned drugs”.
