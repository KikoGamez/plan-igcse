# Diet and nutrition

> **In the exam:** Appears on the theory papers as 1-mark recall (name a nutrient, state a function), 2–3 mark “explain why” questions (why a marathon runner eats carbohydrate, why athletes drink water) and sometimes a 4–6 mark question about the diet of a named performer before, during or after competition. Linking the nutrient to the sport earns the marks. Check with your teacher which paper this sits on.

## What you need to know

- A **balanced diet** and the **seven components**: carbohydrates, fats, proteins, vitamins, minerals, fibre, water.
- The **function and sources** of each, and the difference between **macronutrients** and **micronutrients**.
- Why the **proportions** of nutrients differ between sports and performers.
- **Hydration**: why water matters, effects of **dehydration**, and fluid replacement.
- **Diet before, during and after** exercise (e.g. carbohydrate loading, recovery).
- Energy needs, **energy balance** and the effect of diet on weight and performance.

## Notes

### A balanced diet

A **balanced diet** contains all the nutrients the body needs, in the right amounts, and provides the energy needed for daily life and activity. Typically about **50–60% carbohydrate, 25–30% fat, 12–15% protein** (more carbohydrate for endurance athletes, more protein for strength athletes), plus enough vitamins, minerals, fibre and water.

### The seven components

**Macronutrients** are needed in large amounts (carbohydrate, fat, protein). **Micronutrients** (vitamins, minerals) are needed in small amounts. Fibre and water are also essential.

| Nutrient | Main function | Sources | Sporting link |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Carbohydrate** | Main source of **energy**; stored as **glycogen** in muscles and liver | Simple: sweets, fruit, sports drinks. Complex: pasta, rice, bread, potatoes | Endurance athletes need lots; glycogen runs out in long events |
| **Fat** | Energy store (more energy per gram), insulation, protects organs, carries fat-soluble vitamins | Unsaturated: oily fish, nuts, olive oil. Saturated: butter, fatty meat | Used for long, low-intensity exercise; too much saturated fat harms the heart |
| **Protein** | Growth and **repair** of muscle and other tissue | Meat, fish, eggs, dairy, beans, tofu | Strength athletes need more to build muscle; used after training |
| **Vitamins** | Help body processes: e.g. vitamin C (immune system, collagen), vitamin D (calcium absorption), B vitamins (energy release) | Fruit, vegetables, dairy, sunlight (D) | Deficiency can reduce performance and cause illness |
| **Minerals** | E.g. **calcium** (strong bones and teeth), **iron** (haemoglobin to carry oxygen), sodium (fluid balance) | Dairy, red meat, green vegetables | Iron is vital for endurance; calcium helps prevent stress fractures |
| **Fibre** | Keeps the digestive system working, prevents constipation | Wholegrain, fruit, vegetables | Helps digestive health and keeps you fuller |
| **Water** | Hydration, regulates temperature, transports nutrients and waste, part of blood | Drinks, food | Lost in sweat; essential for performance |

**Energy values:** carbohydrate and protein give about **4 kcal per gram** (17 kJ), fat gives about **9 kcal per gram** (37 kJ).

### Simple and complex carbohydrates

- **Simple carbohydrates** (sugars) are digested quickly, giving a fast energy boost. Useful during or just after exercise.
- **Complex carbohydrates** (starch) release energy slowly, so are good for the meals before a long event.

### Hydration and dehydration

Water makes up about 60% of the body. During exercise you lose water through **sweat** and breathing.

**Effects of dehydration:**
- Blood becomes thicker, so the heart works harder and heart rate rises.
- Body temperature rises (less sweating, heat exhaustion).
- Fatigue, headaches, dizziness, cramp.
- Poorer concentration, reaction time and decision-making.
- Reduced endurance and performance (even 2% loss of body weight can reduce performance).

**Rehydration:** drink water before, during and after exercise. Pale urine shows you are well hydrated; dark yellow urine shows you need to drink. For events over about an hour or very hot conditions, **isotonic sports drinks** replace water, sugar and salts.

### Diet around competition

| Time | What to do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Days before** (endurance event) | **Carbohydrate loading**: eat more carbohydrates (pasta, rice) and reduce training | Increases glycogen stores in muscle and liver |
| **2–4 hours before** | Meal high in complex carbohydrate, low in fat and fibre | Slow, steady energy, easily digested |
| **During (long events)** | Sports drink, gels or banana | Keeps blood sugar up, replaces fluids |
| **After** | Carbohydrate **and protein** within about 30–60 minutes; rehydrate | Replaces glycogen and repairs muscle |

### Diet and different performers

- **Endurance athletes** (marathon): high carbohydrate, plenty of fluids and iron.
- **Strength / power athletes** (weightlifters): more protein for muscle growth, enough carbohydrate for training.
- **Weight-category athletes** (boxers, jockeys): careful control of energy intake; must avoid unsafe methods like dehydration.
- **Young athletes**: need calcium and vitamin D for growing bones, and enough energy for growth as well as training.

### Energy balance

If you take in more energy than you use, extra energy is stored as fat (weight gain). If you take in less than you use, weight is lost. Energy needs depend on **age, gender, body size, activity level and metabolic rate**. An active teenager needs more energy than a sedentary one.

### Poor diet effects

Too much saturated fat, salt and sugar can lead to obesity, high blood pressure, heart disease, type 2 diabetes and tooth decay. Too little energy, protein or iron leads to tiredness, loss of muscle, anaemia and poor performance.

## Key terms and definitions

- **Balanced diet:** a diet containing all the nutrients in the correct amounts to meet the body’s needs.
- **Macronutrients:** nutrients needed in large amounts: carbohydrate, fat and protein.
- **Micronutrients:** nutrients needed in small amounts: vitamins and minerals.
- **Glycogen:** the form in which carbohydrate is stored in muscles and liver.
- **Carbohydrate loading:** increasing carbohydrate intake (and tapering training) before an endurance event to maximise glycogen stores.
- **Dehydration:** excessive loss of water from the body.
- **Isotonic drink:** a drink with the same concentration of salts and sugars as body fluids, replacing fluids and energy.
- **Fibre:** plant material that is not digested and helps the digestive system.
- **Energy balance:** the relationship between energy taken in and energy used.

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** Explain why a marathon runner eats pasta the night before a race. [3]

*Model answer:* Pasta is a **complex carbohydrate** (1) which is stored as **glycogen** in the muscles and liver (1). This provides a store of energy for the long race, delaying fatigue (1).

**Example 2.** Give two effects of dehydration on a footballer during a match. [2]

*Model answer:* Thicker blood so the heart works harder / higher heart rate (1); body temperature rises / cramp / fatigue / slower reactions (1).

**Example 3.** A weightlifter wants to build muscle. Explain what nutrient they should eat more of and why. [2]

*Model answer:* **Protein** (1) because it is needed for the growth and repair of muscle tissue after training (1).

## Common mistakes

- Saying that protein is the main energy source for exercise. Carbohydrate is the main source; protein is for growth and repair.
- Writing “carbs give you energy” without mentioning **glycogen** when asked to explain.
- Forgetting that **fat** has more energy per gram (9 kcal) than carbohydrate or protein (4 kcal).
- Mixing up **vitamins** and **minerals** (iron and calcium are minerals).
- Saying athletes should stop eating fat. A small amount of unsaturated fat is needed.
- Writing “drink water so you don’t get thirsty”. Say it replaces sweat and prevents **dehydration**, which affects performance.
- Saying a sports drink is needed for every session; for short sessions water is enough.

8. Suggest what a rugby player should eat after a match and why. [3]
