# Motion and graphs

> **In the exam:** Motion appears in almost every series. Paper 2: 2–4 multiple-choice items (reading graphs, scalar/vector, average speed). Paper 4: usually part of the first question, 5–9 marks (speed–time graph, gradient = acceleration, area = distance, terminal velocity explanation). Paper 6: timing (pendulums, rolling balls), measuring multiples, plotting graphs and finding gradients.

## What you need to know

- Use rulers and measuring cylinders; time intervals with clocks and digital timers; find an average for a small distance or short time by measuring multiples (e.g. the period of a pendulum).
- Scalars have magnitude only (distance, speed, time, mass, energy, temperature); vectors have magnitude and direction (force, weight, velocity, acceleration, momentum, electric field strength, gravitational field strength).
- *Extended:* determine, by calculation or graphically, the resultant of two vectors at right angles (forces or velocities only).
- Define speed as distance travelled per unit time; velocity as speed in a given direction; average speed = total distance ÷ total time.
- Sketch, plot and interpret distance–time and speed–time graphs; recognise rest, constant speed, accelerating, decelerating.
- Speed from the gradient of a distance–time graph; distance from the area under a speed–time graph.
- Acceleration of free fall g near the Earth's surface is approximately constant, about 9.8 m/s².
- *Extended:* define acceleration as change in velocity per unit time; acceleration from the gradient of a speed–time graph; recognise constant and changing acceleration; deceleration is negative acceleration; describe falling with and without air/liquid resistance, including terminal velocity.

## Notes

### Equations (no formula sheet — learn these)

| Equation | Symbols and units |
|---|---|
| v = s ÷ t | v speed (m/s), s distance (m), t time (s) |
| average speed = total distance travelled ÷ total time taken | m/s |
| a = Δv ÷ Δt *(Extended)* | a acceleration (m/s²), Δv change in velocity (m/s), Δt time taken (s) |
| Resultant of two perpendicular vectors: R = √(A² + B²), angle θ = tan⁻¹(B ÷ A) *(Extended)* | same unit as the vectors (N or m/s) |
| Area of triangle = ½ × base × height; area of trapezium = ½(a + b) × h | used for distance under speed–time graphs |

Unit conversion you must be fast at: 1 km/h = 1000 m ÷ 3600 s, so **÷ 3.6** to go from km/h to m/s (72 km/h = 20 m/s).

### Scalars and vectors

A **scalar** has magnitude only; a **vector** has magnitude and direction. Distance and speed are scalars; displacement and velocity are vectors. A car going round a roundabout at constant speed has a changing velocity (direction changes), so it is accelerating.

**Two vectors at right angles (Extended):** draw them head-to-tail, the resultant is the hypotenuse. Either calculate with Pythagoras and tan, or draw a scale diagram (state the scale, measure length and angle with a protractor).

### Distance–time graphs

| Shape | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Horizontal line | at rest |
| Straight sloping line | constant speed |
| Curve getting steeper | accelerating |
| Curve getting less steep | decelerating |

**Gradient = speed.** For a curve, the speed at an instant is the gradient of the tangent at that point.

### Speed–time graphs

| Shape | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Horizontal line | constant speed |
| Straight line sloping up | constant acceleration |
| Straight line sloping down | constant deceleration |
| Curve with gradient decreasing | acceleration decreasing (e.g. approaching terminal velocity) |
| Line on the time axis | at rest |

- **Gradient = acceleration** (Extended). A negative gradient is a deceleration (negative acceleration).
- **Area under the line = distance travelled.** Split into rectangles and triangles. Check the units on the axes (minutes? km/h?).

### Free fall and terminal velocity (Extended)

- Without air resistance, all objects near the Earth fall with the same constant acceleration g ≈ 9.8 m/s², whatever their mass.
- With air resistance (drag):
  1. At the start, speed is zero so drag is zero; resultant force = weight, acceleration = g.
  2. As speed increases, drag increases; resultant force (weight − drag) decreases, so acceleration decreases.
  3. When drag = weight, resultant force is zero, acceleration is zero: the object moves at constant **terminal velocity**.
- Opening a parachute: area increases → drag increases suddenly → drag > weight → resultant force upwards → object decelerates → drag falls until it again equals weight → new, lower terminal velocity.
- The speed–time graph for a skydiver: curve rising with decreasing gradient → flat → sharp drop → flat at lower level.
- The same reasoning applies to a ball bearing falling through oil (liquid resistance).

### Paper 6 skills for this topic

- **Measuring multiples:** time 20 oscillations of a pendulum and divide by 20; measure the thickness of 100 sheets of paper and divide by 100. This reduces the effect of reaction time / ruler precision on the result.
- **Timing:** stop-watches read to 0.01 s but human reaction time is about 0.1–0.2 s, so record times sensibly and repeat (average the repeats).
- **Pendulum period:** measure from the centre of the bob; start timing as the bob passes the centre (fastest point) or use a fiducial marker; use small amplitude.
- **Rolling ball / trolley down a ramp:** measure distance with a metre rule, time with a stop-watch; repeat; keep the release point the same; release, don't push.
- **Tables:** column headings as quantity / unit (e.g. t / s); same number of decimal places in a column; no units in the body.
- **Graphs:** label axes with quantity and unit, scale using more than half the grid, plot to half a small square, single thin best-fit line, gradient from a large triangle (hypotenuse at least half the line) shown on the graph.

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Speed | Distance travelled per unit time |
| Velocity | Speed in a given direction |
| Acceleration | Change in velocity per unit time |
| Scalar | Quantity with magnitude (size) only |
| Vector | Quantity with magnitude and direction |
| Deceleration | Negative acceleration (speed decreasing) |
| Terminal velocity | Constant (maximum) velocity reached when drag (air/liquid resistance) equals weight, so the resultant force is zero |
| Acceleration of free fall, g | Acceleration of an object falling with no air resistance; approximately 9.8 m/s² near the Earth's surface |

## Worked examples

**1.** A car accelerates uniformly from 4.0 m/s to 18 m/s in 7.0 s. (a) Calculate its acceleration. [2] (b) Calculate the distance travelled in this time. [2]

- (a) a = Δv ÷ Δt = (18 − 4.0) ÷ 7.0 (C1) = **2.0 m/s²** (A1)
- (b) distance = area under speed–time graph = ½ × (4.0 + 18) × 7.0 (C1) = **77 m** (A1)

**2.** A runner runs 120 m in 15 s, rests for 10 s, then runs 80 m in 20 s. (a) Calculate the speed during the first section. [1] (b) Calculate the average speed for the whole journey. [2]

- (a) 120 ÷ 15 = **8.0 m/s** (B1)
- (b) total distance = 200 m, total time = 45 s (C1); average speed = 200 ÷ 45 = **4.4 m/s** (A1). (Include the 10 s rest in the total time.)

**3.** A skydiver jumps from a plane. Explain why she reaches a terminal velocity. [4]

- At first only weight acts / air resistance is zero, so she accelerates (at g). (B1)
- As her speed increases, air resistance increases. (B1)
- The resultant force (weight − air resistance) decreases, so her acceleration decreases. (B1)
- When air resistance equals weight, resultant force is zero, so acceleration is zero and speed is constant. (B1)

**4.** A boat heads straight across a river at 4.0 m/s relative to the water. The river flows at 3.0 m/s at right angles to this. Calculate the resultant velocity of the boat. [3]

- magnitude = √(4.0² + 3.0²) (C1) = **5.0 m/s** (A1)
- direction: tan θ = 3.0 ÷ 4.0, θ = **37° downstream from the straight-across direction** (B1)

## Common mistakes

- Forgetting to convert units: minutes to seconds, km/h to m/s, cm to m.
- Leaving out the time spent at rest when calculating average speed.
- Reading the gradient of a speed–time graph as speed, or the area under a distance–time graph as anything meaningful (it is not).
- Writing "it reaches terminal velocity because forces are balanced" without explaining how drag changes with speed — examiners want the sequence: speed ↑ → drag ↑ → resultant force ↓ → acceleration ↓.
- Saying "the forces cancel so it stops" — zero resultant force means constant velocity, not rest.
- Calling velocity or acceleration a scalar; giving a vector answer with no direction.
- Taking a gradient from a tiny triangle or from data points not on the line.

## Practice questions

1. Which list contains only vector quantities? A distance, force, mass B velocity, acceleration, momentum C speed, weight, energy D temperature, velocity, time [1]
2. A train travelling at 25 m/s decelerates uniformly to rest in 50 s. (a) Calculate its acceleration. [2] (b) Calculate the distance it travels while stopping. [2]
3. A student times 20 complete oscillations of a pendulum as 32.4 s. (a) Calculate the period. [1] (b) Explain why she times 20 oscillations instead of one. [2]
4. A stone is dropped from rest and falls for 2.5 s. Air resistance is negligible. Calculate (a) its speed after 2.5 s, [2] (b) the distance fallen. [2]
5. A car travels at 72 km/h. Calculate the time it takes to travel 1.5 km. [3]
6. An aircraft flies north at 250 m/s through air. A wind blows the air east at 60 m/s. Calculate the magnitude and direction of the aircraft's resultant velocity. [4]
7. Sketch a speed–time graph for a ball bearing released at the surface of a tall tube of oil, and state what the gradient shows at the start and at the end. [3]

1. **B**
2. (a) a = (0 − 25) ÷ 50 = **−0.50 m/s²** (deceleration 0.50 m/s²). (b) area = ½ × 25 × 50 = **625 m** (accept 630 m).
3. (a) 32.4 ÷ 20 = **1.62 s**. (b) Reaction time error is the same for one or 20 oscillations, so dividing by 20 makes it a much smaller fraction of the measurement; more accurate period.
