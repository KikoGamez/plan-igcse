# Moments, centre of gravity and pressure

> **Important for 0654:** **Momentum, impulse and pressure in liquids (Δp = ρgΔh) are NOT in the Co-ordinated Sciences 0654 syllabus** — they are only in Physics 0625, so they will not be examined. 0654 also limits the principle of moments to **one force on each side of the pivot**. Use this hour for moments, centre of gravity, stability and p = F ÷ A, which are examined.

> **In the exam:** In 0654 (Extended) Physics questions appear alongside Biology and Chemistry. Paper 2 (Multiple Choice, 45 min, 40 questions across all three sciences): often 1–2 items here (balancing a beam, stability, pressure and area). Paper 4 (Theory, 2 h, 120 marks): moments or pressure typically appear as a 3–6 mark part of a Physics question (moment = F × d, balancing calculation, equilibrium conditions, p = F ÷ A with unit conversion). Paper 6 (Alternative to Practical, 1 h 30 min, 60 marks): balancing a metre rule on a pivot and finding the centre of gravity of a lamina with a plumb line are classic contexts.

## What you need to know

Syllabus sections P1.5.2, P1.5.3 and P1.7 (Core, then *Supplement* for Extended).

- Describe the moment of a force as a measure of its turning effect and give everyday examples.
- Define the moment of a force as moment = force × perpendicular distance from the pivot; recall and use this equation.
- When there is no resultant force and no resultant moment, an object is in equilibrium.
- *Supplement:* apply the principle of moments to situations with one force each side of the pivot, including balancing of a beam.
- Understand what is meant by centre of gravity and know its position for regularly shaped objects (rectangular blocks, spheres, cylinders).
- Describe an experiment to determine the position of the centre of gravity of an irregularly shaped plane lamina.
- Describe, qualitatively, the effect of the position of the centre of gravity on the stability of simple objects.
- Describe how pressure varies with force and area in everyday examples; define pressure as force per unit area, p = F ÷ A.

## Notes

### Equations

| Equation | Symbols and units |
|---|---|
| moment = F × d | F force (N), d perpendicular distance from pivot (m or cm); moment in N m (or N cm) |
| Principle of moments *(Supplement)*: clockwise moment = anticlockwise moment (about the same pivot) | |
| p = F ÷ A | p pressure (Pa = N/m², or N/cm²), F force (N), A area (m² or cm²) |

1 m² = 10 000 cm², so 1 N/cm² = 10 000 Pa. 1 mm² = 1 × 10⁻⁶ m².

### Moments

- **Perpendicular distance:** measured from the pivot at right angles to the line of action of the force. Longer spanner → bigger moment for the same force; door handles are far from the hinges.
- **Equilibrium:** (1) no resultant force and (2) no resultant moment.
- **Balancing a beam (Supplement):** with one force on each side, F₁ × d₁ = F₂ × d₂.
- A uniform beam's weight acts at its centre. If the pivot is not at the centre, the beam's own weight can be the force on one side.
- The pivot pushes up with a force equal to the total downward force (so the resultant force is zero). Taking moments about the pivot removes this unknown force.

### Centre of gravity and stability

- **Centre of gravity:** the point through which the weight of an object acts. For a uniform rectangular block, sphere or cylinder it is at the geometric centre.
- **Experiment (irregular lamina):** make three small holes near the edge; hang the lamina from a pin through one hole so it swings freely; hang a plumb line from the same pin; when still, mark the line on the lamina; repeat for the other holes; the centre of gravity is where the lines cross. (The third line checks the first two.)
- **Stability:** an object topples when the vertical line through its centre of gravity falls outside its base. More stable: **low centre of gravity and wide base** (racing cars, Bunsen burner base, wide-based glasses).

### Pressure

- Large area → small pressure: snowshoes, wide tractor tyres, building foundations, rucksack straps.
- Small area → large pressure: knives, nails, drawing pins, stiletto heels.
- For a block resting on a table, the force is its weight; the face with the smallest area gives the largest pressure.

### Paper 6 skills for this topic

- **Balancing a metre rule:** find the balance point of the rule alone first (it may not be exactly 50.0 cm); read distances from the pivot to the centre of each mass/load, not its edge; move a load until the rule is horizontal and balanced; repeat for several positions; plot e.g. d against load, or load against 1/d, and comment on proportionality.
- Sources of error: hard to judge exact balance; load positions difficult to read; mass values may not be exact. Improvements: a sharper pivot, read the scale at eye level, repeat and average.
- **Centre of gravity:** use a plumb line, check the lamina swings freely, use three suspension points.
- **Pressure:** measure the area of contact with a ruler in cm, convert to m² if Pa is asked for; weight from a balance reading (W = mg).

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Moment of a force | Force × perpendicular distance from the pivot (a measure of its turning effect) |
| Principle of moments *(S)* | For an object in equilibrium, the clockwise moment about a pivot = the anticlockwise moment about the same pivot |
| Equilibrium | No resultant force and no resultant moment |
| Centre of gravity | The point through which the weight of an object acts |
| Pressure | Force per unit area |

## Worked examples

**1.** A uniform metre rule of weight 1.2 N is pivoted at its 30.0 cm mark. Calculate the load that must hang at the 5.0 cm mark to balance it. [3]

- Weight of rule acts at 50.0 cm, 20.0 cm from the pivot: clockwise moment = 1.2 × 20.0 = 24 N cm (C1)
- Load is 25.0 cm from the pivot: F × 25.0 = 24 (C1)
- F = **0.96 N** (A1)

**2.** A child of weight 250 N sits 1.6 m from the pivot of a see-saw. Calculate where a child of weight 320 N must sit to balance it. [3]

- moment of first child = 250 × 1.6 = 400 N m (C1)
- 320 × d = 400 (C1)
- d = **1.25 m** from the pivot, on the other side (A1)

**3.** A box of weight 600 N measures 0.50 m × 0.40 m × 0.20 m. Calculate the pressure on the floor when it rests on (a) its 0.50 m × 0.40 m face, [2] (b) its 0.40 m × 0.20 m face. [2]

- (a) A = 0.50 × 0.40 = 0.20 m² (C1); p = 600 ÷ 0.20 = **3000 Pa** (A1)
- (b) A = 0.40 × 0.20 = 0.080 m² (C1); p = 600 ÷ 0.080 = **7500 Pa** (A1)

**4.** Explain why a tall, narrow vase falls over more easily than a short, wide one. [3]

- The tall vase has a higher centre of gravity / the narrow vase has a smaller base. (B1)
- When it is tilted, the vertical line through its centre of gravity soon falls outside its base. (B1)
- The weight then has a moment that turns it further over, so it topples. (B1)

## Common mistakes

- Using the distance along a slanting beam or from the end of the rule instead of the perpendicular distance from the pivot.
- Forgetting the weight of the beam itself when it is not pivoted at its centre.
- Giving moments without a unit, or in N instead of N m / N cm.
- Not converting cm² to m² (divide by 10 000) before calculating pressure in Pa.
- Describing stability without mentioning the vertical line through the centre of gravity relative to the base.
- Writing "equilibrium means the forces are equal" — you need **no resultant force and no resultant moment**.

## Practice questions

1. A child of weight 300 N sits 2.0 m from the pivot of a see-saw. Where must a child of weight 400 N sit to balance it? [2]
2. An elephant of weight 50 000 N stands on four feet, each of area 0.10 m². Calculate the pressure on the ground. [2]
3. A mechanic pushes with 40 N at right angles to the end of a 0.25 m spanner. (a) Calculate the moment. [2] (b) Calculate the force needed to give the same moment with a 0.20 m spanner. [2]
4. A drawing pin is pushed with a force of 12 N. The point has an area of 0.10 mm² and the head an area of 1.0 cm². Calculate the pressure (a) under the point, (b) on the thumb. [4]
5. Describe an experiment to find the centre of gravity of a thin irregular card. [4]
6. Explain why a bus with passengers on the top deck is less stable than when they sit downstairs. [2]
7. A uniform metre rule is balanced at its 50.0 cm mark. A 2.0 N load hangs at the 20.0 cm mark. Calculate where a 3.0 N load must hang to balance the rule. [3]
8. State the two conditions for an object to be in equilibrium. [2]

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. 300 × 2.0 = 400 × d; d = **1.5 m** on the other side.
2. A = 4 × 0.10 = 0.40 m²; p = 50 000 ÷ 0.40 = **125 000 Pa** (1.25 × 10⁵ Pa).
3. (a) 40 × 0.25 = **10 N m**. (b) F = 10 ÷ 0.20 = **50 N**.
4. (a) 0.10 mm² = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ m²; p = 12 ÷ 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ = **1.2 × 10⁸ Pa**. (b) 1.0 cm² = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ m²; p = 12 ÷ 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ = **1.2 × 10⁵ Pa**.
5. Hang card from a pin through a hole near the edge so it swings freely; hang plumb line from the pin; mark the vertical line; repeat with two other holes; centre of gravity where lines cross.
6. The centre of gravity is higher; the bus tilts less before the vertical line through the centre of gravity passes outside the base (wheels), so it topples more easily.
7. Moment of 2.0 N load = 2.0 × 30.0 = 60 N cm; 3.0 × d = 60, d = 20.0 cm on the other side → **70.0 cm mark**.
8. No resultant force; no resultant moment.
