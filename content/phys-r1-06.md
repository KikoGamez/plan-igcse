# Waves and sound

> **In the exam:** In Co-ordinated Sciences 0654 (Extended) Physics questions appear alongside Biology and Chemistry. Paper 2 (Multiple Choice, 45 min, 40 questions across all three sciences): typically 1–3 items here (transverse vs longitudinal, v = fλ, diffraction diagrams, pitch and loudness). Paper 4 (Theory, 2 h, 120 marks): a common Physics part of 3–7 marks (labelling wavelength and amplitude, v = fλ, diffraction through a gap, echo calculations, describing a speed-of-sound experiment). Paper 6 (Alternative to Practical, 1 h 30 min, 60 marks): measuring the speed of sound by timing over a measured distance is a possible planning context.

## What you need to know

Syllabus sections P3.1 and P3.4 (Core, then *Supplement* for Extended).

- Waves transfer energy without transferring matter; wave motion as shown by vibrations (oscillations) in ropes and springs and by experiments using water waves.
- Describe the features of a wave: wavelength, frequency, crest (peak), trough, amplitude and wave speed; recall and use v = fλ.
- Describe how waves can undergo reflection at a plane surface and refraction due to a change of speed.
- *Supplement:* transverse wave — direction of vibration at right angles to the direction of propagation (electromagnetic radiation, water waves, seismic S-waves); longitudinal wave — vibration parallel to the direction of propagation (sound waves, seismic P-waves).
- *Supplement:* describe how waves undergo diffraction through a narrow gap, and how wavelength and gap size affect diffraction through a gap.
- Sound is produced by vibrating sources; the audible range for humans is about 20 Hz to 20 kHz; a medium is needed to transmit sound.
- Determine the speed of sound in air using a method involving a measurement of distance and time.
- Describe how changes in amplitude and frequency affect the loudness and pitch of sound; an echo is the reflection of a sound wave; ultrasound is sound with a frequency higher than 20 kHz.
- *Supplement:* sound waves in air are longitudinal, a series of compressions (higher pressure, particles closer together) and rarefactions (lower pressure, particles spread out); in general sound travels faster in solids than in liquids, and faster in liquids than in gases.

**Not in 0654** (they are in Physics 0625): diffraction at an edge, ripple-tank wavefront questions as a named objective, and the uses of ultrasound (medical scanning, sonar, crack detection). Echo calculations are still fair game because echoes and v = s ÷ t are both in the syllabus.

## Notes

### Equations

| Equation | Symbols and units |
|---|---|
| v = fλ | v wave speed (m/s), f frequency (Hz), λ wavelength (m) |
| v = s ÷ t (used for echoes: distance travelled = 2 × distance to reflector) | s distance (m), t time (s) |
| Period T = 1 ÷ f (useful; time for one complete wave) | T (s), f (Hz) |

1 kHz = 10³ Hz, 1 MHz = 10⁶ Hz; 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m (Extended candidates should know the prefixes G, μ and n).

### Describing waves

- **Wavelength (λ):** distance between a point on one wave and the same point on the next (e.g. crest to crest).
- **Amplitude:** maximum displacement from the rest (undisturbed) position — measured from the centre line to a crest, not crest to trough.
- **Frequency (f):** number of waves passing a point per second (Hz).
- **Crest (peak) / trough:** highest / lowest point of a transverse wave.
- Diagrams of water waves from above often show lines along each crest ("wavefronts"); the gap between neighbouring lines is one wavelength.
- **Wave speed:** distance travelled by a wave (crest) per unit time.

On a displacement–distance graph you read λ; on a displacement–time graph you read the period T (then f = 1/T).

### Transverse and longitudinal (Supplement)

| | Transverse | Longitudinal |
|---|---|---|
| Vibration | perpendicular to direction of travel | parallel to direction of travel |
| Examples | all EM waves (light, radio...), water waves, S-waves | sound, P-waves (and ultrasound) |
| Features | crests and troughs | compressions (particles close together) and rarefactions (particles spread out) |

A slinky spring can show both: shake side to side (transverse) or push and pull along its length (longitudinal).

### Reflection, refraction, diffraction (water waves)

- Water waves in a ripple tank (a shallow tray with a vibrating bar and a lamp above) show these effects clearly.
- **Reflection** at a straight barrier: angle of incidence = angle of reflection; wavelength, frequency and speed unchanged.
- **Refraction:** a glass plate makes the water shallower. In shallow water waves **slow down**, so **wavelength decreases**; frequency stays the same. If the waves meet the boundary at an angle, they change direction (bend towards the normal going into the slower region).
- **Diffraction (Supplement):** waves spread out as they pass through a narrow gap; wavelength, frequency and speed unchanged.
  - Most diffraction when the gap size is about the same as the wavelength. A gap much wider than λ → little spreading (only at the edges). For the same gap, longer wavelength → more diffraction.
  - Example: you can hear round a doorway but not see round it — sound wavelengths (≈ 1 m) are similar to the door width, light wavelengths (≈ 5 × 10⁻⁷ m) are much smaller.
- **Frequency never changes** when a wave reflects, refracts or diffracts (it is set by the source).

### Sound

- Produced by vibrating objects (strings, loudspeaker cone, vocal cords). *Supplement:* the vibrations make layers of air particles move back and forth → compressions (higher pressure, particles closer together) and rarefactions (lower pressure, particles spread out) travel outwards.
- Needs a medium: the bell-in-a-vacuum jar demonstration — as air is pumped out, the bell is seen ringing but the sound fades.
- Speed in air is roughly 330–350 m/s (questions normally give the value or ask you to measure it). *Supplement:* generally solids > liquids > gases (water ≈ 1500 m/s, steel ≈ 5000 m/s).
- **Loudness** depends on amplitude (larger amplitude → louder). **Pitch** depends on frequency (higher frequency → higher pitch). On an oscilloscope trace: taller wave = louder; more waves on the screen = higher pitch.
- Human hearing: 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz. **Ultrasound:** frequency above 20 kHz.
- **Echo:** reflection of sound from a hard surface. Sound travels there and back.

### Measuring the speed of sound in air

- **Method 1 (two observers):** observer A fires a starting pistol (or bangs cymbals) and B, at least 100 m away (measured with a trundle wheel/tape), starts a stop-watch on seeing the flash/smoke and stops on hearing the bang. Speed = distance ÷ time. Repeat and average; swap positions to cancel wind effect. Light arrives almost instantly.
- **Method 2 (echo):** stand a measured distance d (e.g. 50 m) from a large wall, clap and time the echo (or clap in rhythm with the echoes and time 20 claps); speed = 2d ÷ t.
- Improvement: large distance makes reaction time a smaller fraction of the time measured.

### Paper 6 skills for this topic

- Plan a speed-of-sound experiment: state the apparatus (stop-watch, measuring tape / trundle wheel), distances (≥ 100 m), repeats, how to calculate, sources of error (reaction time, wind, measuring distance).
- Read oscilloscope-style traces or wave diagrams using a ruler; measure several wavelengths and divide.
- Wave diagrams: measure across several wavelengths with a ruler and divide to get λ.

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Wavelength | Distance between two consecutive identical points on a wave (e.g. two crests) |
| Frequency | Number of complete waves passing a point per unit time |
| Amplitude | Maximum displacement of a particle from its rest/undisturbed position |
| Transverse wave *(S)* | Wave in which the vibration is at right angles to the direction of propagation |
| Longitudinal wave *(S)* | Wave in which the vibration is parallel to the direction of propagation |
| Diffraction *(S)* | Spreading of waves as they pass through a narrow gap |
| Refraction | Change of direction of a wave due to a change of speed when it enters a different medium |
| Echo | Reflection of sound waves |
| Ultrasound | Sound with a frequency higher than 20 kHz |

## Worked examples

**1.** Water waves of frequency 4.0 Hz have a wavelength of 0.15 m. Calculate their speed. [2]

- v = fλ = 4.0 × 0.15 (C1) = **0.60 m/s** (A1)

**2.** A ship sends a sound pulse straight down to the sea bed. The echo returns after 0.80 s. Speed of sound in sea water = 1500 m/s. Calculate the depth. [3]

- distance travelled = 1500 × 0.80 = 1200 m (C1)
- this is there and back, so depth = 1200 ÷ 2 (C1) = **600 m** (A1)

**3.** Water waves pass through a gap in a barrier. Describe and explain what happens when the gap is made narrower until it is similar to the wavelength. [3]

- The waves spread out more / diffraction increases. (B1)
- The waves beyond the gap become curved/circular (spread into the region behind the barrier). (B1)
- Wavelength (and speed and frequency) stay the same. (B1)

**4.** Two students stand 400 m apart. One bangs two blocks together; the other starts a timer on seeing the blocks hit and stops it on hearing the sound. Mean time = 1.20 s. (a) Calculate the speed of sound. [2] (b) Suggest one improvement. [1]

- (a) v = 400 ÷ 1.20 (C1) = **333 m/s** (A1)
- (b) Repeat several times and average / use a larger distance / repeat with the students' positions swapped (B1).

## Common mistakes

- Measuring amplitude from crest to trough (that is 2 × amplitude).
- Saying frequency changes when waves enter shallow water — speed and wavelength change, frequency doesn't.
- Forgetting to halve the distance in echo questions.
- Saying sound travels faster in gases, or that sound can travel through a vacuum.
- Drawing diffracted waves with a changed wavelength — keep the spacing the same.
- Linking loudness with frequency or pitch with amplitude.
- Giving "vibrations" only when asked for a difference between transverse and longitudinal — always compare direction of vibration **relative to direction of travel (energy transfer)**.

## Practice questions

1. Which of these are longitudinal waves? A light and sound B sound and P-waves C water waves and S-waves D radio waves and sound [1]
2. A medical scanner uses ultrasound of frequency 2.0 MHz. The speed in soft tissue is 1540 m/s. Calculate the wavelength. [2]
3. A student counts 10 waves passing a point in 2.5 s. Calculate the frequency and the period. [2]
4. Plane water waves move from deep water into shallow water, travelling at right angles to the boundary. State what happens to (a) speed, (b) wavelength, (c) frequency, (d) direction. [4]
5. A dog whistle has a frequency of 25 kHz. Explain why humans cannot hear it. [1]
6. A loudspeaker produces a note. Describe how the sound changes if (a) the amplitude increases, (b) the frequency increases. [2]
7. A student stands 85 m from a large wall and claps. She hears the echo 0.50 s later. Calculate the speed of sound in air. [3]

1. **B**
2. λ = v ÷ f = 1540 ÷ 2.0 × 10⁶ = **7.7 × 10⁻⁴ m** (0.77 mm).
3. f = 10 ÷ 2.5 = **4.0 Hz**; T = 1 ÷ 4.0 = **0.25 s**.
4. (a) decreases (b) decreases (c) unchanged (d) unchanged — they meet the boundary along the normal, so they do not bend.
5. 25 kHz is above the upper limit of human hearing (20 kHz) — it is ultrasound.
6. (a) louder (b) higher pitch.
7. Distance travelled = 2 × 85 = 170 m (there and back); v = 170 ÷ 0.50 = **340 m/s**.
