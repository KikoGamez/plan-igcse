# Electricity: quantities

> **In the exam:** In Co-ordinated Sciences 0654 (Extended) Physics questions appear alongside Biology and Chemistry. Paper 2 (Multiple Choice, 45 min, 40 questions across all three sciences): typically 1–3 items here (charging by friction, Q = It, R = V ÷ I, resistance of wires, meters). Paper 4 (Theory, 2 h, 120 marks): definitions of current, e.m.f. and p.d. (1–2 marks each) alongside R = V ÷ I and I = Q ÷ t calculations, often inside a circuits question. Paper 6 (Alternative to Practical, 1 h 30 min, 60 marks): measuring current and p.d., finding a resistance, and "the relationship between the p.d. across a wire and its length" are named practical contexts.

## What you need to know

Syllabus section P4.2 (Core, then *Supplement* for Extended).

- There are positive and negative charges; like charges repel, unlike charges attract.
- Describe electrostatic charging by friction and simple methods to find whether an object is charged; charging solids by friction involves only a transfer of negative charge (electrons).
- Distinguish between electrical conductors and insulators and give typical examples.
- *Supplement:* charge is measured in coulombs; an electric field is a region in which an electric charge experiences a force; its direction at a point is the direction of the force on a positive charge at that point.
- Electric current is related to the flow of charge; in metals it is related to the flow of electrons; use ammeters (analogue and digital) with different ranges; know the difference between direct current (d.c.) and alternating current (a.c.).
- *Supplement:* define current as the charge passing a point per unit time, I = Q ÷ t; describe conduction in metals by the movement of delocalised (mobile) electrons; conventional current is from positive to negative, electron flow from negative to positive.
- The voltage of the source is the cause of current in the circuit; it is shared between components in a series circuit; use voltmeters (analogue and digital) with different ranges.
- *Supplement:* define e.m.f. as the electrical work done by a source in moving a unit charge around a complete circuit; define p.d. as the work done by a unit charge passing between two points in a circuit; both are measured in volts (V).
- Recall and use R = V ÷ I; describe an experiment to find resistance with a voltmeter and an ammeter.
- *Supplement:* sketch and explain the current–voltage graph of a resistor of constant resistance; resistance of a metallic conductor is directly proportional to its length and inversely proportional to its cross-sectional area.

**Not in 0654** (they are in Physics 0625): electric field patterns (point charge, sphere, plates), I–V graphs of a filament lamp and a diode, and the equations V = W ÷ Q / E = W ÷ Q. Skip them.

## Notes

### Equations

| Equation | Symbols and units |
|---|---|
| R = V ÷ I | R resistance (Ω), V p.d. (V), I current (A) |
| I = Q ÷ t *(Supplement)* | I current (A), Q charge (C), t time (s) |
| R ∝ L and R ∝ 1/A *(Supplement)* | L length (m), A cross-sectional area (m²); A = πr² for a round wire |

1 A = 1 C/s. Prefixes: mA = 10⁻³ A, kΩ = 10³ Ω, MΩ = 10⁶ Ω; Extended candidates also μ (10⁻⁶).

### Electric charge

- Rubbing a polythene rod with a cloth: electrons move **from the cloth to the rod** → rod negative, cloth positive. Acetate rod: electrons move from the rod to the cloth → rod positive. Only **electrons (negative charge)** move — positive charges do not move in solids.
- Detecting charge: hang a charged rod on a thread and bring another charged rod near — repulsion means like charges; attraction means unlike charges. (A charged object also attracts small pieces of uncharged paper, so only **repulsion** proves something is charged with a particular sign.)
- **Conductors** (metals, graphite) let charge flow; **insulators** (plastic, rubber, glass, dry wood) do not. Test: put the material between crocodile clips in a circuit with a cell and a lamp/ammeter; lamp lights (current) → conductor.

### Electric field (Supplement)

An electric field is a region in which an electric charge experiences a force. Its direction is the direction of the force on a **positive** charge: away from positive charges, towards negative charges.

### Current

- Current is the rate of flow of charge. In metals the charge carriers are delocalised (free) electrons.
- **Conventional current** flows from + to − around the circuit; electrons actually flow from − to +.
- **d.c.** flows in one direction only (cells, batteries); **a.c.** repeatedly reverses direction (mains, 50 Hz in Spain).
- Ammeter: connected in **series**; choose a range just above the expected current for the best precision.

### Voltage: e.m.f. and p.d.

- The voltage of the source makes the current flow. In a series circuit the source voltage is shared between the components.
- *Supplement:* **e.m.f.** belongs to a source (cell, battery, generator) — the electrical work done per unit charge moved around the whole circuit. **P.d.** is between two points (across a component) — the work done by each unit of charge passing between them. Both in volts.
- Voltmeter: connected in **parallel** across the component; choose a suitable range.

### Resistance

- R = V ÷ I. Experiment: resistor in series with an ammeter, a variable resistor and a supply; voltmeter in parallel across the resistor; vary the current with the variable resistor; record pairs of V and I; calculate R for each and average (or plot I against V).
- *Supplement — I–V graph of a resistor of constant resistance:* straight line through the origin (I ∝ V) because R is constant; the gradient of I against V is 1/R, so a steeper line means a smaller resistance.
- *Supplement — wires:* longer → greater resistance (R ∝ L); thicker → less resistance (R ∝ 1/A). Double the length → double R; double the diameter → area × 4 → R ÷ 4.

### Paper 6 skills for this topic

- Draw circuit diagrams with correct symbols: ammeter in series, voltmeter in parallel, no gaps.
- Read analogue meters to the nearest half division; check for zero error; note the units (mA vs A).
- **Resistance against length of wire:** crocodile clips on a resistance wire taped to a metre rule; record V and I for lengths such as 20.0, 40.0, … 100.0 cm; R = V ÷ I; plot R (or V) against L — a straight line through the origin shows proportionality. Switch off between readings so the wire does not heat up (temperature changes resistance); keep the current low.
- Give R to 2–3 significant figures with the unit Ω.
- Typical evaluation: contact resistance at the clips, wire heating, difficulty reading the exact length at a clip.

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Conductor | Material that allows charge (electrons) to flow through it |
| Insulator | Material that does not allow charge to flow through it |
| Direct current (d.c.) | Current that flows in one direction only |
| Alternating current (a.c.) | Current that repeatedly reverses direction |
| Resistance | p.d. across a component ÷ current through it (R = V ÷ I) |
| Electric field *(S)* | A region in which an electric charge experiences a force |
| Direction of electric field *(S)* | Direction of the force on a positive charge at that point |
| Electric current *(S)* | Charge passing a point per unit time |
| Electromotive force (e.m.f.) *(S)* | Electrical work done by a source in moving a unit charge around a complete circuit |
| Potential difference (p.d.) *(S)* | Work done by a unit charge passing between two points in a circuit |

## Worked examples

**1.** A current of 0.40 A flows in a lamp for 5.0 minutes. (a) Calculate the charge that passes. [2] (b) The p.d. across the lamp is 6.0 V. Calculate its resistance. [2]

- (a) Q = It = 0.40 × 300 (C1) = **120 C** (A1)
- (b) R = V ÷ I = 6.0 ÷ 0.40 (C1) = **15 Ω** (A1)

**2.** A wire has a resistance of 2.0 Ω. A second wire of the same metal is twice as long and has twice the diameter. Calculate its resistance. [3]

- doubling length → resistance × 2 (C1)
- doubling diameter → area × 4 → resistance ÷ 4 (C1)
- R = 2.0 × 2 ÷ 4 = **1.0 Ω** (A1)

**3.** Sketch the current–voltage graph for a resistor of constant resistance and explain its shape. [2]

- Straight line through the origin (for positive and negative values). (B1)
- Current is proportional to p.d. because the resistance stays constant. (B1)

**4.** A polythene rod is rubbed with a dry cloth and becomes negatively charged. Explain how it becomes charged and state the charge on the cloth. [3]

- Friction transfers electrons (B1) from the cloth to the rod (B1).
- The cloth is left positively charged (with an equal amount of charge). (B1)

## Common mistakes

- Saying positive charges (protons) move during charging by friction.
- Defining p.d. or e.m.f. as "the energy" — it is work done **per unit charge**.
- Using minutes instead of seconds in Q = It.
- Connecting the voltmeter in series or the ammeter in parallel in circuit diagrams.
- Forgetting that area depends on diameter squared.
- Mixing up the direction of conventional current and electron flow.

## Practice questions

1. A p.d. of 3.0 V across a resistor gives a current of 0.25 A. Calculate the resistance. [2]
2. A current of 2.5 A flows for 4.0 minutes. Calculate the charge that passes. [2]
3. Calculate the time for 36 C of charge to pass a point when the current is 150 mA. [2]
4. Define electric field and state how its direction is defined. [2]
5. State the difference between d.c. and a.c. and give a source of each. [2]
6. Describe an experiment to determine the resistance of a resistor. Include a circuit diagram. [4]
7. Wire P has length 50 cm and resistance 4.0 Ω. Calculate the resistance of 80 cm of the same wire. [2]

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. R = 3.0 ÷ 0.25 = **12 Ω**.
2. Q = It = 2.5 × 240 = **600 C**.
3. t = Q ÷ I = 36 ÷ 0.150 = **240 s**.
4. A region in which an electric charge experiences a force; direction = direction of the force on a positive charge.
5. d.c. flows in one direction only (e.g. a cell/battery); a.c. repeatedly reverses direction (e.g. mains supply / a.c. generator).
6. Resistor in series with ammeter, cell/supply (and variable resistor); voltmeter in parallel across the resistor; record V and I; repeat for several currents; R = V ÷ I for each, take the average (or use the graph).
7. R ∝ L: 4.0 × 80 ÷ 50 = **6.4 Ω**.
