# Electricity: quantities

> **In the exam:** Paper 2: 4–6 items (charging by friction, Q = It, R = V/I, I–V graphs, resistance of wires, field patterns). Paper 4: definitions of e.m.f., p.d. and current are frequently asked for (1–2 marks each) alongside calculations; electrostatics and electric fields appear regularly. Paper 6: determining resistance with an ammeter and voltmeter, I–V characteristics, and resistance against length of wire are very common contexts.

## What you need to know

- Positive and negative charges; like charges repel, unlike attract; charging by friction involves only a transfer of electrons; experiments to produce and detect charge.
- Conductors and insulators: experiment to distinguish; simple electron model; typical examples.
- *Extended:* charge is measured in coulombs; electric field = region in which an electric charge experiences a force; direction = direction of force on a positive charge; field patterns around a point charge, a charged conducting sphere, and between two oppositely charged parallel plates.
- Current is related to flow of charge; ammeters (analogue and digital); conduction in metals by free electrons; d.c. vs a.c.
- *Extended:* current = charge passing a point per unit time, I = Q ÷ t; conventional current flows positive to negative, electrons flow negative to positive.
- E.m.f.: electrical work done by a source in moving unit charge around a complete circuit; p.d.: work done by unit charge passing through a component; both in volts; voltmeters. *Extended:* E = W ÷ Q, V = W ÷ Q.
- R = V ÷ I; experiment with voltmeter and ammeter; resistance of a wire increases with length and decreases with cross-sectional area. *Extended:* R ∝ length, R ∝ 1/area; sketch and explain I–V graphs for a fixed resistor, a filament lamp and a diode.

## Notes

### Equations

| Equation | Symbols and units |
|---|---|
| I = Q ÷ t *(Extended)* | I current (A), Q charge (C), t time (s) |
| E = W ÷ Q *(Extended)* | E e.m.f. (V), W work done / energy transferred (J), Q charge (C) |
| V = W ÷ Q *(Extended)* | V potential difference (V) |
| R = V ÷ I | R resistance (Ω), V p.d. (V), I current (A) |
| R ∝ L and R ∝ 1/A *(Extended)* | L length (m), A cross-sectional area (m²); A = πr² for a round wire |

1 V = 1 J/C; 1 A = 1 C/s. Prefixes: mA = 10⁻³ A, μA = 10⁻⁶ A (Extended), kΩ = 10³ Ω, MΩ = 10⁶ Ω.

### Electric charge

- Rubbing a polythene rod with a cloth: electrons move **from the cloth to the rod** → rod negative, cloth positive. Acetate rod: electrons move from the rod to the cloth → rod positive. Only **electrons (negative charge)** move — positive charges never move in solids.
- Detection: a charged rod hung on a thread is repelled by a rod with the same charge, attracted by an opposite charge. (A charged object also attracts small uncharged objects like paper.)
- **Conductors** (metals, graphite) contain free electrons that can move through the material. **Insulators** (plastic, rubber, glass, dry wood) have no free electrons — electrons are held in atoms. Test: connect the material between crocodile clips in a circuit with a cell and lamp/ammeter; lamp lights (current) → conductor.

### Electric fields (Extended)

- An electric field is a region in which an electric charge experiences a force. Field lines point in the direction of the force on a **positive** charge: away from positive, towards negative.
- **Point charge / charged sphere:** radial lines, outward for positive, inward for negative (lines meet the sphere at right angles).
- **Parallel plates:** evenly spaced parallel straight lines from + plate to − plate (uniform field between them).

### Current

- Current is the rate of flow of charge. In metals the charge carriers are free electrons.
- **Conventional current** flows from + to − around the circuit; electrons actually flow from − to +.
- **d.c.** flows in one direction only (cells); **a.c.** repeatedly reverses direction (mains, 50 Hz in Europe).
- Ammeter: connected in **series**; ideally zero resistance; choose a range just above the expected current for best precision.

### E.m.f. and p.d.

- **E.m.f.** belongs to a source (cell, battery, generator): energy given to each coulomb of charge.
- **P.d.** belongs to a component: energy transferred by each coulomb passing through it.
- Voltmeter: connected in **parallel** across the component; ideally very high resistance.

### Resistance

- R = V ÷ I. Experiment: component in series with ammeter, variable resistor and supply; voltmeter in parallel across it; vary the current with the variable resistor; record V and I pairs; calculate R or plot I against V.
- Wire: longer → greater resistance; thicker → less resistance. *Extended:* double length → double R; double diameter → area ×4 → R ÷ 4.

### I–V graphs (Extended)

| Component | Shape of I against V | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed resistor (constant temperature) | straight line through origin | current proportional to p.d.; resistance constant |
| Filament lamp | curve through origin, gradient decreasing (flattens) | as current increases, filament gets hotter; resistance increases |
| Diode | almost zero current in reverse; current rises sharply above a small forward p.d. (about 0.6 V) | allows current in one direction only — very high resistance in reverse |

For I (y-axis) against V (x-axis), a steeper line means lower resistance. Calculate R at a point as V ÷ I at that point (not the gradient).

### Paper 6 skills for this topic

- Draw circuit diagrams with correct symbols: ammeter in series, voltmeter in parallel, no gaps.
- Read analogue meters to the nearest half division; check for zero error; note the units (mA vs A).
- **Resistance against length of wire:** crocodile clips on a resistance wire taped to a metre rule; record V and I for lengths such as 20.0, 40.0, ... 100.0 cm; R = V/I; plot R against L — straight line through the origin → R ∝ L. Switch off between readings so the wire does not heat up (temperature changes resistance); keep current low.
- Calculated R should have 2–3 significant figures (same as the least precise data); include unit Ω.
- Typical evaluation: contact resistance at crocodile clips, wire heating, difficulty reading exact length at clip.

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Electric field *(Ext)* | A region in which an electric charge experiences a force |
| Direction of electric field *(Ext)* | Direction of the force on a positive charge at that point |
| Electric current *(Ext)* | Charge passing a point per unit time |
| Electromotive force (e.m.f.) | Electrical work done by a source in moving a unit charge around a complete circuit |
| Potential difference (p.d.) | Work done by a unit charge passing through a component |
| Resistance | p.d. across a component ÷ current through it (R = V/I) |
| Conductor | Material in which charge (free electrons) can move easily |
| Insulator | Material with no free electrons, so charge cannot flow through it |
| Direct current (d.c.) | Current that flows in one direction only |
| Alternating current (a.c.) | Current that repeatedly reverses direction |

## Worked examples

**1.** A current of 0.40 A flows in a lamp for 5.0 minutes. (a) Calculate the charge that passes. [2] (b) The p.d. across the lamp is 6.0 V. Calculate the energy transferred. [2]

- (a) Q = It = 0.40 × 300 (C1) = **120 C** (A1)
- (b) W = QV = 120 × 6.0 (C1) = **720 J** (A1)

**2.** A wire has a resistance of 2.0 Ω. A second wire of the same metal is twice as long and has twice the diameter. Calculate its resistance. [3]

- doubling length → resistance × 2 (C1)
- doubling diameter → area × 4 → resistance ÷ 4 (C1)
- R = 2.0 × 2 ÷ 4 = **1.0 Ω** (A1)

**3.** Sketch the I–V graph for a filament lamp and explain its shape. [3]

- Curve through the origin, symmetrical for negative values, with gradient decreasing as V increases. (B1)
- As the current increases, the filament temperature increases. (B1)
- So its resistance increases (current rises less than proportionally). (B1)

**4.** A polythene rod is rubbed with a dry cloth and becomes negatively charged. Explain how it becomes charged and state the charge on the cloth. [3]

- Friction transfers electrons (B1) from the cloth to the rod (B1).
- The cloth is left positively charged (with an equal amount of charge). (B1)

## Common mistakes

- Saying positive charges (protons) move during charging by friction.
- Defining p.d. or e.m.f. as "the energy" — it is work done **per unit charge**.
- Using minutes instead of seconds in Q = It.
- Connecting the voltmeter in series or the ammeter in parallel in circuit diagrams.
- Drawing the lamp curve bending the wrong way (on I against V it flattens) or with a straight start not through the origin.
- Taking resistance as the gradient of a curved I–V graph — use V ÷ I at the point.
- Forgetting that area depends on diameter squared.

## Practice questions

1. A p.d. of 3.0 V across a resistor gives a current of 0.25 A. Calculate the resistance. [2]
2. A battery of e.m.f. 9.0 V moves 50 C of charge round a circuit. Calculate the energy it transfers. [2]
3. Calculate the time for 36 C of charge to pass a point when the current is 150 mA. [2]
4. Draw the electric field between two parallel plates, the top one positive. [2]
5. Explain why a diode is used to protect a circuit if a battery is connected the wrong way round. [2]
6. Describe an experiment to determine the resistance of a resistor. Include a circuit diagram. [4]
7. Wire P has length 50 cm and resistance 4.0 Ω. Calculate the resistance of 80 cm of the same wire. [2]

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. R = 3.0 ÷ 0.25 = **12 Ω**.
2. W = QE = 50 × 9.0 = **450 J**.
3. t = Q ÷ I = 36 ÷ 0.150 = **240 s**.
4. Parallel, evenly spaced straight lines from the top (+) plate to the bottom (−) plate, arrows pointing downwards.
5. A diode only allows current in one direction / has very high resistance in reverse, so no current flows if the battery is reversed.
6. Resistor in series with ammeter, cell/supply (and variable resistor); voltmeter in parallel across resistor; record V and I; repeat for several currents; R = V/I for each, take average (or gradient of V against I).
7. R ∝ L: 4.0 × 80 ÷ 50 = **6.4 Ω**.

</details>
