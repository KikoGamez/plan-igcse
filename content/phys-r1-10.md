# Magnetism and the motor effect

> **In the exam:** In Co-ordinated Sciences 0654 (Extended) Physics questions appear alongside Biology and Chemistry. Paper 2 (Multiple Choice, 45 min, 40 questions across all three sciences): typically 1–2 items here (forces between poles, soft iron vs steel, field around a wire or solenoid, direction of force on a wire, ways to increase a motor's turning effect). Paper 4 (Theory, 2 h, 120 marks): a common Physics part of 3–7 marks (field patterns of a wire and a solenoid, direction of the force with the left-hand rule, how a d.c. motor works and the job of the split-ring commutator). Paper 6: rarely tested directly.

- *Supplement:* describe the pattern and direction of the magnetic field due to currents in straight wires and in solenoids, and the effect of changing the size and direction of the current.
- *Supplement:* a force acts on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field, including the effect of reversing the current and reversing the field; recall and use the relative directions of force, field and current.
- *Supplement:* a current-carrying coil in a magnetic field may experience a turning effect, increased by more turns on the coil, a larger current, a stronger magnetic field; describe the operation of an electric motor, including the action of a split-ring commutator and brushes.

## Notes

### Equations

There are no calculation equations in this topic. You must know the **rules for directions**:

| Rule | What it gives |
|---|---|
| Field lines go from N to S outside a magnet | direction of field (force on a N pole) |
| Right-hand grip rule: thumb along the conventional current, fingers curl in the field direction | field around a straight wire; for a solenoid, fingers along the current in the turns → thumb points to the N pole |
| Fleming's left-hand rule: **F**irst finger = **F**ield (N→S), se**C**ond finger = **C**urrent (conventional, + to −), thu**M**b = **M**otion/force | direction of the force on a current-carrying conductor |

### Magnets and magnetic materials

- Like poles repel, unlike poles attract. A magnet attracts unmagnetised magnetic materials — so only **repulsion** proves that an object is a magnet.
- **Magnetic materials:** iron, steel, nickel, cobalt. **Non-magnetic:** copper, aluminium, plastic, wood, glass.
- **Soft iron:** easily magnetised and easily loses its magnetism → **temporary magnet** (electromagnet cores, transformer cores).
- **Steel:** harder to magnetise but keeps its magnetism → **permanent magnet**.
- **Permanent magnet vs electromagnet:** a permanent magnet is always magnetic and its strength is fixed; an electromagnet (a coil, usually with a soft-iron core) is magnetic only while a current flows, and its strength can be changed by changing the current.
- *Supplement — induced magnetism:* a magnetic material placed in a magnetic field becomes a magnet itself; the end nearest a N pole becomes a S pole, so it is attracted (e.g. a chain of paper clips hanging from a magnet).

### Magnetic fields (Supplement)

- A magnetic field is a region in which a magnetic pole experiences a force. The field direction is the direction of the force on a N pole: outside a magnet, lines go from N to S. Field lines never cross; they are closest where the field is strongest (at the poles).

### Magnetic effect of a current (Supplement)

- **Straight wire:** concentric circles around the wire; direction by the right-hand grip rule. The field is strongest close to the wire (circles drawn further apart further out). Larger current → stronger field; reversing the current reverses the field direction.
- **Solenoid (coil):** field inside is strong and uniform (parallel, evenly spaced lines); outside it looks like a bar magnet's field. Larger current → stronger field (also more turns, soft-iron core); reversing the current reverses the poles.

### Force on a current-carrying conductor (the motor effect, Supplement)

- A wire carrying a current across a magnetic field experiences a force.
- Demonstration: a loose wire (or a copper rod on two rails) between the poles of a U-shaped magnet, connected to a d.c. supply; it moves when current flows.
- Reverse the current **or** reverse the field → force reverses. Reverse both → force unchanged.
- No force if the current is parallel to the field. Larger current or stronger field → larger force.
- Use Fleming's left-hand rule: field, current and force are mutually at right angles.

### The d.c. motor (Supplement)

- A coil carrying a current sits between magnetic poles. The current in the two sides of the coil is in opposite directions, so the forces on them are in opposite directions → a turning effect → the coil rotates.
- Turning effect increased by: **more turns on the coil, larger current, stronger magnetic field**.
- **Split-ring commutator and brushes:**
  - Brushes (carbon) press against the split ring and connect the rotating coil to the supply.
  - Every half turn (when the coil is perpendicular to the field) the commutator **reverses the current in the coil**.
  - So the force on the side of the coil next to each pole is always in the same direction, and the coil keeps turning the same way.
  - The coil's motion carries it past the position where the forces have no turning effect.
- Reversing the current or the field reverses the direction of rotation.

### Paper 6 skills for this topic

- Describe a test for a magnet: bring each end of the bar near each pole of a known magnet; repulsion at some position shows it is a magnet; attraction everywhere shows only a magnetic material.
- Draw field diagrams with a sharp pencil: continuous lines, arrows, no crossing.

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Magnetic material | A material that is attracted by a magnet and can be magnetised (iron, steel, nickel, cobalt) |
| Temporary magnet | Magnet that loses its magnetism easily (soft iron) |
| Permanent magnet | Magnet that keeps its magnetism (steel) |
| Electromagnet | Coil of wire (usually with a soft-iron core) that is magnetic only when a current flows |
| Magnetic field *(S)* | A region in which a magnetic pole experiences a force |
| Direction of a magnetic field *(S)* | The direction of the force on the N pole of a magnet at that point |
| Induced magnetism *(S)* | Magnetism produced in a magnetic material when it is placed in a magnetic field |
| Split-ring commutator *(S)* | Device that reverses the current in a motor coil every half turn so it keeps rotating the same way |

## Worked examples

**1.** State two differences between soft iron and steel as magnetic materials and give one use of each. [4]

- Soft iron is easily magnetised; steel is harder to magnetise. (B1)
- Soft iron loses its magnetism easily (temporary); steel keeps it (permanent). (B1)
- Soft iron: core of an electromagnet / transformer. (B1)
- Steel: permanent magnet, e.g. compass needle. (B1)

**2.** Describe the magnetic field of a current-carrying solenoid and state the effect of reversing the current. [3]

- Inside: strong, uniform field — parallel, evenly spaced lines along the axis. (B1)
- Outside: like the field of a bar magnet, with a N pole at one end and a S pole at the other. (B1)
- Reversing the current reverses the field direction / swaps the poles. (B1)

**3.** Describe the function of the split-ring commutator in a d.c. motor. [3]

- It reverses the current in the coil (B1) every half turn / when the coil is perpendicular to the field (B1).
- So the forces on each side of the coil keep turning it in the same direction. (B1)

**4.** A straight wire lies between the poles of a magnet; the field is from left to right across the page. The current in the wire flows into the page. State the direction of the force on the wire. [2]

- Left-hand rule: first finger to the right (field), second finger into the page (current). (B1)
- Thumb points **down the page** — the force is towards the bottom of the page. (B1)

## Common mistakes

- Saying a magnet attracts all metals (copper and aluminium are not magnetic).
- Saying attraction proves an object is a magnet — only repulsion does.
- Field lines that cross, start in mid-air or have no arrows; arrows going S to N outside a magnet.
- Saying the commutator "changes the direction of the coil" or "makes it a.c." — it reverses the **current in the coil**.
- Answering "how can you make the motor turn faster?" with "more power" — give the three syllabus factors.
- Confusing the motor effect (current + field → force) with induction (movement + field → e.m.f.).

## Practice questions

1. Which material is used for the core of an electromagnet? A copper B steel C soft iron D aluminium [1]
2. Draw the magnetic field pattern around a straight vertical wire carrying current upwards out of the paper (viewed from above). [2]
3. State three ways of increasing the turning effect on a motor coil. [3]
4. State two ways of reversing the direction of rotation of a d.c. motor. [2]
5. A wire between the poles of a magnet moves upwards when a current flows. State what happens when (a) the current is reversed, (b) both the current and field are reversed. [2]
6. Describe a test to find out whether a steel bar is a permanent magnet or just unmagnetised steel. [2]
7. State two differences between a permanent magnet and an electromagnet. [2]

1. **C**
2. Concentric circles centred on the wire, spaced further apart further out; arrows anticlockwise (current out of the paper, right-hand grip).
3. More turns on the coil; larger current; stronger magnet/field.
4. Reverse the current (swap the supply connections); reverse the magnetic field (swap the poles). (Doing both together does not reverse it.)
5. (a) moves downwards (b) moves upwards (unchanged).
6. Bring each end of the bar near the N pole (and the S pole) of a known magnet; if one end is repelled it is a magnet; if both ends are attracted it is unmagnetised.
7. An electromagnet is magnetic only when a current flows (can be switched off); its strength can be changed by changing the current; a permanent magnet is always magnetic with a fixed strength.
