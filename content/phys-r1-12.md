# Nuclear and space physics

> **In the exam:** In Co-ordinated Sciences 0654 (Extended) Physics questions appear alongside Biology and Chemistry. Space physics is new in the 2025–2027 0654 syllabus, so there are few past-paper questions on it — use the practice here. Paper 2 (Multiple Choice, 45 min, 40 questions across all three sciences): typically 1–3 items (nucleus, isotopes, α/β/γ properties, decay equations, half-life, Solar System, life cycle of stars). Paper 4 (Theory, 2 h, 120 marks): a nuclear part of 4–8 marks (nuclide notation, decay equations, half-life from a table or graph, uses and safety) and possibly a space part (light travel time, orbital speed, star life cycle, Big Bang). Paper 6: rarely tested directly; decay-curve graph skills still apply.

## What you need to know

Syllabus sections P5 and P6 (Core, then *Supplement* for Extended).

**Nuclear physics**
- The nucleus is made of protons and neutrons; relative charges of proton, neutron and electron are +1, 0 and −1.
- Define proton number (atomic number) Z and nucleon number (mass number) A; calculate the number of neutrons; use nuclide notation; an element may have more than one isotope, and some isotopes are radioactive.
- *Supplement:* the proton number gives the relative charge on a nucleus; describe nuclear fission and nuclear fusion as the splitting and joining of nuclei.
- Ionising nuclear radiation and background radiation; sources of background radiation: radon gas (in the air), rocks and buildings, food and drink, cosmic rays; radiation measured with a detector connected to a counter; count rate in counts/s or counts/minute.
- Identify α, β (β⁻ only) and γ by their nature, relative ionising effects and relative penetrating abilities.
- Radioactive decay is a change in an unstable nucleus that can result in the emission of α-particles or β-particles and/or γ-radiation; it is spontaneous and random; in α- or β-decay the nucleus changes to a different element; in β-emission a neutron → proton + electron.
- *Supplement:* use decay equations in nuclide notation for α, β and γ emission.
- Define half-life as the time taken for half the nuclei of that isotope in any sample to decay; use it in simple calculations with tables or decay curves (**calculations will not include background radiation**).
- Applications: smoke alarms; irradiating food to kill bacteria; sterilising equipment with gamma rays; measuring and controlling thickness (choice of radiation linked to penetration and absorption); diagnosis and treatment of cancer with gamma rays.
- Effects of ionising radiation on living things (cell death, mutations, cancer); moving, using and storing radioactive materials safely in terms of time, distance and shielding.

**Space physics**
- The Solar System: one star (the Sun); the eight named planets in order; minor planets including dwarf planets such as Pluto and asteroids in the asteroid belt; moons that orbit the planets.
- The Sun is the closest star; astronomical distances can be measured in light-years (the distance light travels in space in one year); calculate the time light takes to travel between objects in the Solar System.
- The Sun contains most of the mass of the Solar System, which explains why the planets orbit it; the force keeping an object in orbit round the Sun is the Sun's gravitational attraction.
- The Sun is a medium-sized star, mostly hydrogen and helium, radiating most of its energy in the infrared, visible and ultraviolet regions.
- *Supplement:* orbital speed v = 2πr ÷ T; the strength of the Sun's gravitational field and the planets' orbital speeds decrease with distance from the Sun; stars are powered by nuclear reactions — in stable stars, fusion of hydrogen into helium.
- Life cycle of stars: stable stars form as protostars from interstellar clouds of gas and dust due to gravitational attraction; next stages depend on mass (small mass, about the Sun's: red giant → white dwarf + planetary nebula; large mass: red supergiant → supernova → neutron star; very large mass: red supergiant → supernova → black hole). *Supplement:* the nebula from a supernova may form new stars with orbiting planets.
- Galaxies are made of many billions of stars; the Sun is in the Milky Way; other stars in the Milky Way are much further from Earth than the Sun; the Milky Way is one of many billions of galaxies and is about 100 000 light-years across.
- *Supplement:* the Big Bang Theory is supported by many astronomical observations and states that the Universe expanded from a single point of high density and temperature, is still expanding, and is about 13.8 billion years old.

**Not in 0654** (they are in Physics 0625): α-particle scattering, deflection of radiation in fields, background-corrected half-life calculations, fission/fusion nuclide equations, Earth's rotation, seasons and Moon phases, the accretion model, elliptical orbits, redshift, CMBR and the Hubble constant. Skip them.

## Notes

### Equations

| Equation | Symbols and units |
|---|---|
| number of neutrons = A − Z | A nucleon number, Z proton number |
| Half-life: after n half-lives, amount remaining = original × (½)ⁿ | count rate (counts/s or counts/min), half-life (s, min, h, days, years) |
| speed = distance ÷ time (light travel time t = d ÷ c) | c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s (Supplement value) |
| v = 2πr ÷ T *(Supplement)* | v orbital speed (m/s), r orbital radius (m), T orbital period (s) |
| 1 light-year ≈ 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s × (365 × 24 × 3600 s) ≈ 9.5 × 10¹⁵ m | work it out if needed |

### The atom and nucleus

| Particle | Relative charge | Location |
|---|---|---|
| Proton | +1 | nucleus |
| Neutron | 0 | nucleus |
| Electron | −1 | outside the nucleus |

- Nuclide notation: nucleon number A top left and proton number Z bottom left of the symbol, e.g. ¹⁴₆C has 6 protons and 8 neutrons. *Supplement:* the relative charge of this nucleus is +6 (= Z).
- **Isotopes:** atoms of the same element (same number of protons) with different numbers of neutrons. Some isotopes are radioactive (e.g. carbon-14), others are stable (carbon-12).
- *Supplement:* **fission** = splitting of a large nucleus (e.g. uranium in a nuclear reactor) into smaller nuclei, releasing energy; **fusion** = joining of small nuclei (e.g. hydrogen into helium in stars), releasing energy.

### Radiation

| | Alpha (α) | Beta (β⁻) | Gamma (γ) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | helium nucleus: 2 protons + 2 neutrons, ⁴₂He | fast-moving electron from the nucleus, ⁰₋₁e | electromagnetic radiation |
| Charge | +2 | −1 | 0 |
| Ionising effect | strongest | medium | weakest |
| Penetration (stopped by) | a few cm of air / a sheet of paper | a few mm of aluminium | reduced (absorbed) by thick lead / concrete |

- **Background radiation sources:** radon gas in the air, rocks and buildings, food and drink, cosmic rays.
- A detector (e.g. Geiger–Müller tube) connected to a counter measures count rate in counts/s or counts/min.

### Radioactive decay

- Decay is **spontaneous** (not affected by temperature or chemical reactions) and **random** (you cannot predict which nucleus will decay next or when).
- **α-decay:** A decreases by 4, Z decreases by 2 → a different element. ²²⁶₈₈Ra → ²²²₈₆Rn + ⁴₂He
- **β-decay:** a neutron changes into a proton and an electron; the electron is emitted. A unchanged, Z increases by 1. ⁹⁰₃₈Sr → ⁹⁰₃₉Y + ⁰₋₁e
- **γ-emission:** no change in A or Z. ⁶⁰₂₇Co → ⁶⁰₂₇Co + γ (often after α or β decay).
- Check equations: top numbers balance, bottom numbers balance.

### Half-life

- **Half-life:** the time taken for half the nuclei of that isotope in any sample to decay (so the count rate also halves).
- From a decay curve: read the time for the count rate to fall from any value to half of it; repeat from another starting point and average.
- In 0654 calculations, background radiation is not included — use the numbers as given.

### Uses

| Use | Radiation | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Smoke alarm | α | α ionises the air between two plates → small current; smoke absorbs α → current drops → alarm sounds; α is absorbed within the alarm |
| Thickness control (paper, foil) | β | partly absorbed by the sheet, so a change in thickness changes the count rate (α would all be stopped; γ would pass through almost unchanged) |
| Sterilising equipment / irradiating food | γ | penetrates packaging and kills bacteria |
| Diagnosis of cancer | γ | passes out of the body to a detector |
| Treatment of cancer | γ | beams aimed at the tumour kill cancer cells |

### Safety

- Effects on living things: cell death, mutations, cancer.
- **Time:** keep exposure time short. **Distance:** handle with tongs, keep the source pointing away from people. **Shielding:** store in lead-lined containers; use lead/concrete screens.

### The Solar System

- One star (the Sun); eight planets in order **Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune**; minor planets (dwarf planets such as Pluto; asteroids in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter); moons orbiting planets.
- The Sun has most of the Solar System's mass; its gravitational attraction keeps the planets in orbit.
- *Supplement:* further from the Sun the Sun's gravitational field is weaker, so planets move more slowly (and take longer to orbit).

### Stars and the Universe

- **The Sun:** the closest star; medium-sized; mostly hydrogen and helium; radiates mostly infrared, visible and ultraviolet. *Supplement:* powered by fusion of hydrogen into helium.
- **Light-year:** distance travelled by light in space in one year (about 9.5 × 10¹⁵ m) — a unit of distance, not time.
- **Galaxies:** each is many billions of stars. The Sun is in the Milky Way (about 100 000 light-years across); other stars of the Milky Way are much further away than the Sun; the Milky Way is one of many billions of galaxies.
- **Life cycle of a star:**
  1. An interstellar cloud of gas and dust is pulled together by gravity → **protostar** → **stable star** (fusion of hydrogen).
  2. Small mass (about the Sun's): **red giant → white dwarf + planetary nebula**.
  3. Large mass: **red supergiant → supernova → neutron star**.
  4. Very large mass: **red supergiant → supernova → black hole**.
  5. *Supplement:* the nebula from a supernova may form new stars with orbiting planets.
- *Supplement — Big Bang Theory* (supported by many astronomical observations): the Universe expanded from a single point of very high density and temperature; it is still expanding; it is about 13.8 billion years old.

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Isotopes | Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons |
| Proton number (Z) | Number of protons in the nucleus |
| Nucleon number (A) | Number of protons + neutrons in the nucleus |
| Background radiation | Ionising radiation that is always present in the environment |
| Radioactive decay | Spontaneous, random change in an unstable nucleus, emitting α, β and/or γ radiation |
| Half-life | Time taken for half the nuclei of that isotope in any sample to decay |
| Light-year | Distance travelled by light in (the vacuum of) space in one year |
| Nuclear fission *(S)* | Splitting of a nucleus, releasing energy |
| Nuclear fusion *(S)* | Joining of nuclei, releasing energy |

## Worked examples

**1.** A detector records a count rate of 800 counts/min from a source. After 18 hours the count rate is 100 counts/min. Calculate the half-life. [3]

- 800 → 400 → 200 → 100 (C1)
- that is 3 half-lives in 18 hours (C1)
- half-life = 18 ÷ 3 = **6.0 hours** (A1)

**2.** Complete the equations: (a) radium-226 (Z = 88) emits an α-particle and becomes radon; (b) carbon-14 (Z = 6) emits a β-particle and becomes nitrogen. [4]

- (a) ²²⁶₈₈Ra → ²²²₈₆Rn + ⁴₂He (B1 for 222/86, B1 for ⁴₂He)
- (b) ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁e (B1 for 14/7, B1 for ⁰₋₁e)

**3.** The Earth orbits the Sun at an average radius of 1.5 × 10¹¹ m once every 365 days. Calculate its average orbital speed. [3]

- T = 365 × 24 × 3600 = 3.15 × 10⁷ s (C1)
- v = 2πr ÷ T = 2π × 1.5 × 10¹¹ ÷ 3.15 × 10⁷ (C1) = **3.0 × 10⁴ m/s** (A1)

**4.** Light from a nearby star takes 4.2 years to reach the Earth. (a) State its distance in light-years. [1] (b) Calculate this distance in metres (speed of light = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s). [2]

- (a) **4.2 light-years** (B1)
- (b) d = 3.0 × 10⁸ × 4.2 × 365 × 24 × 3600 (C1) = **4.0 × 10¹⁶ m** (A1)

## Common mistakes

- Defining half-life as "half the time for the sample to decay" or "time for the mass of the sample to halve" — say **half the nuclei of that isotope**.
- β-decay equations with Z going down, or writing the β-particle as ⁰₁e.
- Saying γ is "stopped" by paper or aluminium — it is only reduced significantly by thick lead or concrete.
- Ordering planets wrongly or including Pluto as a planet (it is a dwarf planet).
- Saying a Sun-sized star becomes a supernova — only red **supergiants** (large mass stars) do.
- Treating a light-year as a time.
- Using days instead of seconds for T in v = 2πr ÷ T.

## Practice questions

1. State the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in a neutral atom of ²³⁸₉₂U. [2]
2. Iodine-131 has a half-life of 8 days. A sample contains 80 mg. Calculate the mass of iodine-131 left after 24 days. [2]
3. The count rate from a source is 240 counts/min at 0 min, 120 counts/min at 5 min and 60 counts/min at 10 min. Determine the half-life. [2]
4. Explain why a β-source is used to monitor the thickness of paper. [3]
5. The Sun is 1.5 × 10¹¹ m from the Earth. Calculate the time for light to reach the Earth. [2]
6. Describe the life cycle of a star much more massive than the Sun after it leaves the stable stage. [3]
7. State three things the Big Bang Theory says about the Universe. [3]
8. Neptune orbits the Sun at a radius of 4.5 × 10¹² m with a period of 165 years. (a) Calculate its orbital speed. [3] (b) Compare it with the Earth's orbital speed (3.0 × 10⁴ m/s) and explain the difference. [2]

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. **92 protons, 146 neutrons, 92 electrons.**
2. 24 days = 3 half-lives: 80 → 40 → 20 → **10 mg**.
3. The count rate halves every 5 min, so half-life = **5.0 min**.
4. β is partly absorbed by paper; a change in thickness changes the count rate measurably (B1); α would be completely stopped by paper (B1); γ would pass straight through with almost no change (B1).
5. t = 1.5 × 10¹¹ ÷ 3.0 × 10⁸ = **500 s** (about 8.3 min).
6. Red supergiant → supernova → neutron star or (for a very large mass) black hole.
7. The Universe expanded from a single point of high density and temperature; it is still expanding; it is about 13.8 billion years old.
8. (a) T = 165 × 365 × 24 × 3600 = 5.20 × 10⁹ s; v = 2π × 4.5 × 10¹² ÷ 5.20 × 10⁹ = **5.4 × 10³ m/s**. (b) Neptune is slower; it is much further from the Sun, where the Sun's gravitational field is weaker, so its orbital speed is smaller.
