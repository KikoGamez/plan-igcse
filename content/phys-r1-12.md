# Nuclear and space physics

> **In the exam:** Paper 2: 4–6 items (atomic structure, isotopes, α/β/γ properties, decay equations, half-life, Solar System facts, life cycle of stars). Paper 4: usually one nuclear question (6–10 marks: decay equations, half-life from a graph or table with background correction, uses and safety) and one space question (5–9 marks: orbital speed, light travel time, star life cycle, redshift and Hubble constant). Paper 6: rarely tested directly; graph skills (decay curve, half-life from graph) still apply.

## What you need to know

- Atom: positive nucleus with electrons around it; ions from losing/gaining electrons. *Extended:* α-particle scattering evidence for a small, massive, positive nucleus.
- Nucleus: protons and neutrons; relative charges +1, 0, −1 (electron); proton number Z, nucleon number A, number of neutrons = A − Z; nuclide notation; isotopes. *Extended:* fission and fusion with nuclide equations; Z gives relative charge, A gives relative mass of a nucleus.
- Background radiation and its sources (radon gas, rocks and buildings, food and drink, cosmic rays); detector + counter; count rate in counts/s or counts/min. *Extended:* corrected count rate.
- Emission is spontaneous and random in direction; α, β, γ: nature, relative ionising effects, relative penetrating abilities. *Extended:* deflection in electric and magnetic fields; ionising effect explained by kinetic energy and charge.
- Radioactive decay changes an unstable nucleus; α or β decay changes the element. *Extended:* excess neutrons / too heavy; effect of each decay on the nucleus; β-decay: neutron → proton + electron; decay equations.
- Half-life definition and simple calculations. *Extended:* half-life from data with background not subtracted; choosing isotopes for uses (smoke alarms, irradiating food, sterilising equipment, thickness control, cancer diagnosis and treatment).
- Effects of ionising radiation (cell death, mutations, cancer); safe moving, use and storage. *Extended:* reduce exposure time, increase distance, use shielding.
- Earth: rotation (24 h, day/night), orbit (365 days, seasons, tilted axis), Moon (≈ 1 month, phases). *Extended:* v = 2πr ÷ T.
- Solar System contents and planet order; inner rocky, outer gaseous, explained by an accretion model; gravitational field strength; light travel time; Sun has most of the mass; gravity keeps objects in orbit. *Extended:* elliptical orbits; analyse planetary data; orbital speed and field strength decrease with distance from the Sun; faster when closer (conservation of energy).
- Sun: medium-sized star, mostly hydrogen and helium, radiates mostly infrared, visible and ultraviolet. *Extended:* stable stars fuse hydrogen into helium.
- Galaxies, Milky Way, light-year = 9.5 × 10¹⁵ m; life cycle of stars. *Extended:* Milky Way diameter ≈ 100 000 light-years; redshift; CMBR; H₀ = v ÷ d = 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹; age of Universe ≈ 1/H₀.

## Notes

### Equations

| Equation | Symbols and units |
|---|---|
| number of neutrons = A − Z | A nucleon number, Z proton number |
| Half-life: after n half-lives, amount remaining = original × (½)ⁿ | count rate (counts/s or counts/min), half-life (s, min, h, days, years) |
| corrected count rate = measured count rate − background count rate *(Extended)* | counts/min |
| speed = distance ÷ time (light travel time t = d ÷ c, c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s) | m, s |
| v = 2πr ÷ T *(Extended)* | v average orbital speed (m/s), r average orbital radius (m), T orbital period (s) |
| H₀ = v ÷ d *(Extended)* | H₀ Hubble constant (s⁻¹) = 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹, v recession speed (m/s), d distance (m) |
| age of Universe ≈ d ÷ v = 1 ÷ H₀ *(Extended)* | s |
| 1 light-year = 9.5 × 10¹⁵ m | distance light travels in one year |

### The atom and nucleus

| Particle | Relative charge | Relative mass | Location |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proton | +1 | 1 | nucleus |
| Neutron | 0 | 1 | nucleus |
| Electron | −1 | very small (≈ 1/1840) | orbiting the nucleus |

- Nuclide notation: nucleon number A written top left and proton number Z bottom left of the symbol X, e.g. ¹⁴₆C has 6 protons, 8 neutrons.
- **Isotopes:** atoms of the same element (same Z) with different numbers of neutrons (different A).
- *Extended — α-scattering (gold foil):* most α-particles passed straight through → atom is mostly empty space; a few deflected through large angles → small, positively charged nucleus; very few bounced back → nucleus contains most of the mass and is very small and dense.
- *Extended — fission:* a large nucleus (e.g. uranium-235) absorbs a neutron and splits into two smaller nuclei plus 2–3 neutrons, releasing energy: ²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n → ¹⁴¹₅₆Ba + ⁹²₃₆Kr + 3 ¹₀n. *Fusion:* two light nuclei join to form a heavier nucleus, releasing energy (in stars): ²₁H + ³₁H → ⁴₂He + ¹₀n. In both, the total mass of the products is slightly less than the reactants; the missing mass is released as energy. A and Z are both conserved.

### Radiation

| | Alpha (α) | Beta (β⁻) | Gamma (γ) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | helium nucleus: 2 protons + 2 neutrons, ⁴₂He | fast-moving electron from the nucleus, ⁰₋₁e | electromagnetic wave |
| Charge | +2 | −1 | 0 |
| Ionising effect | strongly ionising | moderately | weakly |
| Penetration (stopped by) | a few cm of air / thin paper | a few mm of aluminium | reduced by thick lead / concrete |
| Deflection in fields *(Ext)* | yes, small (heavy) | yes, larger and opposite direction to α (light) | not deflected |

- *Extended:* α is most ionising because it has the largest charge and large kinetic energy (it is heavy and slow, so interacts with many atoms and loses energy quickly). γ is uncharged so rarely interacts.
- **Background radiation sources:** radon gas in the air, rocks and buildings, food and drink, cosmic rays (also medical and nuclear industry).
- Detector (e.g. Geiger–Müller tube) connected to a counter; count rate in counts/s or counts/min. *Extended:* measure background with the source removed, then subtract it from each reading.

### Radioactive decay

- Decay is **spontaneous** (not affected by temperature, chemicals) and **random** (cannot predict which nucleus decays next or when; emission random in direction).
- **α-decay:** A decreases by 4, Z decreases by 2. ²²⁶₈₈Ra → ²²²₈₆Rn + ⁴₂He
- **β-decay:** a neutron changes into a proton and an electron; the electron is emitted. A unchanged, Z increases by 1. ⁹⁰₃₈Sr → ⁹⁰₃₉Y + ⁰₋₁e
- **γ-emission:** no change in A or Z; the nucleus loses energy. Often follows α or β decay.
- *Extended:* nuclei are unstable if they have too many neutrons (they β-decay, reducing excess neutrons) or are too heavy (they α-decay). Decay makes the nucleus more stable.
- Check equations: top numbers balance, bottom numbers balance.

### Half-life

- **Half-life:** time taken for half the nuclei of that isotope in any sample to decay (also = time for count rate to halve).
- From a decay curve: read the time for the count rate to fall from any value to half of it; repeat from another point and average.
- *Extended:* if background is included in the data, subtract it **first**, then find the half-life.

### Uses (Extended: choosing the isotope)

| Use | Radiation | Half-life | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Smoke alarm | α | long (years) | α ionises air → small current; smoke absorbs α → current drops → alarm; α can't escape the alarm; long half-life so no replacement needed |
| Thickness control (paper, foil) | β | long | partly absorbed: changes in thickness change the count rate measurably (α all stopped; γ passes through regardless) |
| Sterilising equipment / irradiating food | γ | long | penetrates packaging, kills bacteria; food does not become radioactive |
| Cancer diagnosis (tracer) | γ | short (hours) | passes out of the body to the detector; short half-life limits dose |
| Cancer treatment | γ | — | focused beams kill cancer cells |

### Safety

- Effects of ionising radiation on living things: cell death, mutations, cancer.
- Handling: tongs, never touch; keep source pointing away from people; store in lead-lined boxes; label; minimum time out of storage.
- *Extended:* reduce exposure **time**; increase **distance**; use **shielding** (lead, concrete) to absorb radiation.

### Earth and Solar System

- Earth rotates on its tilted axis once in ≈ 24 h → day and night, apparent motion of the Sun east to west. Orbits the Sun in ≈ 365 days; the tilt causes the seasons. Moon orbits Earth in ≈ 1 month → phases (we see different amounts of its sunlit half).
- **Solar System:** one star (the Sun); eight planets in order **Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune**; minor planets (dwarf planets such as Pluto, asteroids in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter); moons; comets and natural satellites.
- Inner four: small, rocky. Outer four: large, gaseous. **Accretion model:** the Solar System formed from a rotating interstellar cloud of gas and dust containing many elements; gravity pulled material together into a rotating accretion disc; near the hot Sun only rocky materials could condense; further out, gases (and ices) could also collect, forming large gas giants.
- Gravitational field strength at a planet's surface depends on its mass; it decreases with distance from the planet.
- The Sun contains most of the Solar System's mass; its gravitational attraction keeps planets in orbit.
- *Extended:* orbits are elliptical (the Sun is not at the centre unless the orbit is nearly circular). Further from the Sun: weaker field, slower orbital speed, longer period. A comet in an elliptical orbit speeds up approaching the Sun (gravitational potential energy → kinetic energy) and slows down moving away.

### Stars and the Universe

- **Sun:** medium-sized star, mostly hydrogen and helium; radiates most energy as infrared, visible and ultraviolet. *Extended:* powered by nuclear fusion of hydrogen into helium.
- Galaxies contain many billions of stars; the Sun is in the Milky Way; other stars are much further away than the Sun. *Extended:* Milky Way diameter ≈ 100 000 light-years; it is one of billions of galaxies.
- **Life cycle of a star:**
  1. Interstellar cloud of gas and dust (containing hydrogen) collapses under its own gravity → **protostar** (temperature rises).
  2. Fusion starts; **stable star** when the inward gravitational force is balanced by the outward force due to the high temperature in the centre.
  3. Hydrogen in the centre runs out (mostly converted to helium) → expands to a **red giant** (most stars) or **red supergiant** (more massive stars).
  4. Red giant → **planetary nebula** with a **white dwarf** at the centre.
  5. Red supergiant → explodes as a **supernova** → nebula containing hydrogen and new heavier elements, leaving a **neutron star** or **black hole**.
  6. The nebula may form new stars with orbiting planets.
- *Extended — expanding Universe:*
  - **Redshift:** increase in the observed wavelength of EM radiation from receding stars and galaxies. Light from distant galaxies is redshifted compared with light on Earth → galaxies are moving away → Universe is expanding → supports the **Big Bang Theory**.
  - **CMBR:** microwave radiation of a specific frequency observed from all directions in space; produced shortly after the Universe formed; its wavelength has been stretched into the microwave region as the Universe expanded.
  - Speed v of a galaxy from the change in wavelength (redshift); distance d from the brightness of a supernova in it.
  - H₀ = v ÷ d ≈ 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹. d ÷ v = 1 ÷ H₀ estimates the age of the Universe (≈ 4.5 × 10¹⁷ s ≈ 14 billion years), evidence that all matter was once at a single point.

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Isotopes | Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons |
| Proton number (Z) | Number of protons in the nucleus |
| Nucleon number (A) | Number of protons + neutrons in the nucleus |
| Background radiation | Ionising radiation always present in the environment from natural and artificial sources |
| Half-life | Time taken for half the nuclei of that isotope in any sample to decay |
| Radioactive decay | Spontaneous, random change in an unstable nucleus, emitting α, β and/or γ radiation |
| Light-year | Distance travelled by light in (the vacuum of) space in one year (9.5 × 10¹⁵ m) |
| Redshift *(Ext)* | Increase in the observed wavelength of EM radiation emitted from receding stars and galaxies |
| Hubble constant *(Ext)* | Ratio of the speed at which a galaxy is moving away from the Earth to its distance from the Earth |
| CMBR *(Ext)* | Microwave radiation of a specific frequency observed at all points in space, produced shortly after the Universe was formed |

## Worked examples

**1.** A detector records a count rate of 840 counts/min from a source. Background is 40 counts/min. After 18 hours the measured count rate is 140 counts/min. Calculate the half-life. [3]

- corrected rates: 840 − 40 = 800; 140 − 40 = 100 counts/min (C1)
- 800 → 400 → 200 → 100 is 3 half-lives (C1)
- half-life = 18 ÷ 3 = **6.0 hours** (A1)

**2.** Complete the equations: (a) radium-226 (Z = 88) emits an α-particle and becomes radon; (b) carbon-14 (Z = 6) emits a β-particle and becomes nitrogen. [4]

- (a) ²²⁶₈₈Ra → ²²²₈₆Rn + ⁴₂He (B1 for 222/86, B1 for ⁴₂He)
- (b) ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁e (B1 for 14/7, B1 for ⁰₋₁e)

**3.** The Earth orbits the Sun at an average radius of 1.5 × 10¹¹ m once every 365 days. Calculate its average orbital speed. [3]

- T = 365 × 24 × 3600 = 3.15 × 10⁷ s (C1)
- v = 2πr ÷ T = 2π × 1.5 × 10¹¹ ÷ 3.15 × 10⁷ (C1) = **3.0 × 10⁴ m/s** (A1)

**4.** A galaxy is receding at 2.2 × 10⁷ m/s. Using H₀ = 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹, calculate (a) its distance, [2] (b) an estimate of the age of the Universe in seconds. [1]

- (a) d = v ÷ H₀ = 2.2 × 10⁷ ÷ 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁸ (C1) = **1.0 × 10²⁵ m** (A1)
- (b) 1 ÷ H₀ = **4.5 × 10¹⁷ s** (B1)

## Common mistakes

- Defining half-life as "half the time for the sample to decay" or "time for the mass of the sample to halve" — say **half the nuclei** (or the count rate halves).
- Forgetting to subtract background before finding the half-life.
- β-decay equations with Z going down, or writing the β-particle as ⁰₁e.
- Saying γ is "stopped" by lead — it is reduced/absorbed significantly by thick lead.
- Ordering planets wrongly or including Pluto as a planet.
- Saying red giants become supernovae — only red **supergiants** (massive stars) do.
- Explaining redshift as "light gets redder because it is hot" — it is the increase in wavelength because the galaxy is moving away.
- Using days instead of seconds for T in v = 2πr/T.

## Practice questions

1. State the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in a neutral atom of ²³⁸₉₂U. [2]
2. Iodine-131 has a half-life of 8 days. A sample contains 80 mg. Calculate the mass of iodine-131 left after 24 days. [2]
3. Count rates (including background 20 counts/min) at 0, 5 and 10 minutes are 260, 140 and 80 counts/min. Determine the half-life. [3]
4. Explain why a β-source with a long half-life is used to monitor the thickness of paper. [3]
5. The Sun is 1.5 × 10¹¹ m from the Earth. Calculate the time for light to reach the Earth. [2]
6. Describe the life cycle of a star much more massive than the Sun after it leaves the main stable stage. [3]
7. State two pieces of evidence for the Big Bang Theory. [2]
8. A galaxy is 4.0 × 10²⁴ m away. Calculate its recession speed. [2]

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. **92 protons, 146 neutrons, 92 electrons.**
2. 24 days = 3 half-lives: 80 → 40 → 20 → **10 mg**.
3. Corrected: 240, 120, 60 — halves every 5 min, so half-life = **5.0 min**.
4. Some β is absorbed by paper — change in thickness changes the count rate (α would be completely stopped, γ would pass straight through with almost no change); long half-life so the count rate doesn't fall noticeably due to decay and the source need not be replaced.
5. t = 1.5 × 10¹¹ ÷ 3.0 × 10⁸ = **500 s** (≈ 8.3 min).
6. Hydrogen runs out in the core → expands into a red supergiant → explodes as a supernova → nebula with heavier elements, leaving a neutron star or black hole.
7. Redshift of light from distant galaxies (galaxies receding, Universe expanding); cosmic microwave background radiation.
8. v = H₀d = 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁸ × 4.0 × 10²⁴ = **8.8 × 10⁶ m/s**.
