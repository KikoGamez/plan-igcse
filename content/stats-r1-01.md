# Collecting data

> **In the exam:** Appears on both papers, usually as the opening question or inside a longer context question. Typical tasks: classify data (1 mark each), name or describe a sampling method (2 marks), calculate a stratified sample (2–3 marks), criticise a question on a questionnaire and write a better one (2–3 marks), give an advantage/disadvantage of a method (1–2 marks). These are "easy marks" only if you use precise wording and refer to the context.

## What you need to know

- Types of data: qualitative (categorical) and quantitative; discrete and continuous; primary and secondary; raw data; bivariate data.
- Advantages and disadvantages of primary and secondary data.
- Populations and samples: population, census, sample, sampling frame, sampling unit; reasons for sampling; bias.
- Sampling methods: simple random sampling (including use of random numbers), stratified sampling, systematic sampling, quota sampling — how to carry them out, and their advantages and disadvantages.
- Methods of collecting data: questionnaires and interviews; open and closed questions; designing questions and response boxes; leading questions and other sources of bias; pilot studies.

## Notes

### Types of data

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Qualitative (categorical) | described in words, not numbers | eye colour, favourite sport |
| Quantitative | numerical | number of siblings, height |
| Discrete | can only take particular values (often counted) | number of goals, shoe size |
| Continuous | can take any value in a range (measured) | time, mass, length |
| Primary | collected by you (or for you) for the purpose | your own survey |
| Secondary | collected by someone else, already exists | census data, internet tables |
| Bivariate | two variables recorded for each item | height and arm span of each student |

Primary data: you control how it is collected and it is exactly what you need, but it takes time and money. Secondary data: cheap and quick, often large data sets, but it may be out of date, not exactly what you need, or its accuracy may be unknown.

### Population and sample

- **Population**: everything or everyone that could be studied.
- **Census**: data collected from every member of the population. Accurate and complete, but expensive and time-consuming; impractical for large populations or when testing destroys items.
- **Sample**: a part of the population. Cheaper and quicker, but may not represent the population (may be biased).
- **Sampling frame**: a list of all members of the population from which the sample is chosen (e.g. the school register).
- **Bias**: when the sample is not representative, e.g. some members are more likely to be chosen. A larger sample generally gives more reliable results.

### Sampling methods

| Method | How to do it | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simple random | Number every member of the sampling frame; select using random numbers (calculator, table, computer); ignore repeats | Every member has an equal chance; free of selection bias | Needs a full sampling frame; may by chance not represent all groups |
| Stratified | Split population into groups (strata), e.g. year groups; sample from each in proportion to its size, using random sampling within each group | Sample reflects the population's structure | Need to know the size of each group; more work |
| Systematic | Find k = population ÷ sample size; choose a random start between 1 and k; then take every kth member | Quick and simple; spread through the list | Biased if the list has a repeating pattern |
| Quota | Interviewer is told how many of each type to ask (e.g. 20 men, 20 women) and chooses them | Quick, no sampling frame needed | Not random — interviewer's choice can cause bias |

**Stratified sample size for a group = (group size ÷ population size) × total sample size.** Round to whole numbers and check the totals add up to the sample size.

### Describing a random sample (2–3 marks)

"Give each student a number from 1 to 1200 using the school register. Use a random number generator to pick 60 different numbers between 1 and 1200, ignoring repeats. Survey the students with those numbers."

### Questionnaires and interviews

Good questions:
- are clear and unambiguous, and not personal or embarrassing;
- are not **leading** (e.g. "Don't you agree that…?" pushes a particular answer);
- have **response boxes** that do not overlap, cover every possible answer (exhaustive), and include a time frame where needed ("per week").

Bad response boxes: 0–1, 1–2, 2–3 (overlap); no box for "more than 5" (not exhaustive).

| | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Open question | allows any answer, more detail | hard to analyse |
| Closed question (tick boxes) | easy to analyse and compare | limits responses |
| Postal/online questionnaire | cheap, wide reach, anonymous | low response rate; questions can't be explained |
| Interview | high response rate; can explain questions | expensive, time-consuming; interviewer may influence answers |

A **pilot study** (a small trial run before the main survey) is used to check the questions are clear and that the method works, so problems can be fixed before the main survey.

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Population | The whole group about which information is wanted |
| Census | A survey of every member of the population |
| Sample | A subset of the population |
| Sampling frame | A list of the members of the population from which a sample is taken |
| Discrete data | Data that can only take certain individual values |
| Continuous data | Data that can take any value within a range |
| Biased sample | A sample that does not fairly represent the population |
| Pilot study | A small-scale trial of a survey, carried out before the main survey to identify problems |

## Worked examples

**1.** A school has 420 students in Year 10, 380 in Year 11 and 400 in Year 12. A stratified sample of 60 is taken by year group. How many Year 11 students should be in the sample? [2]

380 ÷ 1200 × 60 M1 = **19** A1 (Year 10: 21, Year 12: 20; total 60 ✓)

**2.** Describe how to take a systematic sample of 60 students from the 1200. [2]

1200 ÷ 60 = 20 B1. Choose a random starting number between 1 and 20, e.g. 7, then take every 20th student on the list: 7, 27, 47, … B1

**3.** A survey asks: "How much time do you spend on homework? 0–1 hour □ 1–2 hours □ 2–3 hours □"
Give two criticisms and write an improved question. [3]

Response boxes overlap (e.g. 1 hour fits two boxes) B1; no time frame / no box for more than 3 hours B1.
Improved: "How many hours did you spend on homework last week? 0 ≤ h < 2 □ 2 ≤ h < 4 □ 4 ≤ h < 6 □ 6 or more □" B1

## Common mistakes

- Calling data "continuous" because it has decimals: shoe size 7.5 is discrete.
- Saying random sampling means "choosing people at random in the corridor" — that is not random; you must use a sampling frame and random numbers.
- Stratified sample numbers that don't add up to the total sample size after rounding.
- Advantages and disadvantages that are too vague ("it's easier", "it's more accurate") — say *why*, in the context of the question.
- Writing an "improved question" that still has overlapping or missing boxes.
- Forgetting to say "ignore repeats" in a random sample description.

## Practice questions

1. Classify each as qualitative, discrete or continuous: (a) the number of cars in a car park, (b) the colour of cars, (c) the time to run 100 m. [3]
2. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of using secondary data. [2]
3. A sports club has 150 juniors, 270 adults and 80 seniors. A stratified sample of 50 members is needed. Calculate the number from each group. [3]
4. Explain why a census is not suitable for testing the lifetime of light bulbs made in a factory. [1]
5. A researcher stands outside a gym at 7 a.m. to find out how much exercise people in a town do. Explain why the sample may be biased. [1]
6. Give one advantage of quota sampling over stratified sampling, and one disadvantage. [2]
7. "Don't you agree that the canteen food is too expensive?" Give one reason why this is not a good question and write an improved version with response boxes. [3]
8. State one reason for carrying out a pilot study. [1]

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. (a) discrete (b) qualitative (c) continuous
2. Advantage: cheap/quick to obtain, or large amount of data. Disadvantage: may be out of date / not exactly what is needed / accuracy unknown.
3. Total 500. Juniors 150/500 × 50 = **15**; adults 270/500 × 50 = **27**; seniors 80/500 × 50 = **8** (15 + 27 + 8 = 50 ✓)
4. Testing destroys the bulbs (they must burn out), so none would be left to sell.
5. People at a gym exercise more than average, and only early risers are included, so the sample does not represent the town.
6. Advantage: no sampling frame needed / quicker. Disadvantage: not random, so interviewer bias is possible.
7. Leading question (suggests the answer). Improved: "How would you rate the price of canteen food? Very cheap □ Cheap □ About right □ Expensive □ Very expensive □"
8. To check that the questions are clear/understood, so they can be improved before the main survey (or to test the method).
