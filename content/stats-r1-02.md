# Representing data

> **In the exam:** Both papers. Typical tasks: complete a two-way table (2–3 marks), draw or read a pie chart (3–4 marks), comparative pie charts (radius calculation, 2–3 marks), back-to-back stem-and-leaf diagram and compare the two sets (3–5 marks), interpret a population pyramid (2–4 marks), say why a diagram is misleading (1–2 marks). Comparisons must refer to the context.

## What you need to know

- Tabulation: tally charts, frequency tables, grouped frequency tables, two-way tables.
- Pictograms, bar charts (including multiple/dual and composite/stacked), line graphs.
- Pie charts, including comparative pie charts whose areas are in proportion to the totals.
- Stem-and-leaf diagrams, including back-to-back stem-and-leaf diagrams.
- Population pyramids.
- Choosing an appropriate diagram; recognising and explaining misleading diagrams.
- (Histograms, frequency polygons, cumulative frequency and box plots are on other pages.)

## Notes

### Two-way tables

Show two categorical variables at once. Fill in missing values using row and column totals; the grand total is in the bottom-right corner and must equal both the sum of the row totals and the sum of the column totals.

### Bar charts

- Bars of equal width with gaps (for qualitative or discrete data); label both axes and give a title.
- **Multiple (dual) bar chart**: bars side by side for comparing groups category by category.
- **Composite (stacked) bar chart**: each bar split into parts; good for comparing totals and proportions.

### Pie charts

- Angle for a category = (frequency ÷ total) × 360°.
- Shortcut: find "degrees per item" = 360 ÷ total.
- Reading: frequency = (angle ÷ 360) × total.
- Pie charts show **proportions**, not actual numbers — you cannot say one category is larger in two different pie charts unless you know the totals.

### Comparative pie charts

When two pie charts represent different totals, their **areas** should be in proportion to the totals:

r₂ ÷ r₁ = √(n₂ ÷ n₁), so r₂ = r₁ × √(n₂ ÷ n₁)

Example: 2022 total 400, radius 5 cm; 2023 total 625: r = 5 × √(625/400) = 5 × 1.25 = **6.25 cm**.

### Stem-and-leaf diagrams

- Leaves in order, one digit per leaf, with a **key** (e.g. 3 | 7 = 37 seconds).
- **Back-to-back**: two data sets share the stem; leaves on the left read right-to-left (the key must show this, e.g. 7 | 3 | = 37 for the left side).
- Easy to find the median, mode, range and quartiles while keeping the raw data.

### Population pyramids

- Two horizontal bar charts back to back: males on one side, females on the other, by age group; usually percentages of the population.
- Wide base, narrow top (triangle): high birth rate, high death rate, short life expectancy — typical of a less economically developed country.
- Narrow base, wide middle and top: low birth rate, longer life expectancy — an ageing population, typical of a more economically developed country.
- Compare: proportion of young people (0–14), working age, and elderly (65+); differences between males and females at older ages.

### Choosing and criticising diagrams

| Data / purpose | Good choice |
|---|---|
| Proportions of a whole | pie chart, composite bar chart |
| Comparing categories across groups | multiple bar chart |
| Keeping raw values, small data sets | stem-and-leaf |
| Age–sex structure of a population | population pyramid |

Misleading features: vertical axis not starting at zero, uneven scales, 3D effects that exaggerate size, pictograms with different-sized symbols, missing labels or key.

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Two-way table | A table showing frequencies for two variables, one in the rows and one in the columns |
| Comparative pie charts | Pie charts whose areas are proportional to the totals they represent |
| Back-to-back stem-and-leaf | Two stem-and-leaf diagrams sharing the same stem, used to compare two data sets |
| Population pyramid | Back-to-back bar charts showing the percentage of males and females in each age group |

## Worked examples

**1.** 72 students chose their favourite fruit: apples 18, bananas 24, oranges 12, grapes 18. Calculate the angles for a pie chart. [3]

360 ÷ 72 = 5° per student M1; apples **90°**, bananas **120°**, oranges **60°**, grapes **90°** A2 (A1 for 2 correct). Check: total 360° ✓

**2.** A shop's sales are shown in a pie chart of radius 5 cm for 2022, when there were 400 sales. In 2023 there were 625 sales. Calculate the radius of the 2023 comparative pie chart. [2]

r = 5 × √(625 ÷ 400) M1 = **6.25 cm** A1

**3.** Complete the two-way table. [3]

| | Walk | Bus | Car | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boys | 12 | ? | 8 | 35 |
| Girls | ? | 10 | ? | ? |
| Total | 27 | ? | 20 | 70 |

Boys bus = 35 − 12 − 8 = **15**; girls walk = 27 − 12 = **15**; girls car = 20 − 8 = **12**; girls total = 70 − 35 = **35**; bus total = 15 + 10 = **25**. Check girls: 15 + 10 + 12 = 37 ≠ 35? — see below.

Check carefully: girls total from the row = 15 + 10 + 12 = 37, but 70 − 35 = 35. So the column totals must be checked: 27 + 25 + 20 = 72 ≠ 70. This means the table as printed is inconsistent — in an exam this never happens, so recheck the question. With a girls' bus value of **8** instead of 10, everything works: bus total 23, girls total 35, grand total 27 + 23 + 20 = 70 ✓. Lesson: **always check rows and columns both add to the grand total.**

## Common mistakes

- Pie chart angles that don't add to 360° (check!), or sectors drawn inaccurately (±2° is allowed).
- Comparing pie charts with different totals as if they showed numbers.
- Using the ratio of totals, not its square root, for comparative pie chart radii.
- Stem-and-leaf with no key, leaves not in order, or two-digit leaves.
- Reading the left side of a back-to-back stem-and-leaf the wrong way round.
- Population pyramid comments with no figures or no context ("it is bigger") — quote percentages and age groups.

## Practice questions

1. In a pie chart, a sector of 75° represents 40 people. How many people are represented altogether? [2]
2. A pie chart of radius 4 cm represents 300 people. A comparative pie chart has radius 6 cm. How many people does it represent? [2]
3. Draw a stem-and-leaf diagram (with key) for: 23, 35, 41, 28, 37, 33, 45, 29, 31, 40. Find the median. [4]
4. Give two reasons why a bar chart with a vertical axis starting at 50 might be misleading. [2]
5. Country A's population pyramid has 40% of people aged 0–14 and 4% aged 65+. Country B has 15% aged 0–14 and 22% aged 65+. Compare the two populations and suggest which is more economically developed. [3]
6. 120 people were asked how they travel to work. 54 drive, 30 take the train, the rest cycle. Calculate the angle for "cycle". [2]

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. 40 × 360 ÷ 75 = **192**
2. 300 × (6 ÷ 4)² = 300 × 2.25 = **675**
3. 2 | 3 8 9; 3 | 1 3 5 7; 4 | 0 1 5. Key: 2 | 3 means 23. n = 10, median = (33 + 35) ÷ 2 = **34**
4. Differences between bars look much bigger than they are; the reader may not notice the axis doesn't start at zero (also: no labels/uneven scale).
5. A has a much larger proportion of young people (40% vs 15%) and a smaller proportion of elderly people (4% vs 22%), suggesting a higher birth rate and lower life expectancy in A. **B** is more likely to be more economically developed.
6. Cycle = 120 − 54 − 30 = 36; 36 ÷ 120 × 360 = **108°**
