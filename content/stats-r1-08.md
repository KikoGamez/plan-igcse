# Index numbers and rates

> **In the exam:** Appears in most series as a 6–10 mark question. Typical tasks: calculate a simple index number (1–2 marks), work backwards to a price (2 marks), complete a table of chain base index numbers and interpret one (3–4 marks), calculate a weighted index (2–3 marks), calculate crude and standardised birth or death rates and explain why the standardised rate is better for comparing places (4–6 marks).

## What you need to know

- Simple index numbers: index = (value ÷ value in base year) × 100; interpreting them as percentage changes.
- Chain base index numbers: each year compared with the previous year; interpreting and converting.
- Weighted index numbers (weighted mean of index numbers), e.g. price indices such as the Consumer Prices Index.
- Crude rates (e.g. birth rate, death rate per 1000).
- Standardised rates using a standard population, and why they are used to compare populations with different age structures.

## Notes

### Simple index numbers

**Index number = (value in given year ÷ value in base year) × 100**

- The base year always has index 100.
- An index of 125 means a 25% increase since the base year; an index of 92 means an 8% decrease.
- Working backwards: value = base value × index ÷ 100; base value = value × 100 ÷ index.

### Chain base index numbers

**Chain base index = (value this year ÷ value in previous year) × 100**

- Shows the year-on-year change. A chain index of 104 means a 4% rise **since the previous year**.
- A chain index below 100 means the value **fell** that year. A chain index that drops from 110 to 105 still means the value **rose** (by 5%) — just more slowly.
- Overall change over several years: multiply the chain indices as decimals. Example: 110, 110, 105 → 1.10 × 1.10 × 1.05 = 1.2705 → about a 27% increase.

### Weighted index numbers

**Weighted index = Σ(index × weight) ÷ Σweights**

- Weights show the relative importance of each item (e.g. how much households spend on it).
- Example: food index 120 (weight 5), transport 110 (weight 3), energy 140 (weight 2): (600 + 330 + 280) ÷ 10 = **121**.
- Official price indices such as the Consumer Prices Index (CPI) are weighted indices of a "basket" of goods and services; they are used to measure inflation.

### Crude rates

**Crude rate = (number of events ÷ total population) × 1000** (per 1000 of the population)

- Crude death rate = deaths ÷ population × 1000; crude birth rate = births ÷ population × 1000.
- Problem: crude rates do not take into account the **age structure**. A seaside town with many retired people will have a high crude death rate even if it is a healthy place to live.

### Standardised rates

Use the **group rates** of the town with the age structure of a **standard population**:

**Standardised rate = Σ(rate for age group × standard population % for that group) ÷ 100**

Steps:
1. For each age group, find the town's rate per 1000 = deaths in group ÷ population in group × 1000.
2. Multiply each group rate by the standard population percentage for that group.
3. Add and divide by 100.

Standardised rates allow a fair comparison between areas with different age distributions, because the same standard population is used for each.

## Key terms and definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Index number | A value showing the change in a quantity compared with a base year, which has index 100 |
| Base year | The year with which other years are compared |
| Chain base index number | An index number comparing each year with the previous year |
| Weighted index number | A weighted mean of index numbers, where weights reflect the importance of each item |
| Crude rate | Number of events per 1000 of the whole population |
| Standardised rate | A rate calculated using the age-group rates of an area applied to a standard population, so that areas with different age structures can be compared fairly |

## Worked examples

**1.** A loaf cost €2.00 in 2021, €2.20 in 2022, €2.42 in 2023 and €2.54 in 2024.
(a) Using 2021 as base year, find the index number for 2024. [2]
(b) Calculate the chain base index numbers for 2022, 2023 and 2024. [3]
(c) Interpret the chain base index for 2024. [1]

(a) 2.54 ÷ 2.00 × 100 M1 = **127** A1
(b) 2.20 ÷ 2.00 × 100 = **110**; 2.42 ÷ 2.20 × 100 = **110**; 2.54 ÷ 2.42 × 100 = **105.0** M1 A2
(c) The price rose by about 5% from 2023 to 2024 B1

**2.** Data for a town:

| Age group | Population | Deaths | Standard population (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–24 | 12 000 | 18 | 35 |
| 25–64 | 20 000 | 80 | 50 |
| 65+ | 8 000 | 320 | 15 |

(a) Calculate the crude death rate. [2]
(b) Calculate the standardised death rate. [4]
(c) Explain why the standardised rate is better for comparing this town with others. [1]

(a) Total deaths 418, population 40 000: 418 ÷ 40 000 × 1000 M1 = **10.45 per 1000** A1
(b) Group rates: 18 ÷ 12 000 × 1000 = 1.5; 80 ÷ 20 000 × 1000 = 4; 320 ÷ 8000 × 1000 = 40 M1 A1
(1.5 × 35 + 4 × 50 + 40 × 15) ÷ 100 M1 = (52.5 + 200 + 600) ÷ 100 = **8.53 per 1000** A1
(c) It takes account of the different age structures of the populations, by applying the town's age-group rates to the same standard population B1

## Common mistakes

- Dividing the wrong way round (base ÷ current) or forgetting to × 100.
- Reading a falling chain index (e.g. 110 → 105) as a fall in price: it is still a rise.
- Adding chain indices to find the overall change instead of multiplying.
- Weighted index: dividing by the number of items instead of the sum of the weights.
- Standardised rate: multiplying the town's **population** by the standard %, instead of the group **rate**; forgetting ÷ 100.
- Rates not given "per 1000".

## Practice questions

1. A ticket cost €45 in 2015 (base year). In 2024 the index is 134. Find the 2024 price. [2]
2. A bicycle cost €80 in 2022, when the index (2018 = 100) was 120. Find its 2018 price. [2]
3. Chain base indices for three years are 105, 103 and 98. Find the overall percentage change. [3]
4. A town of 84 000 people had 1260 births in a year. Calculate the crude birth rate. [2]
5. Age-group death rates per 1000 are 2, 6 and 50 for groups 0–29, 30–64 and 65+. The standard population is 40%, 45% and 15%. Calculate the standardised death rate. [3]
6. Calculate the weighted index for: clothing index 108 (weight 4), leisure 115 (weight 1), food 96 (weight 5). [2]
7. Town A has a higher crude death rate than Town B but a lower standardised death rate. Suggest a reason. [2]

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. 45 × 134 ÷ 100 = **€60.30**
2. 80 × 100 ÷ 120 = **€66.67**
3. 1.05 × 1.03 × 0.98 = 1.0599 → **about a 6.0% increase**
4. 1260 ÷ 84 000 × 1000 = **15 per 1000**
5. (2 × 40 + 6 × 45 + 50 × 15) ÷ 100 = (80 + 270 + 750) ÷ 100 = **11.0 per 1000**
6. (432 + 115 + 480) ÷ 10 = **102.7**
7. Town A has a larger proportion of elderly people, which raises its crude rate; when age structure is accounted for, its age-group death rates are actually lower.
